Jeff Miller and Nora Olsen September 16, 2026 Potato harvest can reveal two troublesome diseases – pink rot and pythium leak. Understanding their causes and managing harvest conditions are critical to protecting tubers through storage. The phrase “pretty in pink” was once used to describe somebody who was charming when wearing pink. While this may be true for people, it is not true for potatoes. When it comes to potato harvest, pink is what you don’t want to see – specifically pink rot. Pink rot is caused by the pathogen Phytophthora erythroseptica , which is a cousin to the pathogen that causes late blight. Potato tubers infected with this pathogen turn a distinctive pink color when cut open. Pythium leak is a little different in that it turns infected tissue a gray to black color. If you squeeze a potato with this disease, water will “leak” out, which is where the disease gets its name. Once tubers are fully rotted, these diseases are often collectively called “water rots," but it is best to identify if you have one or the other or even both since field and postharvest management will vary depending upon which disease is causing the primary problem. Dissecting the tuber can quickly help identify which disease it is. Pink rot can develop in potato tubers almost any time after tubers start to form on the plant. The pathogen usually infects near the end of the season. Potato growers can manage pink rot with crop rotation (the longer the better between potato crops), variety selection (some varieties are more susceptible than others) and the use of fungicides. The pathogen can spread at harvest when infected tubers come in contact with healthy tubers. Harvesting at lower tuber pulp temperatures (below 60ºF) can reduce this risk, as can the use of phosphite-based fungicides as a postharvest spray before going into storage. Pythium leak is a disease that has two basic requirements: wounds (shatter bruises = entry point) and high pulp temperatures. The disease organism is soilborne and usually gains entrance into the tuber through wounds inflicted at harvest. Wounding can also occur before harvest, such as nicks and cuts that may happen to tubers high in the hill at vine beating or infection that occurred in the field through entry points. Advertisement Some level of wounding at harvest is difficult to avoid, but the other requirement – high pulp temperatures – can be managed. The danger posed by pythium leak is a major reason why pulp temperatures above 65°F are to be avoided during harvest. But that 65°F threshold is not absolute – it may be too high for cultivars more susceptible to pythium leak, if truck unloading is slowed or if the storage system does not have the capacity to immediately cool down the incoming crop. Identify the maximum pulp temperature going into storage based upon the specific conditions. Pythium leak-infected tubers break down quickly in storage, exuding a tremendous amount of liquid, which results in soft rot invasion and the creation of hot spots.
