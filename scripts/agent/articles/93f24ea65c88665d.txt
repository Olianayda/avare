Brady Goetz September 23, 2026 Regularly benchmarking both average particle size and grind consistency can help dairies identify opportunities to improve starch utilization and get more value from the corn they are already feeding. In the dairy industry, we spend a lot of time looking for small opportunities to improve performance. We balance rations closely, monitor forage changes, evaluate feed costs and consider new ingredients or additives that might improve efficiency. However, sometimes there are relatively simple things already in the ration that deserve a closer look. Corn grind has become one of those areas for us. Corn is one of the primary sources of starch in most lactating cow diets, and how that corn is processed affects how well the cow can utilize it. Reducing particle size increases the surface area available for digestion, which can improve starch utilization and the amount of energy we capture from the ration. For an ingredient that represents a significant portion of both the ration and feed cost, it makes sense to know what we are getting. Why we started looking closer Our interest in corn grind really started with frustration on client farms. We would pull a corn sample and get a great result, then come back a month later, sample corn from the same source and get something completely different. Nothing had intentionally changed on the dairy, yet the grind wasn't the same. This led us to start collecting more samples across farms, mills and regions. As the number of samples grew, it became apparent there was more variation out there than we expected. Within the samples we've collected, we've seen average particle sizes ranging from approximately 250 microns to more than 850 microns. A large percentage fell somewhere between 400 and 550 microns. Furthermore, as we looked at more samples, we also realized that focusing only on the average micron size doesn't always tell the entire story. An average doesn’t tell the whole story One way I would like to explain this is to picture two jars. Fill one with golf balls, marbles and sand. Fill the other entirely with marbles. Now imagine that, mathematically, the average particle size in both jars works out to be the same. On paper, they look identical. Visually, we know they aren't even close. We can see something similar in ground corn. One sample may contain a large amount of very fine material along with larger pieces of corn, while another sample may be much more uniform throughout. Both could end up with a similar average particle size. Advertisement This has made particle-size distribution an increasingly important part of the conversation for us. Rather than only asking what the average micron size is, we also want to understand how the corn got to that average. Is most of the sample relatively uniform or are we averaging together a combination of fines and much larger particles? That is where benchmarking starts to become useful. The more samples we collect, the better we can understand what a consistent grind looks like and how individual farms and mills compare over time. The mill matters We have also seen differences between roller mills and hammer mills. In general, roller mills tend to produce a narrower distribution of particle sizes. Hammer mills can produce very fine corn as well, but we will often see a wider range of particles within the sample. That doesn't mean one type of mill is always better than the other. We have seen good corn come from both systems. What matters is whether the mill can repeatedly produce the grind the dairy and nutritionists are looking for. The equipment itself is only one part of that equation. How it is maintained and managed over time matters too. How much can the grind change? There are plenty of reasons corn grind can change throughout the year. Screens and hammers wear. Rolls wear. Mill settings get adjusted. Corn sources change. Moisture changes. Eventually, a new crop of corn enters the system, bringing another change in the physical characteristics of the grain being processed. Even if we continue buying corn from the same supplier, we shouldn't automatically assume the product being delivered today is identical to what we tested six months ago. That was one of the biggest lessons we've taken from this project. A single good corn sample doesn't necessarily mean you have a consistently good corn grind. We routinely monitor forage dry matters because we know they change. We track inventories, watch refusals and make ration adjustments as ingredients change. Ground corn is another major part of the ration that deserves periodic verification, especially when changing corn sources, moving into a new crop of corn or seeing unexpected changes in fecal starch, milk production or feed efficiency. Putting a dollar value on grind quality The economics can add up quickly. Consider a 1,000-cow dairy feeding 10 pounds of dry corn per cow per day. That dairy is feeding approximately 5 tons of corn every day. At $170 per ton for corn, grinding and delivery, that represents about $850 per day and close to $310,000 per year invested in that one ingredient. When that much money is being spent on corn, even a relatively small difference in how well the starch is utilized can become meaningful over the course of a year. This is also what makes corn grind an interesting efficiency opportunity. We aren't necessarily talking about adding another ingredient to the ration or asking the cow to consume more feed. The corn is already being purchased. It is already being delivered, mixed and fed. The question is how much value we are capturing from it. If improving processing allows cows to utilize more energy from the same amount of corn, there may be an opportunity to improve feed efficiency and potentially milk production without simply increasing feed cost. Before looking for another ingredient to add to the ration, it can be worthwhile to make sure we are getting the value we expect from the ingredients already there. Working with your mill For dairies purchasing ground corn, monitoring particle size doesn't need to become a combative conversation with the feed mill. In fact, having actual samples and benchmarking information can make that conversation easier. Rather than saying the corn "looks coarse" or "doesn't look right," the dairy, nutritionist and mill can work from objective information. More importantly, repeated sampling allows everyone to see whether the grind stays consistent over time. For some larger dairies, grinding corn on-farm may also be worth evaluating. Bringing that process on-farm provides greater control over particle size and may change grinding, mixing and delivery costs. But owning the grinder doesn't eliminate the need to monitor the finished product. Screens, hammers and rolls still wear, corn characteristics still change, and equipment settings still need attention. Whether the grinder sits at the feed mill or on the dairy, somebody still needs to check what is coming out of it. Measure what you’re feeding The biggest change this project has made in how we think about corn grind is probably the simplest one: Don't assume. A particle-size result gives us useful information, but one sample shouldn't become a permanent description of the corn being fed. The average micron size matters but so does the distribution of particles within the sample and the consistency of that grind from one delivery to the next. There probably isn't one micron number that tells the entire story for every dairy. Ration composition, corn source, processing method and particle-size distribution all need to be considered. What we can do is establish what we want the corn to look like, measure it periodically and make sure we're receiving that product consistently. We spend a lot of time looking for the next opportunity to improve feed efficiency. Sometimes it is worth going back and measuring the basics. We are already buying the corn. We are already putting it in the ration every day. The question is whether we're getting everything out of it that we paid for. Related Who is in your corner when times get tough?
