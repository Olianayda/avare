U.S.-Mexico border reopens to live cattle imports Tyrell Marchant September 2, 2026 On Aug. 24, the port of entry in Douglas, Arizona, resumed imports of live cattle, bison and horses from Mexico. It is the first step in a planned phased reopening of live cattle trade between the two countries that was halted in May 2025 due to concerns over the northward spread of New World screwworm (NWS). Tyrell Marchant Editor / Progressive Cattle Email Tyrell Marchant Related Screwworm less than 70 miles from the U.S.-Mexico border USDA suspends cattle imports from Mexico USDA announces plans to reopen border for Mexican cattle imports after screwworm closure In the days leading up to the scheduled reopening of the Douglas port, concern arose that the plan might be pushed back further after the Aug. 17 detection of the first active case of NWS in the Mexican state of Sonora, which borders Arizona. Sonora activated emergency surveillance, containment and control protocols, and the USDA deemed conditions adequate to accept Mexican cattle at Douglas on Aug. 24. The USDA reported that at total of 716 head of feeder cattle from Sonora were presented for entry, with 692 passing inspection and crossing the border. There was no indication of NWS infection in the 24 animals denied entry; they were rejected out of an abundance of caution due to open cuts and scratches. Sexes, weights and specific destinations of the accepted animals was not immediately made available. The reopening is set to begin with imports of 700 head of cattle per day through the Douglas port in the first week, 900 per day the second week and a gradual increase to 1,300 head per day. Image courtesy of USDA-APHIS. The process required by the USDA’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) for Mexican cattle to enter the U.S. requires inspections from an accredited Mexican veterinarian as well as government veterinarians from both the U.S. and Mexico. They are required to be treated with an injection of ivermectin or dormectin at the ranch of origin in Mexico, visually inspected at the Mexican export facility, and put through a dipping vat under supervision of a USDA veterinarian before crossing into the U.S. If NWS is detected at any point during the inspection process, all entry will be immediately halted again until the facility can be disinfected and deemed free of any possible contamination. The reopening of the port of entry in Santa Teresa, New Mexico, is contingent on at least 30 successful operational days in Douglas. The port at Columbus, New Mexico, will then be eligible for reopening upon 30 successful days at the Santa Teresa crossing. As yet, there is no schedule for the opening of other ports of entry along the border in Texas and Arizona, though the USDA has struck a hopeful tone that live animal imports can resume in the coming months.
