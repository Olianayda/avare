Tyson September 7, 2026 Whether resurfacing or grooving new concrete, these guidelines can help improve cow comfort on concrete flooring throughout the dairy operation. Cows require a walking surface that provides traction, good hoof support and minimum chance of injury. The durability of concrete makes it the preferred choice for walking surfaces in dairy shelters, but careful consideration must be given to the quality, strength and finish to create a surface suitable for dairy cattle. Flat surface between grooves
Smooth (not slippery) surface between grooves
Smooth groove edge with right angle between groove and floor surface edge
Proper groove width, depth and spacing
New concrete cow alleys   require special attention. The following guidelines provide a basis to developing quality concrete cow alleys:
Develop a well-drained and compacted sub-grade
Use 3,500 pounds per square inch (psi) or better air entrained concrete and 4-inch or less slump
Floor thickness of 4 to 8 inches depending on anticipated vehicle weight
Create level surface across alley width to prevent liquid puddling/removing exposed aggregate
Grooves may be formed as concrete sets or sawn in after curing
Broom finish on surface between grooves (no steel toweling)
Moist cure (cover) for three to seven days, which improves strength and minimizes shrinking
Grooves Grooves help improve traction and drain moisture away from the floor surface. Current recommendations aim to prevent initial slip with a groove size and pattern that allows the cow to place each foot on a groove with each step.
Preferred groove size is 3/8- to 1/2-inch wide and 3/8- to 1/2-inch deep.
Parallel groove floor pattern should be 2.5 to 3.5 inches on center, with grooves following the length of the alley in the direction of scraper travel.
Diamond groove floor pattern should be 3 to 5 inches on center. This pattern offers better traction in more directions than parallel groove and is highly recommended for all cow alleys, but especially where cows turn.
Grooves formed in wet concrete are time-sensitive and require installation experience. If concrete is too wet, grooves tend to fill in, and if concrete is too dry, grooves may not be adequate depth.
Sawn grooves offer more control of the floor pattern and quality of the groove and produce a clean, well-defined groove edge. For this reason, sawn grooves are preferred in new construction.
Preparing and sealing concrete surfaces Properly prepare new concrete surfaces   before introducing cattle. Inspect and prepare them to minimize the chance of injury. Dragging a heavy concrete block, or using a steel scraper with down pressure, over the cow alleys several times will remove exposed sharp edges and grind down exposed aggregate. Then sweep and power wash the alleys to remove debris. Finally, walk on the alleys with bare feet. If they are uncomfortable for you, the same is probably true for the cows. Advertisement Sealing concrete   can minimize moisture penetration, provide better wear resistance and reduce the alkalinity of newly poured concrete. Portland cement concrete can be an extraordinarily alkaline material for days or months. However, normal carbonation of concrete reduces the pH to 8.5 to 9, although that reaction usually is restricted to a thin layer at the surface. The “pore water” can be as high as 13. This level of alkalinity can be a concern and may irritate cow feet. Properly sealing the concrete floor surface fills the pores and reduces the risk. Common sealants mixtures used on concrete cow alleys include boiled linseed oil and a 50/50 solution of boiled linseed oil and mineral spirits. Sealant can be applied to concrete surface when temperatures are above 60°F by spraying, brushing or rolling. Apply a second coat 24 hours later after first coat has dried. Water should bead on a properly sealed floor. Rubber flooring and pasture Resilient flooring materials   can offer cows some relief from hard, unyielding concrete surfaces. Commonly used materials include reclaimed rubber belting, rolled rubber flooring and rubber mats. Each of these materials requires a solid base for support. Resilience and durability can vary significantly between suppliers, so select a product suitable for the intended purpose and location. More resilient materials provide better traction and comfort but may need more frequent replacement. More durable materials hold up better to vehicle traffic but provide less cushion and often become slippery when wet. Areas to consider resilient flooring, in order of priority, include:
Milking center holding area, where cows stand idle for longer periods of time
Travel lanes (more than 200 feet) to reduce excessive wear of the cow’s hoof
Hospital area
Feeding alleys
Cow alleys
Access to pasture and/or a well-drained, unpaved exercise lot can also provide cows relief from walking and standing on hard floor surfaces. The lot should be free of materials such as stones that may be embedded into the cow’s foot and/or tracked onto hard surfaces. Improving traction on existing flooring Years of scraping manure from cow alleys, especially with a steel blade, can polish the floor surface smooth. Resurfacing provides cows a more confident footing. Common resurfacing methods include:
Sawn grooves.  Diamond pattern following the groove size and spacing described earlier.
Scabbling  uses a series of hammers to smash the surface, creating wide irregular ribbons. Depth of concrete removal should be limited to less than 1/4-inch. Deep-scabbled grooves create uneven surfaces that are uncomfortable for cows.
Milling   is a process that removes a thin layer (about 1/8-inch) of concrete leaving a relatively level surface with a corduroy appearance.
Microgrooving   uses a series of narrow, closely spaced blades to create texture on the smooth area between existing deep grooves.
Summary Reducing lameness will improve the well-being of dairy cows and improve profitability. Dairy shelters contain features that may directly or indirectly cause or contribute to the severity of lameness in dairy cows. Confinement facilities offer several advantages to both cows and caregivers. Producers have the opportunity to closely monitor the health and performance of the cows in their care. Recognizing the facility, feeding and management factors that contribute to lameness, and making adjustments to prevent, or at least minimize, their role can go a long way in improving herd health and the success of the dairy business.
