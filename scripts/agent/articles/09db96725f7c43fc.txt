Building better replacement heifers: The critical role of trace mineral nutrition Laurentia van Rensburg October 8, 2026 A strong cow herd starts with a strong group of heifers. Understanding how their nutritional needs differ from mature cows can go a long way toward maximizing their long-term potential. A replacement heifer is more than the next female entering the herd. She is an investment in the future productivity, reproductive efficiency and profitability of the cow-calf operation. Developing that investment successfully takes more than just putting pounds on an animal. From weaning through first calving, a replacement heifer must continue growing, reach puberty, conceive, maintain pregnancy, raise a calf and eventually transition into a productive mature cow. The roller coaster of trace mineral needs The role of trace minerals in embryo transfer pregnancies Energy and protein are typically at the center of discussions about heifer development, and for good reason. But there is another part of the nutritional program that often receives less attention than it deserves: optimizing trace mineral status. Trace minerals are required in relatively small amounts, yet they participate in virtually every major physiological process associated with growth, immunity and reproduction. For a replacement heifer, that makes trace minerals an important piece of the developmental equation. Heifers vs. mature cows The first step in developing replacement heifers is recognizing that they have different nutritional needs. A mature cow has completed most of her structural growth. Her nutritional priorities revolve around maintenance, pregnancy, lactation, body condition and rebreeding. Advertisement A replacement heifer, however, is doing all of this while she is still growing. Therefore, the focus should not necessarily be on creating the biggest heifer possible. The goal should be to develop an animal that reaches an appropriate percentage of mature bodyweight, achieves puberty at the right time and enters the breeding season prepared to conceive. This is where trace mineral supplementation becomes particularly important as the developing reproductive system, skeletal system, immune system and growing tissues all depend on adequate supplies of essential trace minerals. Trace mineral functions Trace minerals such as copper, zinc, manganese and selenium may be required in much smaller quantities than energy or protein, but their biological roles are critically important. Copper is involved in connective tissue formation, iron metabolism, immune response and antioxidant defense. Zinc is important for cellular replication, protein synthesis, immune function and reproductive processes. Manganese contributes to skeletal development and reproductive function, while selenium plays an important role in antioxidant protection, immune and reproductive function. For a replacement heifer, these functions are not occurring independently. She is developing bone and muscle while her reproductive system is maturing. She is building immune competence while adapting to changes in diet, environment and management. At the same time, she is preparing for one of the most demanding events in her life: getting bred and raising a calf. Trace mineral nutrition should thus not be viewed simply as a way to prevent a visible deficiency. It should be viewed as part of providing the nutritional foundation required for normal development and future productivity. More is not necessarily better When it comes to trace mineral nutrition, one challenge is that the conversation often focuses on amount: How many parts per million of copper? How much zinc and selenium? These values are important, but they are only part of the story: Trace mineral concentration in a diet does not necessarily tell us how much of that mineral the animal can effectively utilize. Trace mineral source, interactions among minerals, forage composition and antagonists can all influence trace mineral bioavailability and trace mineral status. Since organic trace minerals are typically better absorbed and more bioavailable, they can often be supplemented at lower levels with better results. For example, a recent study evaluated the effect of proteinated organic trace minerals on fertility parameters in successive generations. Cows were supplemented with either standard inclusion rates of inorganic minerals or lower levels in the form of proteinated trace minerals for the duration of two calving cycles. Heifer calves from cows supplemented with proteinated organic trace minerals had better average daily gains, body condition scores and were confirmed pregnant between eight and 18 days earlier than heifers from cows supplemented with inorganic trace minerals. Similarly, in a dairy study evaluating the long-term effect of organic trace minerals on growth, reproductive performance and first lactation in dairy heifers, it was concluded that heifers supplemented with proteinated organic trace minerals not only had higher milk yield but also had an improvement in age at calving, which may affect future reproductive lifespan. The goal is not simply to feed more mineral but to deliver the right minerals, in an appropriate form, at the right time and at a level that meets the animal’s needs. Don’t wait until breeding season One of the biggest opportunities in replacement heifer management is to take a proactive approach to trace mineral nutrition. By the time a heifer enters breeding season, much of her developmental journey has already occurred. Her skeletal growth, body composition and reproductive development have been progressing for months. If mineral status has been inadequate during that period, adding a mineral supplement immediately before breeding may not fully correct the situation. Mineral supplementation should be part of the development program – not a last-minute reproductive strategy. A good program starts with understanding the animal’s requirements and evaluating the factors that influence mineral availability, including trace mineral source, forage mineral concentrations, water quality and potential antagonists. The cost of getting it wrong Replacement heifer development is expensive. By the time a heifer enters her first breeding season, the producer has already invested in genetics, feed, pasture, labor, health, mineral supplementation and management. That makes the economics of heifer development different from simply minimizing the cost of each individual input. A heifer that reaches puberty on time, conceives early, calves successfully and rebreeds has the opportunity to return that investment over multiple productive years. Studies show that heifer calving date positively influences calf weaning weights through at least six parturitions; simply put, heifers that calved early in the calving season with their first calf had increased longevity and pounds weaned compared to heifers calving later. Reproductive performance in ruminants is highly dependent on their nutritional and trace mineral status. Trace minerals are necessary for synthesis of reproductive hormones as well as fetal growth and development. This is why trace mineral programs should be evaluated in the context of production outcomes rather than simply the cost per ton of minerals and why form or source of trace minerals play such a critical role. Given the impact of reproductive efficiency and pounds weaned per exposed female on profitability, that is ultimately where the value of trace mineral nutrition should be measured. A replacement heifer gets only one opportunity to be developed correctly. Investing in her is not simply an investment in the heifer standing in the pasture today. It is an investment in the cow she can become – and in the future of the herd. Related Can trace mineral nutrition influence growth-enhancing technology response?
