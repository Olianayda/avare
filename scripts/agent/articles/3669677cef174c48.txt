Consider fall cover crop options Lynn Grooms September 14, 2026 Fall‑seeded cereals – especially early‑maturing cereal rye – offer dependable overwintering and spring biomass. Mixes with small grains, legumes and brassicas add grazing flexibility and nutrient‑cycling when seeded early. For fall cover crop establishment, overwintering and biomass production, forage producers have several options, including new early-maturing cereal rye varieties. Depending on their cropping systems and weather conditions, they may also consider mixtures of small grains, legumes and brassicas. University research can help producers fine-tune those decisions. Reliable overwintering and forage value make cereal rye a popular choice in states such as Iowa, Nebraska and Wisconsin. The cool-season winter annual grass establishes in the fall when planted from mid-August to late October. Given the right conditions, it can also be planted as late as mid-November.  Biomass quantity depends on planting and termination dates as well as precipitation, but cereal rye can produce from 2,500 to 6,000 pounds of dry matter per acre with excellent grazing quality, according to Iowa State University and the USDA’s Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) in Iowa. The institutions collaborated to produce a series of fact sheets. A link to the series is included at the end of this article. In Wisconsin, cereal rye can be planted almost up to the time the soil freezes. It won’t emerge then but will generally germinate in late March. By the time temperatures hit 65ºF to 70ºF, cereal rye biomass will explode, says Dan Smith, University of Wisconsin –
Madison Division of Extension weed science outreach specialist. University of Wisconsin researchers have studied a wide range of cereal rye varieties, including variety not stated (VNS) as well as early-maturing varieties. Madison.   "Some of the new early-maturing varieties grew as much as knee-high to thigh-high by mid-May the following year," says Smith. In one study, the researchers planted cereal rye varieties at a rate of 1 bushel per acre at a depth of 1 inch before no-till soybeans. If using such a system and spring soil is dry enough, farmers could terminate the cereal rye earlier or wait until the soybeans emerge. The researchers planted an early-maturing variety, and by Memorial Day it was in full anthesis. They crimped the cereal rye. Cereal rye will generally produce more biomass than winter wheat or triticale, making it the cover crop of choice for most Midwest farmers, according to Katja Koehler-Cole, an educator at the University of Nebraska Extension, and her colleagues who co-authored “Selecting the Right Winter Small Grain Cover Crop This Fall.” The team found that the quick spring growth of cereal rye can add as much as 1,000 pounds per acre of biomass per week in late April to early May. But winter wheat's shorter stature can make it more manageable than cereal rye, which can grow to 5 feet by mid-May, they noted. The Nebraska researchers studied the forage quality of three small-grain cover crops – cereal rye, wheat and triticale. Their total digestible nutrients and crude protein percentages are shown in Table 1 . "In Wisconsin, oats establish reliably after corn silage or small-grain harvest," says Smith. "Planted in late September, oats produce a lot of biomass – because they winterkill, they don’t have to be terminated in the spring.” In Iowa, oat cover crops can be planted from mid-August to mid-September. Again, biomass quantity depends on planting and termination dates and precipitation, but oats can produce from 1,000 to 4,000 pounds of dry matter per acre and grazing quality is very good, according to the Iowa NRCS. Triticale is another fall-planted cover crop option. It’s typically planted from mid-August to late October in Iowa. “While you could plant it in November, you’d see more winterkill,” says Mark Licht, Iowa State University associate professor and extension cropping systems specialist. “It’s better to plant triticale in early October.” Other considerations are whether there’s wheat in the rotation, which often precludes the growing of cereal rye or triticale, according to University of Nebraska researchers. Wheat and triticale may mature later than cereal rye. If grazing is intended for the cover crop, growing wheat or triticale can extend the grazing season. Both produced less biomass than cereal rye but retained forage quality later into spring, the researchers noted. Triticale produces from 2,000 to 5,000 pounds of biomass per acre per year, depending on precipitation and planting and termination dates, according to the Iowa NRCS. Cattle graze a fall-planted cover crop. Image provided by Dan Smith, University of Wisconsin –
Madison. Mixtures considered There’s been increasing interest in planting cover crop mixtures for grazing purposes. Legumes and brassicas may be added to small grains to enhance forage value. In Iowa, Licht has studied mixtures of small grains, forage turnip, forage radish, hairy vetch and balansa clover. He studied the mixtures primarily to evaluate how they reduce soil nitrates. Forage turnips and radishes planted from mid-August to early September can provide good fall grazing value. Producers could plant them after an early soybean crop or drill them in after silage or seed corn harvest.  "Balansa clover is more difficult to establish and isn’t consistent over winter – and forage cabbage and kale will generally winterkill," adds Licht. Red clover and crimson clover also can be challenging. “They’re slower to establish and can be difficult to overwinter,” he says. “And there’s generally not enough fall precipitation for them to do well.” Licht and his colleagues found that small grains tend to dominate mixtures. While legumes and brassicas are part of the biomass, they’re a smaller part. Reducing the percentage of small grain in a mix with legumes and brassicas is challenging. If planting 1.25 million to 1.5 million seeds per acre, one might try planting less than 50% of the mixture to a small grain and split the remaining amount evenly between the legumes and brassicas.  "There’s no definitive mix – it depends on personal preference. Some producers have had more success with brassicas," says Licht and adds that research showed that the biomass tonnage of mixtures is similar to single species of small grains.
