Where potato harvest shapes the school calendar Cassidy Woolsey October 7, 2026 As Idaho potato harvest rolls in, students trade classrooms for fields, working alongside farmers and experiencing firsthand the harvest that helps sustain their communities. There’s spring break, there’s Thanksgiving break, and there’s Christmas break, but for some Idaho schools there’s also harvest break – and it coincides with the area’s potato harvest. Across eastern Idaho, communities from Grace to Shelley and Rexburg to Aberdeen have built their school calendars around that reality. The traditions look a little different from district to district, but the premise is the same: When the potatoes are ready, school becomes the hands-on kind. According to Grace School District Superintendent Jason Moss, about 95% of the district’s older students participate in the area’s annual harvest. Advertisement Stoddard Farms sells russets, reds and yellow potatoes, as well as other produce and goods, at their farmers market in Grace. Image by Cassidy Woolsey. But the break is about more than getting potatoes out of the ground. Moss says it also gives students an opportunity to develop a work ethic that stays with them long after harvest is over. They can earn a substantial amount of money in just a couple of weeks, but the work is far from easy. During the break, students involved in school activities might practice at 6 a.m. before heading straight to the fields, where they can work until 10 p.m. They go home, go to bed and do it all over again the next day. “It is a sight to see,” Moss says. “It brings our community together.” Stoddard Farms is one of the Grace area’s seed potato growers. Now in its third generation, Stoddard Farms has benefited from and greatly appreciated the local help for many years. “It would be difficult,” says J.J. Stoddard. “We definitely need quite a few hands. We do have machinery that has taken the place of employees, but we still need those bodies to pull it off. There’s only so much that machines can do. We still have to have the human element to it.” This year, roughly 25 students are working for the farm, with some helping package potatoes and others working at the farmers market, where they load vehicles, direct traffic, greet customers, operate the cash register and even cook and serve french fries. Depending on the crew, some are working 10- to 12-hour days, Stoddard says. Many of the students return year after year, Stoddard says, and some come to the farm because an older sibling has worked there. Others reach out as early as midsummer looking for a harvest job. Students remove dirt clods and other debris at Webster’s Inc. in Rexburg.  Image provided by Jason Webster. Cole Barthlome, 16, is one of those students. This is his fifth or sixth year working during potato harvest, he says, and while the paycheck is part of the appeal, he also enjoys working alongside his friends. “Might as well work, if we’re off school, to get some money,” Barthlome says. Madi Musgrave, 17, is new to the Grace area and decided she wanted to experience the harvest tradition for herself. “I just thought it was kind of cool, like part of the culture, because I’ve never done it before,” Musgrave says. “It’s good to get to know lots of different people as well.” On her third day of harvest, Musgrave had already found herself doing a little bit of everything, from lifting and loading potato bags into customers’ vehicles to washing potatoes, helping make french fries and working the cash register. For Musgrave, the experience was already teaching her something about the community – and about work itself. “Hard work can be fun with people,” she says. Farther north, in Rexburg, potato grower Jason Webster of fifth-generation Webster's Inc. says students make up about 70% of his harvest workforce. He hires students from Madison, Sugar-Salem, South Fremont and North Fremont high schools, as well as West Jefferson High School. “You can see how detrimental that’d be to us if they ever decided to get rid of the harvest break. I mean, there goes 70 percent of my workforce,” Webster says. Webster’s Inc. employees and family members dig potatoes early in the morning. Image provided by Jason Webster. But keeping the break has not always been easy. Webster says farmers in the area have spent years defending the tradition as school boards have debated whether it fits the needs of the broader community. “There were probably 10 to 15 years in the last 20 years where we didn’t know if we were going to have it or not,” he says. “They were having school board meetings and arguments, and there are a lot of people who don’t like the break because it messes up their schedule. It’s tough because they let out kindergarten through high school, and we only hire a handful of those kids and the rest are terrorizing their parents. So I understand their frustrations with it.” Still, Webster says the students who do spend their break working on the farm gain an education that can be difficult to replicate in a classroom.  “We’re talking about everything,” he says. “We’re talking about economics. We’re talking about food supply chains. We’re talking about work ethics. We’re talking about what it means to be a good employee, what it means to be a safe employee, what you can and can’t do at a worksite.” Webster gives them responsibilities that go well beyond simple labor. High school students operate pilers, sort dirt clods, rocks and vines, run tractors and fill more than half of his truck-driving positions. The work also gives some students their first real connection to agriculture. “Most of them have never been on a farm,” Webster says. “Most of them have no idea where their food came from other than the grocery store.” That connection is becoming more important as Rexburg grows, Webster says, with families moving into the area from across the country. And while the work is demanding, harvest also creates a sense of community. Each evening around 6 p.m., Webster’s entire crew – truck drivers, digger operators and cellar workers – comes together for dinner, with his wife cooking for more than 30 people. A student helps load a 50-pound bag of russets at Stoddard Farms’ Farmers Market.  Image by Cassidy Woolsey. The logistics  As one could imagine, planning harvest break isn’t an easy feat. Weather is a huge factor in determining when harvest can begin, and no one has a crystal ball. Some school districts are more flexible, while others set a date and stick to it. Grace School District tends to be a little more flexible. Moss says the break typically falls during the third and fourth weeks of September, sometimes stretching into October depending on the year. Each year, he connects with the president of the Potato Growers Association to discuss when farmers expect to need student labor. The association president then checks with other growers before the district settles on its dates. Staff are typically notified by Labor Day. This year, farmers determined they needed to start harvest earlier, so the district moved the break up by a week. Not every district, however, adjusts its calendar in quite the same way. Douglas McLaren, Shelley School District superintendent, says their harvest break is determined when the school board approves the school calendar. The intent is for the break to coincide with the local potato harvest. However, because the calendar is established so far in advance, there is no way to account for all of the variables that can influence when the actual harvest takes place.
