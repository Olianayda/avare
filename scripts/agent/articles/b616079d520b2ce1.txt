Time for safety, a season for awareness Marian Viney September 7, 2026 Fall harvest tightens every margin – time, pace, attention – making National Farm Safety and Health Week a reminder that safety is the habit that keeps routine from becoming risk. Sept. 21-26 marks National Farm Safety and Health Week, a reminder that safety isn’t a separate chore on the farm or ranch – it’s part of every decision we make. Fall harvest tightens everything: the weather window, schedule, pace, margin for error. It’s the season when urgency becomes instinct – and instinct, as every farmer or rancher knows, is where blind spots come to light. Email Marian Viney John Mitchell’s story  is one we wish we didn’t have to share but one we can’t afford to forget. A routine forage harvest chore – clearing a plug on a pull‑type chopper – became an accident that changed a life in an instant. In the seconds it took to lean in and feed corn into the rolls, Mitchell was pulled into the machine. Part of his right arm was gone, his left foot nearly gone, and his face was inches from the feed rolls. Mitchell survived because his brother arrived quickly, rescue crews used their skills, and MedFlight got him to immediate care. Now, he farms because AgrAbility of Wisconsin provided producer‑centered adaptations – custom‑modified equipment, reconfigured controls, ultrasensitive foot pedals – tools built around his abilities, not his limitations. His message is simple: It takes two seconds – two seconds to lose balance, two seconds to reach into a machine that isn’t fully stopped, two seconds to assume a chore you’ve done a thousand times will go the same way it always has. I grew up with those moments etched in memory. One of the earliest was on our Illinois farm, where our sledding hill became anything but fun. My brother was driving our Allis Chalmers 45 with a wagon when the tractor rolled going downhill, throwing him between the seat and the wheel fender. I remember never seeing my dad run so fast. A shoelace caught on the pedal kept my brother from being pulled out. He walked away with a scratch on his ribs – God looking over him, nothing more. Advertisement Years later, I learned my own lesson unloading corn. I leaned in a little too far – far enough to get pulled in. I grabbed the edge of the wagon, pulled as hard as I could and managed to get myself out of the corn, fear probably giving me the strength to do it because no one else was around. These aren’t isolated stories. They’re part of a pattern seen nationwide. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), agriculture recorded more than 21,000 injuries requiring days away from work between 2021 and 2022, and the industry’s fatality rate remains among the highest in the nation at 18.6 deaths per 100,000 workers. Transportation incidents – including machinery and equipment – are the leading cause of agricultural deaths. Nearly one-third of injuries requiring days away from work are falls, and more than half of agricultural fatalities occur among workers 55 and older. A 2024 study supported by the CDC estimates more than $11 billion in annual agricultural injury costs nationwide. According to the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration, agricultural workers experienced 476 fatalities and 36,900 nonfatal injuries in 2024. National Farm Safety and Health Week has been recognized by presidential proclamation since 1944, underscoring the need to highlight the hazards of agricultural work.
The National Education Center for Agricultural Safety reinforces these statistics and remains the only organization with a hands‑on farm equipment safety training center, offering programs ranging from tractor rollover rescue to grain entrapment extrication – the scenarios that continue to claim lives. Fall harvest amplifies every risk. This fall, widen the window in the only way we can: by slowing the rush, not the work; choosing planning over pressure; making safety part of the harvest strategy instead of something that waits its turn. Because it only takes two seconds for everything to change – and two seconds to make the safer choice.
