Milking point checklist: Are all moving parts up to par? Rick Vander Veen October 9, 2026 Scheduled milking system maintenance is a non-negotiable requirement for the milking facility, and understanding its value and importance is essential. The dairy industry plays a vital role in creating the world’s most perfect food enjoyed in many different ways. Just as vast as the variety of dairy products we consume is the number of ways dairies are different, including their size, the way they milk and handle cows, and their goals and management strategies. The milking facility must produce a high-quality product 24-7, 365 days of the year – and be more efficient this year than the last. Milk must be produced in a way that protects the dairy’s most precious asset: the cow. The milking facility and equipment should be set up and kept in excellent condition so the people responsible for harvesting milk can meet the farm’s quality standards and udder health goals. This is why scheduled maintenance at a dairy facility is so critical. The forementioned goals cannot be consistently achieved if the milking center is not maintained, checked and serviced on a consistent basis. The reality is, if you don’t schedule your service, it will schedule itself for you. And often, it won’t be when it is convenient and it will cost more compared to if it was scheduled. These added costs may also have ripple effects in areas that go unnoticed. Ben Franklin famously said to his fellow Philadelphians, “An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure,” pleading that they focus on preventing fires rather than fighting them, which was spot on then and is true today. Preventing problems in your parlor and herd is more cost-effective than treating or repairing when failure occurs. Change is inevitable, and the focus on needing more out of your equipment, out of your cows and out of your people will continue. Today, many conventional parlors are now running longer and milking more cows per stall point per day. No longer is a stall point milking 60 cows per day, but over 100 and even up to 150 cows per day. Many box robots complete over 200 milkings per day. This means an issue with one pulsator, one takeoff or one claw has a much broader impact on the herd than before. Scheduled maintenance and routine service of milking systems can have a positive impact on a dairy’s finances in a number of ways: Advertisement
Maximum milk quality. The cost of production is there either way; capitalizing on milk premiums means additional margin.
Optimal udder health. With low animal inventories and the high cost of replacements, striving for minimal mastitis and a low cull rate are keys to being profitable. Take care of the cows, and they will take care of you.
Lower operational costs. A trained technician works more efficiently when service is scheduled, which in turns lowers your cost when items can be serviced at the optimal time and around your milking schedule. Changing milk pump seals or rebuilding pulsators between milkings takes less time and does not inconvenience employees or cows. Most dealerships also have lower hourly service rates for scheduled service compared to emergency service.
Improved efficiency. Issues like a slow crowd gate, a stall with a bad pulsator, a faulty milk pump or a flooding system during washdown can have a negative impact on your people and cow flow. When the equipment is not working well, it elevates the frustration level of a team. Equip them to do their tasks correctly and efficiency improves.
A cleaning analysis of milking equipment helps identify inefficiencies in the parlor.  Image courtesy of DeLaval. If scheduled maintenance and routine service are worth doing, then what is the plan for success? This is where partnering with a milking equipment provider comes in. Every part and component in a system has an expected life. Working with a dealership technician to develop a timely and consistent schedule to service or replace worn items before they fail is mission critical. Key items for scheduled maintenance include:
Pulsators: Perform regular graphing to check operation and rebuild pulsators to eliminate failures to avoid improper milkout, which could lead to mastitis.
Claws: Replace gaskets periodically and routinely check air distributors and outlets so claw vacuum stays consistent during milking and bacteria does not build up, affecting milk quality.
Milk pumps: Replace seals to eliminate downtime during milking and wash problems, protecting milk quality.
Automation/milk meters:  Replace shut-off diaphragms regularly to keep claw vacuum consistent and milk from penetrating the vacuum line. Retract ropes or chains should also be replaced before failing, which could slow down parlor flow and be a risk to milk quality.
Vacuum system: Check vacuum level and pump capacity to ensure optimal milk harvesting and vacuum stability. Also, change oil, filters and belts to ensure peak equipment life and reduce the risk of down time.
Air system: Regularly service air compressors and air driers to ensure optimal equipment life and minimal downtime.
Crowd gates: Regularly grease, oil and service to ensure optimal equipment life and smooth, consistent cow flow.
Parlor/gates: Lubricate and service cylinders and gates to ensure peak cow flow with minimal milker interaction.
CIP system: Rebuild chemical pumps, air injectors, diverter valves for consistent washing and optimal milk quality.
Milk/pulsation hoses: Replace all hoses on a regular basis to ensure optimal udder health and milk quality.
Pipeline gaskets and rubber goods: Replace gaskets before inking, cracking or leaking to protect milk quality, and avoid vacuum leaks, which can impact udder health.
Tanks/silos: Replace agitator seals, tank seals and wash pump seals routinely and perform regular gearbox servicing to protect milk quality and equipment life.
Cooling system: Routinely check freon levels, clean condensers and check glycol levels to ensure milk quality and optimal efficiency and minimize the risk of losing milk.
A local milking equipment dealer is there to help a dairy achieve success. If no scheduled service plan is in place today, reach out to a specialist to get one started. A local dealership can also do an in-depth audit of a dairy’s current scheduled maintenance program to identify gaps and help them reach their milk quality or udder health goals. At the end of the day, it comes down to peace of mind. This is what scheduled maintenance can provide, in addition to healthy cows and happy people – and it is hard to put a price on that.
