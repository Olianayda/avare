Walk around enough dairies, and you'll eventually hear someone say, "People just aren't accountable anymore." Usually, the comment comes after something goes wrong. I heard one dairy manager lament that a manure pump was making noise for days before it failed, but no one said anything before it was too late. The natural reaction is to blame the employees and a lack of accountability. More often than not, they're clarity problems, support problems or ownership problems. In other words, leaders often try to fix accountability before they have built the conditions that make accountability possible. The best operations I've worked with tend to get three things right: alignment, accessibility and accountability. Those three things are not complicated, but they do require a plan. When they are in place, accountability becomes less of a struggle and more of a culture of accountability. Accountability is not created through pressure. It is created through clarity, support and ownership. Alignment: Everybody needs the same playbook People can't hit a target they can't see or don’t understand. One of the biggest mistakes leaders make is assuming everyone understands the job the same way they do. The reality is that dairy owners and managers have years and decades of experience. What seems obvious to them is often far less obvious to someone who started six months ago. More employees are coming to the farm with zero farm experience, so making an assumption that something is “so obvious" is a dangerous mindset. Advertisement Think about something as simple as telling an employee to "take care of the calf barn." To one person, that might mean feeding calves. To another, it means cleaning pens. Someone else might focus on monitoring animal health. None of them are intentionally doing a poor job. They are simply operating from different definitions of what success looks like. It is not obvious and not clear. That is why alignment matters. Effective leaders create alignment by clearly defining expectations and documenting critical processes. Standard operating procedures are valuable because they put everyone on the same page. They create a common understanding of how important tasks should be performed. It also ensures that every employee is trained to the same standard. The goal isn't to create more paperwork. In fact, we aren’t a fan of complicated and paper-based standard operating procedures (SOPs). Many farms have moved to digital-only SOPs that are always available and easily updated. However you do it, the goal is to reduce confusion, and I've found that people rarely fail expectations they clearly understand. More often, they fail expectations that were never fully defined in the first place. I fall into the habit myself. My kids call it “Dad instructions,” and it involves them guessing what’s on my mind, trying to keep up with half-thoughts and incomplete sentences. The more I have on my mind, the more stress … the worse it gets. No wonder they don’t always hit the mark. It is barely clear in my own head, let alone theirs. When leaders take the time to create clarity around expectations and processes, a surprising number of so-called accountability problems disappear on their own. Accessibility: Help without creating dependency Once expectations are clear, leaders need to be accessible. Employees need support and coaching, and they need someone they can turn to when they encounter a challenge. However, this is where many leaders unintentionally create a problem. Let’s say an employee runs into a difficult situation. The manager jumps in and fixes it. Maybe the manager makes the decision. Maybe the supervisor mediates the issue between two employees. The problem gets solved quickly. At first, that feels like supportive leadership. Over time, it creates dependence. This might work when the dairy is small and maybe just a few employees. But at scale, dependency on leadership will grind leaders down with the sheer number of problems to solve. Every time we rescue someone, we teach them where the answer lives. If the answer always lives with the boss, eventually the boss becomes the bottleneck. I've met many leaders who are frustrated because employees won't take initiative. At the same time, those same leaders are answering every question, solving every problem and making every important decision. Those two things simply cannot co-exist. Employees who are new to a role need plenty of support. They need instruction and coaching, and if we don’t give it, they are very apt to become disillusioned and disengage. But as they grow, our role should begin to shift from providing answers to helping them think through solutions. Instead of immediately telling them what to do to solve their problem, try asking: ”What do you think we should do?” ”What options have you thought of?” Those questions may take a little longer in the moment, but they build judgment, confidence and problem-solving skills over time. The goal of leadership is not to create employees who depend on you. The goal is to develop employees you can depend on. Accountability blueprint: Ownership of the result This is where accountability finally enters the conversation. Notice, I said finally. Most leaders start here, but the best leaders build toward it. It becomes part of the culture, not something people are forced into. Forcing accountability doesn’t work any better than forcing cattle movements. It works a little bit, but mostly it doesn’t. One tool we use with clients is an accountability blueprint. Every major work area of the business is mapped to a singular owner. Someone owns herd health, someone owns maintenance, someone owns feed, someone owns people management. When ownership is unclear, accountability becomes nearly impossible because everyone assumes someone else is handling the issue. Especially the hard issues that require leadership. When we build an accountability blueprint, the conversation is straightforward: What outcome are we trying to achieve? Who owns it? What does success look like? Those questions move accountability beyond doing tasks and into ownership of outcomes. Consider the difference between these two assignments: "Clean the maternity pen" versus "Make sure the maternity area is always ready to support a clean, safe and healthy calving." The first is a task; the second is an outcome. The distinction matters because people tend to think differently when they own a result instead of a checklist. When employees understand the outcome they are responsible for, they begin solving problems before they become issues. They make decisions. They improve processes. They take initiative. In short, they stop acting like task-doers and start acting like owners. Accountability starts with leadership Every dairy operation needs accountability. But accountability is not created through tougher conversations, tighter controls or more pressure. It starts much earlier. Accountability is something you build into the culture through clear expectations, growing responsibility and mutual respect. When leaders create alignment, provide support without rescuing and delegate outcomes instead of tasks, accountability stops being a management problem and starts becoming part of the culture. And when that happens, leaders spend less time chasing people and more time developing them. That's how stronger teams and stronger dairies are built.
