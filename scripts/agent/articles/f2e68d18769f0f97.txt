Image by Dawson Uhl, South Dakota State University. Virtual fencing: Key considerations before adoption Jameson Brennan August 27, 2026 Is virtual fencing right for your operation? It might be, but there are some important points every ranch needs to consider before adopting the technology. Virtual fencing is increasingly being adopted by livestock producers as a tool to control livestock movement and distribution across the landscape. The technology uses GPS-enabled collars and mapping software to create boundaries that influence where livestock can and cannot graze. These boundaries are then transmitted to GPS-enabled collars worn by livestock. Animals are managed via a combination of auditory and electrical cues in relation to virtual boundaries, similar to an electric collar used for dogs. This provides producers with considerable flexibility to change grazing areas without constructing or moving physical fences. Although virtual fencing creates numerous opportunities to improve grazing management, producers should consider several factors before investing in this technology. Identify your management objective Perhaps the most important question before purchasing virtual fencing is: What problem are you trying to solve? Virtual fencing can be an exciting technology, but purchasing it simply because it is a new, exciting tool may not provide sufficient value for an operation. Producers should first identify specific grazing or livestock management objectives and then determine whether virtual fencing is the most appropriate tool for accomplishing them. Alternatives should be considered for accomplishing your grazing objective, such as the use of physical or temporary polywire fence, while evaluating the cost benefit of materials and labor to achieve those goals. Potential objectives might include increasing the number of grazing rotations, more even forage utilization, protecting sensitive wildlife habitat, controlling access to creeks or streams, implementing targeted grazing to control invasive or undesirable species, or grazing new areas where physical or temporary electric fencing would be difficult. Additional factors to consider are if virtual fencing could open up new acreage to graze or help integrate livestock grazing on crop residue or cover crops on acres with poor physical fence. In some situations, conventional fencing or polywire may accomplish the same objective more economically. When considering adopting virtual fencing, the objective should drive the technology decision, not the other way around. Advertisement Comfort with computers and software Virtual fencing isn’t simply a fencing technology; it is also a digital management tool. Producers should consider their comfort level with computers, smartphones, mapping software and digital data before adopting a system. Most virtual fence platforms provide a software interface that allows producers to digitize their ranch infrastructure. This typically includes mapping: physical pasture boundaries, water sources, gates and working facilities, virtual paddocks and exclusion areas, and other important infrastructure. Precise mapping of physical infrastructure is essential to virtual fencing success so virtual fence boundaries don’t inadvertently exclude cattle from critical resources like watering locations. Most platforms are designed to be relatively intuitive, but successful implementation still requires a willingness to regularly interact with the software. Access to real-time animal locations can provide peace of mind for checking cattle but isn’t a replacement for physical checks on the herd. Producers should expect to check the system frequently rather than treating virtual fencing as a technology that can simply be programmed and forgotten. Livestock handling Successful deployment and collaring of animals requires access to adequate livestock-handling facilities. Virtual fence collars generally need to be installed while animals are restrained in a squeeze chute and head catch. Installing and properly adjusting a collar may take around 30 seconds per animal, but every animal must be individually restrained for collaring. Good working facilities, preferably with a reliable hydraulic squeeze chute, can make collar installation safer and more efficient for both livestock and the people handling them. Producers should also consider how frequently they normally work their livestock. Operations accustomed to handling cattle once or twice per year may need to increase handling frequency when using virtual fencing. Animals may occasionally need to be worked to adjust collar fit, especially with growing classes of animals such as yearlings. In addition, damaged collars, collars that fall off or collars that rely solely on battery power without solar panels may need to be replaced or re-installed to ensure the entire herd is collared. Though individuals that break or lose their collar will likely stay with the rest of the herd, to make the technology work most effectively, all animals should be collared to ensure compliance with the virtual boundaries. When considering implementing virtual fence systems, producers should consider both the quality of their livestock-handling facilities and their willingness to work animals more frequently than normal within their production system. Communication infrastructure Communication is another important consideration. Different virtual fence companies use several different communication technologies to transfer information between collars and the management software. Some systems use LoRa long-range radio communication. These systems typically require one or more base stations on the ranch. Collars communicate their location to the base station, which then transfers the information to the software platform. Likewise, new virtual fence boundaries are transmitted from the software through the base station to individual collars. Base stations can add infrastructure and cost to a virtual fencing system. Depending on topography and ranch size, multiple base stations may be necessary to provide adequate coverage. Placement is particularly important because hills, rough terrain and other obstacles can create communication dead zones. An important feature of virtual fence systems is that a collar can continue enforcing a previously downloaded virtual fence even if communication with the base station is temporarily lost. However, new instructions and real-time data generally cannot be received or transmitted until communication is restored. Other systems use direct-to-cellular communication, allowing individual collars to communicate through cellular networks without requiring base stations to communicate data and virtual fence boundaries. This can reduce the need for base stations but makes cellular coverage an important consideration for your operation. An easy test of cellular communication on your ranch is to drive to different areas of your property and see how many bars your cellphone can receive. Although 5G cellular networks are not required for virtual fence collars, at least one or two bars of cellular connection consistently throughout your property will likely be necessary for these devices to work as intended. More recently, some companies have integrated direct satellite communication, which can expand connectivity in remote areas where cellular service is limited, eliminating the need for base stations in locations without cell coverage. Regardless of communication method, producers should recognize that changes to virtual fence boundaries are not always instantaneous. Depending on connectivity, transmitting a new boundary or turning off an existing boundary may take anywhere from 30 minutes to several hours for all collars to receive the updated boundaries. This often requires advance planning to work or move cattle to ensure animals are not being pushed through virtual boundaries. For example, if an animal needs to be treated for an illness, producers may need to deactivate that boundary hours ahead of time before moving individuals or herds to working facilities. Think beyond rotational grazing Producers should also consider how many different applications they can find for virtual fencing throughout the year. Greater utilization of the technology can improve its potential return on investment, as the annual costs of the collars and data are typically fixed whether the collar is utilized four months or 12 months out of the year. For example, virtual fence might be used for spring cover crop grazing, rotational grazing during the summer, fall cover crop grazing, crop residue grazing or bale grazing during winter. Thinking about grazing and feed resources year-round will increase utility of the technology for your operation. It is worth considering if virtual fencing can also help with management pain points such as keeping cows in smaller virtual pastures during bull breeding to increase chances of natural service. Using virtual fencing across multiple seasons and management objectives allows producers to capture more value from their investment than using the technology for simply seasonal rotational grazing. Consider the full cost Finally, producers should evaluate the total cost of owning and operating a virtual fence system. Costs may include more than the collars themselves. Depending on the system, producers may need to consider base stations, communication infrastructure, installation, data plans, replacement equipment and annual collar fees. Pricing structures vary considerably among manufacturers. Some systems operate primarily under a subscription or leasing model, where producers pay an annual fee for the use of each collar. Other systems allow producers to purchase collars outright and then pay ongoing communication fees for transmitting data. When comparing systems, producers should compare the total cost over several years, rather than simply comparing the initial price of a collar. Recently, several states have allowed cost-sharing programs using contracts through the Natural Resources Conservation Service’s (NRCS) Environmental Quality Incentives Program (EQIP) that can help offset the initial cost. Producers looking to apply to these programs should consider how this technology can be used to stack multiple practices beyond prescribed grazing to make these applications most successful. Additional practices could include those mentioned above, such as targeted grazing for invasive species or managed grazing within stream systems to make these applications more successful. Virtual fencing can provide livestock producers with a powerful and flexible tool for managing grazing animals. However, successful adoption requires more than purchasing collars and placing them on cattle. Before investing, producers should evaluate their livestock handling facilities, communication infrastructure, comfort with digital technology, grazing objectives, potential year-round applications and total cost of ownership. Most importantly, virtual fencing should be viewed as a management tool rather than simply a replacement for physical fence. Operations that have clearly defined management objectives and can use the technology across multiple applications are likely to capture the greatest value from their investment.
