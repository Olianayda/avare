Mycoplasma bovis: The hidden challenge behind chronic respiratory disease Rogerio Eduardo de Souza Junior August 27, 2026 Mycoplasma bovis silently spreads through cattle herds, causing chronic respiratory disease, arthritis, mastitis and treatment failures. Prevention through biosecurity, stress reduction, diagnostics, vaccination support and veterinary guidance is essential to protect herd health. Respiratory disease remains one of the greatest health and economic challenges in cattle production. While viruses such as bovine viral diarrhea (BVD) and infectious bovine rhinotracheitis (IBR) often receive considerable attention, one bacterial pathogen continues to cause significant losses in both dairy and beef operations: Mycoplasma bovis. It commonly works alongside other respiratory pathogens, making disease outbreaks more severe, treatment less effective and recovery considerably slower. Although pneumonia is its best-known manifestation, the organism may also cause arthritis, mastitis and ear infections in calves. These conditions can increase veterinary costs, reduce production and lead to premature culling. Perhaps the greatest concern is that infected animals may appear perfectly healthy while silently spreading the organism throughout the herd. By the time obvious clinical signs appear, the infection may already be well established. For producers, understanding how Mycoplasma bovis behaves is the first step toward reducing disease pressure and protecting long-term herd performance. Advertisement Why Mycoplasma bovis is different Most bacterial respiratory infections respond well to early diagnosis and appropriate treatment. Mycoplasma bovis  presents a different challenge. Unlike most bacteria, it lacks a cell wall, making several commonly used antibiotics naturally ineffective. At the same time, antimicrobial resistance has increased worldwide, further reducing treatment success. Another important characteristic is its ability to establish chronic infections. Animals may initially improve after treatment but continue carrying and shedding the organism for weeks or even months. Some remain healthy-looking carriers, allowing the disease to spread unnoticed within the herd. Rather than causing dramatic outbreaks with high mortality, Mycoplasma bovis often produces gradual economic losses through repeated treatments, slower growth and premature culling. These hidden losses frequently have a greater economic impact than the treatment costs themselves. More than just pneumonia Although pneumonia is the most common clinical presentation, Mycoplasma bovis affects far more than the lungs. Following respiratory infection, the organism may spread to other tissues, causing chronic arthritis, swollen joints and lameness. Young calves commonly develop ear infections characterized by drooping ears, head tilt or loss of balance. When respiratory disease is accompanied by arthritis, ear infections or repeated treatment failures, Mycoplasma bovis  should be considered as a possible contributor. How infection spreads – and why stress matters One of the greatest challenges with Mycoplasma bovis is that it often enters a herd unnoticed. Apparently healthy animals can carry the organism and spread it through close contact and respiratory secretions without showing obvious clinical signs. Introducing replacement animals without knowing their health status remains one of the most common routes of introduction. Once inside the herd, the organism may circulate quietly until stressful events trigger clinical disease. Transportation, weaning, regrouping, overcrowding, poor ventilation and sudden weather changes all weaken the immune system, creating ideal conditions for respiratory outbreaks. Mycoplasma bovis rarely acts alone. It frequently works alongside viral pathogens such as BVD and IBR and bacterial pathogens including Mannheimia haemolytica , Pasteurella multocida and Histophilus somni . This combination often explains why some respiratory outbreaks become more severe and difficult to control than expected. For producers, reducing stress is not simply a management goal – it is an essential part of respiratory disease prevention. When treatment doesn't work One of the most frustrating situations on any farm is treating respiratory disease without seeing the expected improvement. Animals may recover slowly, relapse after treatment or continue coughing for weeks. Others may later develop arthritis or chronic lameness. When this pattern occurs, Mycoplasma bovis should be considered as a possible contributor. Because the organism lacks a cell wall, several commonly used antibiotics have little or no activity against it. Increasing antimicrobial resistance further complicates treatment, making laboratory diagnosis more valuable than repeatedly changing medications. PCR has become one of the most useful diagnostic tools because it allows rapid and accurate detection of the organism from appropriate clinical samples. Identifying the cause early helps veterinarians make better treatment decisions while avoiding unnecessary antimicrobial use. Successful control begins with an accurate diagnosis – not with additional treatments. Prevention starts before disease appears Because treatment outcomes are often inconsistent, prevention remains the most effective strategy for reducing the impact of Mycoplasma bovis . Strong prevention programs focus on both biosecurity and herd management. Key practices include:
Purchasing replacement animals from herds with known health status whenever possible
Quarantining newly introduced animals
Reducing stress during transportation, weaning and regrouping
Maintaining good ventilation and avoiding overcrowding
Feeding high-quality colostrum to support calf immunity
Monitoring young animals closely for early signs of respiratory disease
Although none of these measures alone will eliminate Mycoplasma bovis , together they significantly reduce disease pressure and improve overall herd health. Vaccination: One piece of the puzzle Interest in vaccines against Mycoplasma bovis has grown considerably over the past decade. However, developing highly effective vaccines has proven challenging because of the organism's ability to evade the immune system and establish persistent infections. For producers, the key message is clear: Vaccination should be viewed as one component of a comprehensive respiratory health program rather than a stand-alone solution. The most successful programs combine good biosecurity, sound management, timely diagnostics and appropriate vaccination strategies to reduce the overall impact of respiratory disease. Just as importantly, controlling the major viral and bacterial pathogens involved in the bovine respiratory disease (BRD) complex helps reduce the conditions that allow Mycoplasma bovis to become a significant problem. Working with your veterinarian No two cattle operations face exactly the same challenges. Herd size, housing, purchasing practices and animal movement all influence disease risk. For this reason, successful control programs should be tailored to each operation. When respiratory disease becomes persistent, treatment response is poor or arthritis begins appearing after pneumonia outbreaks, producers should work closely with their veterinarians to investigate whether Mycoplasma bovis is involved. Combining laboratory diagnostics with a review of management practices allows veterinarians to identify risk factors and develop practical strategies that reduce future outbreaks. The goal is not simply to treat sick animals but to improve long-term herd health and productivity. The bottom line Mycoplasma bovis remains one of the most challenging pathogens involved in bovine respiratory disease. Its ability to spread silently, establish chronic infections and respond inconsistently to treatment makes prevention far more valuable than reacting after disease occurs. Producers who combine strong biosecurity, effective management, early diagnosis and comprehensive respiratory disease prevention are better positioned to reduce treatment costs, improve animal welfare and protect herd performance. The tools to reduce the impact of Mycoplasma bovis already exist. The real question is this: Do you know whether Mycoplasma bovis is quietly circulating in your herd?
