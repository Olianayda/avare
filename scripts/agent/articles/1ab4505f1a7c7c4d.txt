Economics of slaughter plant closures and long-term market implications Andrew Anderson September 3, 2026 The recent history of packing plant closures in the U.S. has many producers wondering how it will affect their operations. Here’s what the economic history suggests. Related Industry grapples with plant closures nationwide Production slightly down on slow pace of slaughter Slower pace of slaughter partially offset by heavier weights Andrew Anderson Assistant Professor and Livestock Economics Extension Specialist / Utah State University Email Andrew Anderson Three plant closures since November 2025 have caused a sudden reduction in U.S. beef slaughter capacity. Together, recently closed plants had a total capacity of 9,500 head per day, or 47,500 per week ( Figure 1 ). Because operational capacity is lower than theoretical capacity, we can multiply this number by an assumed productivity factor of 0.95 to obtain an operational capacity loss of 45,125 head per week. I estimate the total industry operational slaughter capacity to be approximately 647,000 head per week before the closures. So the lost capacity comes to around 7% of current capacity – not an insignificant reduction. Why do packing plants shut down? Reports indicate that packers are losing as much as $200 to $400 per head as cattle prices remain elevated. One could reasonably wonder why packers would continue to compete so intensely for cattle they are losing money on. The answer from economics is that it remains rational to keep operating at a loss, so long as variable costs are covered and future profits are expected to outweigh current losses. Additionally, packers have relatively high fixed costs, so more throughput minimizes the losses by spreading those high fixed costs over more head. In the case of the plants in Lexington, Nebraska; Joslin, Illinois; and Souderton, Pennsylvania, the companies apparently do not believe future profits will make up for the losses they would absorb in the meantime. Capacity utilization and fed cattle prices Prices are always determined by supply and demand, but in the case of cattle, the demand side is derived from the use of cattle as an input into the production of beef. Capacity constraints in slaughter and processing can restrict the demand for cattle, even when there is strong consumer demand for beef. In practical terms, capacity limitations determine the strength of packers’ incentive to compete for cattle. When the cattle cycle reverses and the cattle herd eventually expands, fed cattle supplies will increase, leading to lower cattle prices. At that point, if cattle demand is limited by slaughter capacity constraints, prices will decline more abruptly to rising supplies. One way to gauge whether capacity constraints are impacting price is that the capacity utilization ratio is near 100% (e.g., the mid-90s). This ratio measures cattle slaughter in a given week or month relative to effective operational capacity (capacity utilization equals actual slaughter divided by operational capacity). This is a very useful number, but a difficult one to nail down because operating capacity itself is hard to measure. To estimate industry-wide operational kill capacity, I use a three-year rolling maximum of slaughter. (This statistic measures the capacity that the industry has demonstrated in can hit. There are several limitations to this approach, not least of which is the lagged nature and sluggish updating of capacity, which can abruptly change when plants close. Nonetheless, this measure does contain helpful information.) The capacity utilization ratio can tighten if the numerator increases (more cattle for slaughter) or if the denominator decreases (plant closures). When the ratio tightens, it increases downside price risk for producers. In Figure 2 , we see the 5-area weighted average steer price alongside an estimate of capacity utilization. The inverse relationship is clear, especially in recent years. Advertisement
How will the recent reduction of around 7% in industry slaughter capacity impact cattle prices? For the short run, there is likely still enough slaughter capacity to keep prices supported. With the plant closures, slaughter capacity utilization will likely increase from the lower 80s to the upper 80s, still short of levels that will drive prices down. But it is something to monitor over the coming years, especially if more plant closures occur.
Why should producers care about prices years in advance? Because herd rebuilding requires a capital investment in breeding stock with a productive life of eight years or more, so decisions made today depend on expectations of future returns. Plant closures add additional downside risk to herd rebuilding decisions.
