In 2026, strong balance sheets, healthy liquidity, improving productivity and favorable beef markets continue to support financial performance. Financial and production benchmarks provide valuable insight into dairy profitability and the management decisions that drive long-term success. While benchmark information typically lags three to six months behind current conditions, it remains one of the most useful tools producers have for evaluating performance, measuring progress and identifying opportunities for improvement. As producers begin planning for the coming year, year-end 2025 results and partial 2026 benchmark information offer a clear picture of the financial and production trends shaping today's dairy industry. Several key observations stand out. Profitability remains strong Full-year 2025 benchmark results show net income generally ranging from $400 to $1,000 per cow. While 2026 began with softer milk prices, margins improved by the end of the first quarter, and profitability appears to be tracking similarly to last year. These figures represent averages, and profitability continues to vary widely among farms. However, the overall takeaway is encouraging: Many dairy operations continue to generate solid margins despite changing market conditions. Financial position continues to strengthen Perhaps the most encouraging benchmark trend is the overall financial position of many dairy farms. Strong profits over the past four to five years, combined with increasing values for land, facilities and dairy cattle, have significantly strengthened balance sheets. Owners equity, adjusted to market values, now exceeds 60% of total assets on many farms. Traditionally, an equity position above 50% has been viewed as a strong financial benchmark. Advertisement Liquidity also remains healthy. Working capital averages well above $1,000 per cow, and many farms continue to maintain available operating credit. Even with higher interest rates, interest expense generally remains manageable at 5% to 7% of total revenue. Strong financial positions provide producers with greater flexibility as they evaluate future investments, navigate changing market conditions and prepare for the next production cycle. Productivity continues to improve Benchmark data also reflects continued gains in dairy productivity. Average milk production now exceeds 27,000 pounds per cow annually, while component production continues to climb. In many regions, combined butterfat and protein production exceeds 6.5 pounds per cow per day. Production levels naturally vary depending on facility design, milking frequency and breed type, but the long-term trend continues upward as producers adopt improved genetics, management practices and facility investments. Higher component production has also contributed to mailbox milk prices averaging $2 to $4 per hundredweight (cwt) above Class III milk prices, providing another boost to overall profitability. Beef revenue continues to support profitability One of the most significant contributors to dairy profitability over the past two years has been beef revenue. Crossbred beef calf sales, combined with historically strong cull cow prices, added an estimated $3 to $4 per cwt to farm revenue during 2025. That contribution has continued through the first half of 2026 and remains an important reason many farms are maintaining solid profitability despite softer milk prices. Higher cull values have also lowered replacement costs for operations maintaining relatively stable herd numbers. At the same time, lower heifer inventories have reduced raising costs as producers retain fewer replacements and extend the productive life of mature cows. Current benchmark data indicates heifer inventories now average less than 80% of mature cow inventories. While this trend has improved efficiency and lowered costs, producers should continue monitoring replacement inventories to ensure they maintain enough quality animals to replace less productive cows as needed. One of the strengths of benchmark information is that it helps producers look beyond individual numbers and better understand how management decisions influence overall profitability. The trends seen today provide valuable insight into where many successful dairy operations are focusing their attention. Feed costs continue to moderate Feed costs have continued to move lower after peaking at approximately $13 per cwt in 2023. Current benchmark data shows mature herd feed costs averaging $10 to $11 per cwt on a raw milk basis and $9 to $10 per cwt on an energy-corrected milk basis, excluding heifer feed costs. Lower commodity prices have played a significant role in reducing feed costs, but improved forage quality and continued gains in milk production per cow have also contributed. Together, these factors have helped improve overall operating margins for many dairy farms. Labor costs balanced through improved efficiency Although labor availability continues to be a challenge and wage rates have increased, benchmark data suggests many dairy farms have successfully balanced higher labor costs through gains in productivity and efficiency. Midwestern dairy farms currently average labor costs between $3.30 and $3.50 per cwt. Those figures generally include labor associated with heifer raising and crop production. Operations that utilize custom growers for heifers or custom farming services often report labor costs closer to $2.40 to $2.80 per cwt because those expenses are reflected elsewhere in the operation. The benchmark data suggests that while labor costs continue to rise, many producers are successfully managing those increases through improved efficiency. Capital costs continue to vary Capital costs remain one of the areas with the greatest variation among dairy farms. Depreciation based on actual asset use, interest expense and lease payments all contribute to capital cost. For many operations, those costs average between $2.40 and $2.80 per cwt. However, the averages tell only part of the story. Operations that have recently expanded often report capital costs approaching $4 per cwt because of today's higher construction, equipment and land costs. In contrast, farms operating with older facilities and lower debt levels may maintain capital costs below $2 per cwt. For farms making significant investments, the long-term goal should be working capital costs back toward the $3 per cwt range through productivity gains and continued debt reduction. Maintaining capital costs near $4 per cwt over an extended period can become more challenging during periods of lower milk prices. Other income continues to influence cost of production Overall cost of production has declined significantly during the past two years. Benchmark data indicates average cost of production now ranges from approximately $16 to $17 per cwt on a raw milk basis compared with more than $20 per cwt in 2022 and 2023. Lower feed costs have certainly contributed, but they are only part of the story. Strong beef revenue has become one of the largest factors reducing overall cost of production. Additional income from farming operations, government programs and digesters has also helped offset production costs. While these additional revenue streams strengthen profitability, they can also mask increases in expense categories such as repairs, energy, utilities and insurance. Looking beyond total cost of production helps producers better understand where expenses continue to increase and where additional efficiencies may still be possible. Putting benchmarks to work Benchmark information is most valuable when it becomes part of the management process rather than simply another report. Lenders, consultants and accountants can all help producers interpret benchmark data and compare results with similar operations. The goal isn't to lead every category. Every dairy operation has different resources, goals and management priorities. Instead, benchmark information should help identify where an operation is performing well and where opportunities exist to improve. Those opportunities become the foundation for future planning, management decisions and long-term profitability. Used consistently over time, benchmark information provides producers with one of the clearest ways to measure progress, evaluate investments and position their operations for continued success.
