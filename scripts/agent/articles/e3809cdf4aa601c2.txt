Accurately estimating ruminal starch digestibility is an important aspect of dairy ration formulation. In the Cornell Net Carbohydrate and Protein System (CNCPS), the rate of starch digestibility ( k d ) determines how much starch is available to rumen microbes and ultimately drives both metabolizable protein (MP) and metabolizable energy (ME). In fact, in CNCPS, MP is the most sensitive to variable starch k d numbers as increased degradable starch drives microbial protein production and vice versa. Impact of starch digestibility rates on nutrition model predictions Spilling the beans: High-oleic soybeans Dairy nutritionists rely on 7-hour in vitro starch digestibility (dStarch7). Researchers in 2009 developed an equation, which estimates starch k d based on dStarch7. Most commercial labs estimate dStarch7 by NIR equations for most corn products, as NIR is faster and more cost-effective compared to performing chemistry analysis. Prior to NIR readings, samples are typically ground through a 1-millimeter screen using an abrasion mill (Retsch); additionally, the NIR calibration equations are based on in vitro analyses conducted on corn ground through a 4-mm screen (Wiley mill). Although these procedures allow for more repeatable NIR readings, it alleviates variation attributable to processing and particle size. Instead, dStarch7 could only reflect inherent corn characteristics, such as hybrid, vitreousness and growing conditions. Determining the influence of particle size on dStarch7 is more important for corn in comparison to most other starch sources. Corn kernels are protected by a resistant outer pericarp. Additionally, corn starch is embedded in a prolamin (zein) matrix that is hydrophobic. Grinding corn disrupts the pericarp and prolamin matrix surrounding starch granules, improving microbial access to the starch. As a result, particle size plays a central role in determining starch k d in dry corn.  To evaluate the impact of particle size on dStarch7, whole dry corn samples from the Northeast and Midwest were collected. Corn was mixed and pooled. The pooled sample was sent to Cumberland Valley Analytical Services (CVAS) and processed through two mills with five different screens in total to create five ground corn samples with various particle sizes. Mill 1 (Wiley) processed corn through a 1-, 4-, and 6-millimeter screen. Mill 2 (Retsch) processed corn through a 0.5- and 2-millimeter screen. Geometric mean particle size (ANSCI/ASAE S319.4) was determined to be 150, 251, 194, 739 and 1,094 µM for the 0.5-, 1-, 2-, 4- and 6-millimeter grind sizes, respectively. The five ground samples were analyzed by CVAS for in vitro starch digestibility at 4, 7, 12, 16 and 24 hours. Samples were analyzed as-is (not further processed). Measured starch digestibility from the five time points were used to derive a starch k d value for each sample. In Figure 1 , the measured dStarch7 from the in vitro analysis is on the x-axis and NIR estimated dStarch7 is on the y-axis. The dashed orange line indicates a hypothetical perfect fit between in vitro measured and NIR. It is important to highlight that the samples were all sourced from the same corn, the only difference is particle size. As expected, there is little variability in NIR dStarch7 (range 53.7-57.1 % Starch); on the other hand, in vitro dStarch7 indicates increasing values as corn particle size gets finer (1,094-150 micrometer [µM] ranges from 62.8-27.7 % Starch). Furthermore, in vitro dStarch7 averaged 44.3%, approximately 80% of averaged NIR dStarch7 (55.3%). Advertisement How much does adjusting starch k d of ground corn affect predicted milk? In CNCPS, a Midwestern diet at a DMI of 59 pounds per day and including 12 pounds of dry ground corn was evaluated across different particle sizes. Starch k d  (described above) and starch intestinal digestibility were updated according to particle size. It is generally accepted that if ruminal starch digestibility increases, post-ruminal starch digestibility also improves. Going from our estimate of starch k d  of 700 µM   (starch k d  of 6.8% per hour and starch intestinal digestibility of 81.4% escape) dry corn to 200 µM   (starch k d  of 10.7% per hour and starch intestinal digestibility of 92.1% escape), dry corn, ME and MP predicted milk increased by about 2.6 and 4.8 pounds, respectively. This is a substantial change. These results highlight the importance of fine corn grinding, as smaller particle size increases starch fermentability and drives a greater response in MP than ME, reflecting the strong role of fermentable starch in supporting microbial protein synthesis. How do starch k d   book values of ground corn in CNCPS compare to what we measured in this study? CNCPS starch k d   values for dry corn appear to be approximately 1.5x that of estimates generated from in vitro measurements ( Figure 2 ), suggesting that model defaults may overestimate actual starch fermentation rates. Conclusions NIR dStarch7 should be interpreted carefully. It remains a useful indicator of intrinsic starch digestibility potential and likely reflects factors such as corn genetics, kernel hardness and growing environment. However, it is not a reliable indicator of the effects of processing or particle size. In other words, dStarch7 reflects what the corn could do, not necessarily what it is doing in the cow. Book values in CNCPS appear to be overestimated. To better align ration formulation with on-farm reality, nutritionists should routinely measure corn particle size and adjust starch k d in models such as CNCPS.
