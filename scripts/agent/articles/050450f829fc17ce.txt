Image courtesy of Axiota Animal Health. Is it BRD or acidosis in the feedlot? Mark Corrigan September 9, 2026 BRD and ruminal acidosis are both capable of triggering inflammation and can worsen each other’s effects and contribute to similar clinical signs, making a collaborative approach between veterinarians and nutritionists essential for improving cattle health outcomes. When a health outbreak hits a feedlot pen, the question of cause is rarely simple. Bovine respiratory disease (BRD) and acidosis have more in common than most people realize – and managing one without the other may mean leaving the problem only partially solved. He takes the calf to the chute, checks its temperature and calls it BRD. It's a reasonable call, but it might only address part of the issue. The outcomes and clinical signs of BRD and ruminal acidosis overlap to a remarkable degree. Both conditions can cause cattle to go off feed and become depressed or lethargic; in some cases, acidosis can also cause labored breathing and nasal discharge. Neither condition is especially easy to differentiate at the chute with the diagnostics typically available. Researchers have noted in the scientific literature that acidosis is frequently misdiagnosed as BRD. This is not a failure of judgment so much as a reflection of how closely these two conditions can mimic each other. A study published in The Bovine Practitioner illustrated this connection. In that study, high-risk calves that received an oral dose of Megasphaera elsdenii , a bacterium that helps stabilize rumen pH by consuming lactic acid, at processing had 31% lower BRD morbidity, better feed intake, faster gains and lower treatment costs than untreated calves. The authors indicated they could not determine whether fewer calves had true BRD or whether a portion of the control calves treated for BRD were suffering from subclinical acidosis. Acidosis and inflammation When rumen pH drops sharply, as it does during acidosis, gram-negative bacteria in the rumen die off and release their cell wall components containing endotoxin into the rumen fluid. Other inflammatory compounds are also produced in the rumen during acidotic fermentation, including histamine and various biogenic amines. Advertisement Endotoxin released during acidosis can trigger a systemic inflammatory response, leading to the release of chemical messengers that activate destructive immune cells. Research published in peer-reviewed journals has documented elevated blood endotoxin levels in cattle with experimentally induced acidosis, as well as increases in prostaglandins and other inflammatory molecules in the joints of acidotic cattle. Acidosis, in other words, is not just a rumen problem. It also elicits a whole-body inflammatory event, and that fact matters enormously when trying to understand a health outbreak. Early in my career, I was involved in a study in which heifers were challenged intravenously with E. coli endotoxin to study the immune response. What we observed in that experiment that we did not anticipate was that all the heifers developed ruminal bloat following the injection of the endotoxin. The endotoxin went into the bloodstream and the rumen responded. Research has confirmed that rumen epithelial cells express TLR4, the primary receptor through which the body detects and responds to endotoxin. This means the rumen wall is not a passive bystander during an inflammatory event; it is an active participant. Studies have also shown that intravenous endotoxin infusion decreases rumen motility and disrupts fermentation in dairy cattle. One plausible interpretation is that the rumen has a built-in mechanism to slow its own activity when endotoxin is present. This feedback loop that may help reduce further acid production during an acidosis event by slowing down mixing in the rumen and thereby slowing down fermentation. This also means that endotoxin produced during a respiratory infection could affect rumen function. A calf in the middle of an acidosis event is already carrying a significant inflammatory burden due to elevated endotoxin in the rumen. When that same calf encounters a respiratory pathogen, whether it is a virus or a bacterium, the body's response is amplified. The inflammatory response is already primed, and a respiratory pathogen will further amplify it. Alternatively, a calf fighting a respiratory infection will stop eating, which disrupts the rumen microbial community. When appetite returns, the rumen may be poorly prepared for the starch load, thus setting the stage for acidosis. Endotoxin from that acidosis event adds to the inflammation already present from the respiratory disease, potentially worsening disease severity, prolonging recovery and, in the worst cases, contributing to death. A problem that requires both sides of the conversation Consider what a typical high-risk calf faces during the receiving period. It arrives at the feedlot already immune-compromised from the combined stress of weaning, mixing with unfamiliar cattle and transportation. Because of that stress – and because the feedbunk is unfamiliar – intake is often inconsistent, which delays rumen microbial stability. At the same time, the calf’s immune system is being challenged by pathogen exposure in a new environment, commingling, and the antigens and endotoxin associated with vaccination. Viewed together, these pressures help explain why BRD and acidosis are often closely linked. Managing BRD and acidosis effectively requires both the veterinarian and the nutritionist to be involved. From the nutritional side, a well-designed receiving program should give the rumen time to adapt to concentrate-based diets while still supplying enough energy and protein to support immune function. From the health side, appropriate vaccination, pathogen surveillance, metaphylaxis and clear pull protocols remain essential. The point is not to determine whether BRD or acidosis is the sole culprit. In most feedlot health outbreaks, some degree of both is likely involved, and each can make the other harder to manage. Stress, pathogen exposure and inconsistent intake are realities of most cattle marketing and finishing systems. When the veterinarian, the nutritionist and the producer recognize that reality, approach the challenge together and share an understanding of the biology connecting rumen health to respiratory health, everyone is better positioned to improve the outcome.
