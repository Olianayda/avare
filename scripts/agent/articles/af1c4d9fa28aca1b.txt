The real value of farm and ranch recordkeeping Rebecca Mills September 7, 2026 Strong farm and ranch records do more than track expenses – they help producers reduce taxes, control costs, make informed decisions and strengthen financial opportunities. Calving book columns that have real value Every December, Mike Fry visits the University of Idaho Extension office in Emmett, Idaho, to do some Christmas shopping. His purchase is a deeply rooted tradition that his daughter Sarah looks forward to in her stocking: an Integrated Resource Management Redbook. Sarah Fry Allred is the sixth generation to contribute to her family's farming and ranching operation in Boise County. “I went off to college, and when I came back [in 2015], I started to buy replacement heifers from my parents … as time goes on, they’re culling out their cows and I will phase in,” she explains. With those heifers comes years of data carefully collected in redbooks and other meticulously recorded notebooks. Joe Welker has a different memory of recordkeeping on his family’s dairy cattle and farming operation. Now an associate professor of accounting at the College of Western Idaho and owner of J.A. Welker & Associates PLLC, Welker describes his dad’s method. “He had a 1984 Ford extended cab, and in the back was a gunnysack. In the gunnysack were lunch sacks with different titles on them.” Every expense had its place in the gunnysack and later stacked in piles on the kitchen table. Skye Root recounts his dad’s ability to track data on their family hay farm. He’s capturing information through “what I would call a litany of Excel spreadsheets.” As a professional farm manager, farmland real estate adviser and founder of Root Agricultural Advisory, Root has seen a wide array of record-keeping styles. He is often amazed how neighboring farms can have drastically different results in financial success. The difference? Records. Advertisement Lessons learned Allred will be the first to say she feels incredibly fortunate to have inherited a solid system. Personally, she attributes her ability to keep good records to growing up in the 4-H program. “I learned if I track things throughout the year, it’s not a big headache later.” In addition to her redbook, she keeps a calendar on the wall to record the daily details she may not remember later. Welker offers sound advice to keep people away from his dad’s old system. The first thing he wants producers to know is, “Your memory is not an accounting system.” He continues, “A good record is a separated record. We need financial separation of business and personal accounts.” From there, he encourages “using dedicated cards, checkbooks [and] accounts to account for business versus personal” expenses. The reality, Welker says, is producers will “overpay on their income tax because they are failing to track.” He gives the example of someone paying $90 for supplies in the spring but not writing it down thinking they’ll just remember. “When tax season rolls around seven months later, that 90 dollars, if not organized correctly, would cost them 30 dollars in taxes. All those nickels and dimes add up to something substantial if they are not keeping their records appropriately.” The failure to track can lead to missed opportunities, too. Root recalls farmers who were “able to expand their operations because they were above-average recordkeepers.” In addition, when it comes to selling farmland, there is real money to be made if records are descriptive and complete. Farmland with production history, water data and soil tests can command a premium. In a very real sense, it all comes down to controlling costs. Root shares that at the end of the day, “Revenue minus cost equals net income. If people don’t know their costs, it’s really hard to figure out what to change, and you become reactionary in moments where you don’t have time to be reactionary.” Records as a decision-making tool Decision-making doesn’t have to be reactionary, though. Allred’s dad has a favorite saying: “Every ranch could be a success if we all had a crystal ball.” Knowing there’s a fair share of chance-taking in the cattle market, she’s learned to rely on the data to inform her decisions. “I can have all this exceptional information about a cow and what she’s expected to do, but sometimes their performance just doesn’t line up with what they look like on paper.” It’s then that she has choices to make. “If I have data or records in front of me, I feel like I can make an educated decision; if it’s the right decision or not is hard to tell,” but that information brings confidence. This is true for the farming side of their operation, too. With rising electricity costs to pump irrigation water from the Payette River, they decide if “it would make more sense to irrigate twice or irrigate three times.” Or if it would it be more cost-effective to get one cutting and then buy hay. Weighing costs while keeping enough funds in the bank to cover expenses is top of mind. For Welker, having confidence brings peace of mind. He says, “The IRS doesn’t care what happens; they care what you can prove.” He encourages farms and ranches to structure their businesses in such a way that they can plan ahead to spend their annual revenues and take advantage of tax breaks and depreciation schedules built into tax code for agriculture. Realized benefits As a beginning producer carrying on a family legacy, Allred feels the gravity of being the next generation. She admits, “I put pressure on myself to be successful. It’s [also] been made very clear to me from everyone that they want to see me succeed, so they’re doing everything they can to help build me up and get there.” Welker sees it in his clients and his students – from the former student who got a job keeping books for a large dairy in southern Idaho to the young business person not losing money on his taxes because of the record-keeping system he’s established – record-keeping skills pay dividends. Root says, “He who has good records and a clean equipment yard wins.” Those are the farms and ranches that get preferential treatment with banks and lenders, command premium land prices and find greatest financial success overall.
