On Aug. 26, Trump signed a proclamation titled “Further Ensuring Affordable Beef for the American Consumer,” which is slated to bring in 300,000 metric tons of “product for ground beef to be imported with no out-of-quota tariff” and that it would be sold “at 25 percent below current market prices.” Trump gave no initial indication which countries would provide that beef but revealed on Sept. 5 that the meat would be “coming from Argentina. It’s coming from Brazil,” as well as a “couple of other places.” Trump touted the move as a way to make beef more affordable for consumers and ultimately help cattle producers rebuild the national cow herd from record-low numbers. Almost immediately, several cattle producer groups and beef industry leaders loudly and publicly panned the move. In a statement released shortly after the announcement, the National Cattlemen’s Beef Association (NCBA) said, “For generations, cattle producers have invested in building consumer confidence in American beef and creating the gold standard of food safety systems. Putting that trust at risk in pursuit of a short-term political solution would be a serious mistake. Beef sold to American consumers should continue to meet rigorous, science-based food safety and inspection standards, regardless of the size of the processor. … Constant government interference creates uncertainty for producers making long-term decisions about their businesses and the future of the cattle herd.” After a meeting with Trump on Sept. 4, American Farm Bureau Federation President Zippy Duvall said he had told the president “that the plan to increase beef imports is hurting farm families. I shared with him that we have witnessed significant price drops for cattle across the country. It has caused a setback to rebuilding the U.S. herd. We’ll continue to urge the administration to reverse the decision to increase imports before cattle prices fall further.” The administration followed the bump in tariff-free beef imports with a wave of measures directly affecting the U.S. beef industry. On Sept. 4, Trump signed two executive orders directing federal agencies to actively seek solutions to multiple longstanding beef producer concerns. Among other things, the orders: Advertisement
Direct USDA to review existing authorities that could support mandatory country-of-origin labeling (MCOOL) for beef products, which was repealed by Congress in 2015. Since only Congress can reinstate MCOOL, the order simply directs the agency to review the practice’s feasibility.
Direct USDA to speed up outreach and participation in state-federal meat inspection programs. Trump’s stated goal is to provide farmers and ranchers with more opportunities to process their own meat, which he views as vitally important to an industry largely dependent on the “Big Four” meat-packing companies.
Directs the secretary of the interior to definitively determine whether gray wolves and Mexican wolves met the recovery criteria for delisting under the Endangered Species Act, and immediately begin the process of delisting or downlisting, which would provide state governments and livestock producers more tools to combat wolf predation.
The executive orders were met with mixed reviews from beef industry organizations and lawmakers from major ranching states. At the same time, two separate bills were introduced in the U.S. House of Representatives – by Reps. Gabe Vasquez (D-New Mexico) and Shomari Figures (D–Alabama), respectively – aimed at halting the tariff-free beef imports.
