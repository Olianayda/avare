Home &raquo; Progressive Cattle &raquo; Southeast: Don't let one bad winter undo a good grazing year Cattle Grazing Southeast: Don't let one bad winter undo a good grazing year Katie Mason September 22, 2026 After spending all spring and summer managing pastures, it can be tempting to push the thought of pasture management aside during the winter. However, how pastures and cattle are managed when temperatures drop can have a major impact on pasture productivity the following spring. Katie Mason Extension Beef Cattle Specialist / University of Tennessee Institute of Agriculture Email Katie Mason Related Winter feeding and management: Don’t let the cold freeze up your dairy’s progress this winter Don’t let your herbicide program ruin your grazing plans Don't let your pasture kill your profits: Grazing and feeding hazards One of the biggest concerns during winter is overgrazing. Cool-season grasses may continue growing during mild weather, but growth slows considerably as temperatures drop. When cattle repeatedly graze plants too closely, the plant is unable to recover as quickly or store energy reserves in the root. Maintaining adequate residual forage is especially important heading into late winter, when plants are preparing for spring growth. Cattle naturally spend more time around hay feeding areas, water sources, gates and other high-traffic locations, concentrating manure and hoof traffic in relatively small areas. Weather can compound the situation further; wet winter conditions can lead to soil compaction, pugging and damage to forage plants. Some areas may recover quickly, while others can remain damaged well into the following growing season. This is where a sacrifice paddock can become one of the most useful tools in a winter grazing plan. A sacrifice paddock is an area intentionally designated for heavy use during periods when grazing conditions are poor. The goal is not to preserve every acre through the winter. Rather, it is to concentrate unavoidable damage in one manageable location rather than allowing cattle to damage the entire farm. A sacrifice area can be an existing low-productivity paddock, a heavy-use area or another location that can be repaired or renovated more easily than an entire pasture. Ideally, it should have good access to water and be located where feeding equipment can easily reach it. The area can be used when the ground is too wet to support normal pasture traffic or when available forage has been depleted. Hay can be fed there until conditions improve, and the area can then be renovated or allowed to recover. Advertisement The key is to use a sacrifice paddock intentionally. It should not become a permanent feeding area simply because it is convenient. Feeding cattle in the same location all winter can create excessive mud, nutrient accumulation and soil compaction. Rotating feeding locations within the sacrifice area, using heavy-use pads where appropriate and moving cattle back to pasture when conditions allow can help minimize long-term damage. The goal of winter grazing is not simply to make it to spring without running out of forage. It is to arrive at spring with productive pastures ready to respond to favorable growing conditions.
