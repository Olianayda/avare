Getty Images. Improving human-animal relationship in cattle production Maria Camila Ceballos August 24, 2026 Cattle-handling practices are a key factor in animal welfare and productivity. When cattle are handled calmly and confidently, life runs more smoothly for both handlers and livestock. In cattle production systems, the human-animal relationship is a critical, yet often underestimated, component of animal welfare and productivity. Producers have an essential role in recognizing and modulating the impacts of human behaviour on cattle welfare and productivity. A practical perspective on cattle production The human-animal relationship is built through repeated interactions. In livestock operations, these interactions include various sensory stimuli, including visual, auditory, tactile and olfactory. These interactions can be positive, neutral or negative, and animals quickly learn to associate human presence with these experiences. Many routine management practices, e.g., vaccinations, dehorning, castration and transport, are needed to keep animals healthy and ensure production systems’ efficiency. However, from the animal’s perspective, these procedures can be perceived as aversive, especially when combined with negative interactions during handling, such as rushing, shouting or the use of electric prods. As a result, cattle may come to associate human presence with discomfort or fear, increasing their stress and compromising future handling. Fear of humans is not just a welfare concern; negative interactions of humans with cattle reduce reproductive performance, milk yield and growth rate. In beef cattle, increased fear responses reduced pregnancy rates and altered stress physiology. However, this fear is not permanent; through habituation and positive reinforcement, cattle can learn to tolerate or even seek out human contact, especially when interactions are calm, consistent and respectful. The behaviour of stockpeople is shaped by their attitudes, knowledge and working conditions. Importantly, most negative behaviours toward animals are not intentional but due to a limited understanding of animals’ perception of those interactions. Cattle handlers may not recognize the impacts of their actions, especially if they perceive them as routine or harmless. This highlights the importance of training that addresses both technical skills and attitudes toward animals. Attitudes are predictors of human behaviour. According to the theory of cognitive dissonance, once a person engages in a particular behaviour, they are likely to adjust their attitudes to align with that behaviour. Therefore, encouraging positive handling practices can promote more positive attitudes toward animals. In studies we conducted in Brazil, trained stockpeople not only had more positive behaviours and fewer negative interactions with cattle but also maintained better handling quality throughout the workday compared to untrained individuals. In contrast, on farms without training, handling deteriorated over time, with increased use of aversive techniques and a rise in undesirable animal behaviours and accidents. Advertisement Recent research from our lab at the University of Calgary Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, conducted in collaboration with cow-calf producers across western Canada, has reinforced these outcomes. These studies included both qualitative interviews and a regional survey of more than 100 cow-calf handlers. Producers consistently emphasized the importance of low-stress handling and recognized the direct link between handling practices, animal welfare and productivity. They expressed strong motivation to adopt low-stress handling techniques and viewed cattle-handling training as a valuable learning experience with positive effects on both animal outcomes and operational efficiency. The study collected information about four training modalities:
In-person training with hands-on demonstrations
In-person training without demonstrations
Hybrid formats combining online modules with practical sessions
Fully online training
Little is known about the content, duration or quality of delivery of these training sessions, which may explain why the study did not detect consistent differences in handlers’ attitudes across all training types. These results emphasized the importance of not only offering training but ensuring it is well designed, context-specific and focused on both technical skills and behavioural change. Despite recognizing the benefits of training, producers identified barriers to participation, including limited access to local opportunities, time constraints and cost. They expressed a strong preference for practical, farm-based training that reflects real-world conditions and emphasizes the need to focus on patience, calmness and understanding of cattle behaviour. We plan to conduct research to explore how training modalities affect stockperson attitudes, empathy and handling behaviours, as well as cattle responses and their effect on productivity. By offering the same training content through various formats (online, in-person with or without hands-on components), we aim to evaluate which approaches are most effective in fostering positive human-animal interactions and improving welfare and productive outcomes in beef cattle production systems. Producers are well positioned to influence these dynamics. By recognizing the role of human behaviour in animal welfare, participating in evidence-based training and adopting low-stress handling practices, they can foster a culture of empathy and respect. Seeking guidance from trusted advisers, including veterinarians and animal welfare specialists, may further support the implementation of these practices on-farm. These efforts not only enhance animal welfare and productivity but also contribute to safer, more sustainable cattle production systems across western Canada.
