Home &raquo; Idaho Ag Proud &raquo; The Good Dirt: Victory lap Idaho Ag Proud The Good Dirt: Victory lap Chloe Nichols September 15, 2026 Derek has a crick in his neck from scanning the sky the last four days, trying to outguess the clouds coming over the mountains. I’m contemplating calling The Weather Channel and AccuWeather to request monetary benefits from hourly refreshing their apps. If their monthly reports show an uptick, it is definitely due to us and our desire to get several hundred acres of alfalfa hay on our north farm in a bale without the windrows being rain-soaked. Related The Good Dirt: Camping with cows The Good Dirt: Big burn The Good Dirt: Is It a weed? To combat the strain of a gray sky and a forecast that refuses to grant any favors, we drive into Mackay. Slipping into our usual booth at 9 Peaks, we order breakfast. Last night was a late one, and we yawn as we wait for fried eggs and toasted English muffins with homemade strawberry jam. Derek was up past midnight moving bales with the telehandler so the baling crew could finish what was raked. “Was” being the key word. A rogue wind that afternoon picked up the windrows and threw them sideways, wrapping them around bales already sitting in the field. The crew managed to bale one small pivot and sneak out the overhang on another before the rain shut them down. When Amber sets our breakfast in front of us, she asks how the farming is going. “It’s a little stressful,” Derek says. “I know we need rain, just not on our hay.” Advertisement “My husband, Will, says he doesn’t envy you farmers. He worked for Valley Ag, you know, and saw all the effort it takes to get a crop. It’s intense.” “It is,” Derek says, “but there are the good days. Like in spring when everything is greened up and growing good. I drive around my fields and the feeling of satisfaction – there’s nothing like it. When the wheat comes up beautiful, and you get a nearly perfect stand, and things are just hummin’ along. Checking crops on those days, it’s like a victory lap.” “So what? Three days?” Amber asks, shaking her head. “I’m guessing you get about three days out of the year when it’s that good.” “Yep, but those are three very, very good days,” Derek says. I order a cappuccino and look out the window, hoping for sunshine. I ruminate on weather patterns and how farmers inevitably inherit a tendency to joke about what they can’t control. “I noticed the kochia weeds around the edges of my field are gettin’ dry,” Shawn says. “They could use the moisture. Didn’t I tell you about the monsoon?” We apparently weren’t listening, too occupied with watching the sky or scrutinizing the latest weather prediction. Or maybe we were running in tight, little circles at the moment. It’s our only version of a sun dance. Finishing breakfast, we head for the farm to check the hay. Darkish clouds are looming over Sheep Mountain and Big Flat Top to the southwest. They’ll be on us about four o’clock this afternoon, and the chance of rain again is a strong 55%. After kicking the decision around with the raking and baling crew, we decide to sit it out for today. It’s a hard choice; the regrowth is already poking through the windrows. As we move on to the next task, gates for a new fence around the barn, I remember a small instance from several days ago. Justin drove into the stackyard with his truck for a load of feeder hay to haul to his ranch on the East Fork of the Salmon. He apologized for not showing up earlier. “I blew out two tires on my equipment trailer. Sat for a day-and-a-half beside the highway trying to get it fixed. Everything that could go wrong did.” Derek commiserated and loaded the hay while Justin strapped it down. As Justin climbed back in the semi for the steady pull over Willow Creek Pass, I heard him yell to Derek above the noise of the engine, “The great thing about being in the cellar is there’s no place to go but up!” By late afternoon, the storm clouds are closing in. The rain is fitful, but it’s undeniably here. Derek leaves to check on the progress of wheat harvest. There had been a short window of sunshine around noon and the combine started rolling. I consider taking a walk. The fresh smell of a mountain valley rain is hard to beat. My phone rings. Derek tells me they finished the field they were working on. “Mike baled the straw right behind the combine; we might even get some of it stacked. And I’m going to take a load of wheat to Blackfoot yet this evening.” The rain moves on; the day winds down. I take my walk. We shut off the wells on our south farms where the rain bumped the soil moisture. It will help carry us to the end of the season. I open the weather apps, and a cheerful sun icon pops up for tomorrow’s forecast. I feel a victory lap comin’ on.
