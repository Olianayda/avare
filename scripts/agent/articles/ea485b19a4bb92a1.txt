One of the biggest misconceptions I encounter is that estate planning is primarily about what happens when you're gone. Questions to ask to uncover inheritance expectations I disagree. Estate planning is really about creating clarity while you're alive. Clarity that surviving spouses will be financially secure and taken care of. Clarity around future ownership and decision-making authority. Clarity for employees, successors, family members, lenders and business partners about what happens if the unexpected occurs.   And clarity that the business has a realistic opportunity to remain viable and sustainable through a transition. Estate planning isn't just about what happens when you're gone. It’s about creating confidence that the family and the business can continue moving forward. That's why I frequently encourage families to work with their lawyers to write what I call an “emergency will.” I often find people are paralyzed to put pen to paper because they're trying to write a will that will be read when they're 99 years old. They're trying to build a flexible enough plan that can account for an endless list of future “what if” scenarios. Advertisement My definition of an emergency will is a will you're comfortable with for the next one to five years, depending on how much change is happening within your family and business. It isn't meant to be your forever stagnant estate plan. It is meant to reflect today's realities while recognizing that life, families, businesses and tax rules all change over time. During this “limbo” stage, no one expects the unexpected to happen – but if it does, your family and business have a plan in place. Some families may need to be reviewing their plan annually because of the pace of change, while others may not need to make updates as frequently. Marriages, divorces, births, deaths, grandchildren, new assets, business growth, successors taking on greater ownership or management responsibilities, changes in tax legislation or shifts in your family's goals can all be reasons to revisit and amend your will. The goal is simple: Ensure your estate plan continues to reflect your family's current reality – not the reality of five or 10 years ago. Ironically, a will is a living document – not something written once and shoved in a drawer. As ownership, operations and family dynamics change, the plan must evolve and documents need to be updated. 1. Does every asset need the same succession plan? Most dairy families own several different asset buckets:
Corporate share equity such as quota, land, equipment, cattle and inventory
Personally owned farmland
Buildings
Investments
Cash and personal assets
A question worth asking: Should every one of those assets follow the same succession path? For example:
Should all or a portion of farmland remain with the surviving spouse?
Should all or a portion of corporate ownership begin transitioning to the farming successor?
Should investment assets follow a completely different path?
The answer depends entirely on the family's goals. The key takeaway is that estate planning does not need to be all-or-nothing.  Different assets can transfer to different people at different times if that better supports both family and business objectives. 2. Is ‘I leave everything to my spouse’ still the right answer? One of the most common clauses I see in wills is: “I leave everything to my spouse.” For many farm families, that clause made perfect sense when the children were young, the business transition had not yet begun and/or both parents were actively involved in operating the farm. As parents continue to age, grief, changing health, cognitive decline or simply a desire to step away from the business may influence their ability – or willingness – to assume all of the ownership, liability exposure, risk and major decision-making authority associated with the dairy operation. I often ask dairy families: At this stage of your life, is that still the strategy that best supports your family, your dairy business and your long-term goals?
If one spouse passes away unexpectedly, should 100% of the deceased parent’s ownership automatically transfer to the surviving spouse?
Would that model actually strengthen the long-term success of the business?
Or has the family’s transition journey reached a point where a different strategy such as 50%, 100% of their shares transferring to the successor better support both the business and the next generation?
The goal is not to replace the phrase “I leave everything to my spouse.” The goal is to determine whether that clause still aligns with your family’s values, business realities and long-term vision. Once you've answered that question, the next planning discussion becomes even more important: What happens if a successor passes away prematurely? 3. Why is it critical to ‘Follow the asset’?  One exercise I recommend to almost every farm family is “Follow the asset.” For example, if your successor passes away unexpectedly, with a corporation, where do their shares go? Seems like a straightforward question. Often, it isn't.
Do the shares transfer to a spouse?
Back to the parents?
To a surviving farming sibling?
To a surviving non-farming family member?
To minor children?
To a third party?
Would a potential surviving farming sibling be comfortable operating the dairy alongside an in-law shareholder? If shares eventually end up with grandchildren, who manages them until they become adults? Too many succession plans stop at the first transfer and never follow ownership down the road. The goal isn't to predict every possible outcome. The goal is to follow ownership far enough to determine whether the shares ultimately end up where the family hopes they will. Homework: Follow the arrow One of the simplest exercises I encourage dairy families to complete is what I call the “Follow the arrow” exercise. Take a piece of paper and write down each major asset such as:
Land
Quota
Corporate shares
Investments
Then begin drawing arrows. Let’s follow the shares, for example. If Dad passes away first, where does his share ownership go?
Does it go to Mom?
If Mom passes away next, where does it go?
If the farming successor passes away, where does it go?
If their spouse later remarries, where does it go?
If the successor's children ultimately inherit it, who manages the business?
Keep following the arrows. The exercise isn't designed to predict the future. It’s designed to identify whether the assets ultimately end up where the family intends them to end up. Many families are surprised by what they discover. Often, the biggest estate-planning gaps are uncovered by simply following ownership one more step and seeing the lack of continuity between generations for the sustainability of the business to continue.  4. Is continuity planning just for the senior generation? As families work through their estate planning, I encourage them to remember that continuity planning doesn't stop with the senior generation. A common question I ask families is:   If your children inherit land, shares or quota tomorrow and then pass away unexpectedly, where does that equity go? If you don't know the answer, it may be time to review not only Mom and Dad's estate plan, but the junior generation's intentions as well.
Does the equity go to a spouse?
To their children?
To a sibling?
Somewhere else?
The reality is that effective continuity planning requires everyone involved in the ownership structure to have current, cohesive, estate planning documents. If your goal is to preserve opportunities for future generations and create a clear path for family assets, then estate planning conversations need to extend beyond Mom and Dad. After all, continuity planning isn't just about the transfer to the next generation – it's about what happens after the transfer occurs. 5. What happens after the inheritance? For example, if a non-farming heir inherits farmland, does the farming successor still have access to that land?
Under what terms?
For how long?
At what rental rate?
Should there be a formal access agreement included in the will and estate plan alongside the inheritance? As I often remind families:   Access to the land can be just as important as ownership of the land.
The same questions apply to corporate ownership. If non-farming heirs inherit shares in the operating dairy corporation:
How will those shares be valued?
How quickly can they be bought out?
How will that buyout be funded?
Can the dairy business actually afford it?
Has a unanimous shareholders agreement been established that outlines the “rules” and financial feasibility before a crisis occurs?
Too often, families focus on how to divide the wealth without discussing how the business will continue operating afterward under various “what if” scenarios. If the family's vision is for the dairy to continue, the estate plan must provide a realistic pathway for both farming and non-farming heirs while ensuring the business remains viable and sustainable long after the senior generation is gone. This is also why the emergency will concept is so important. The financial position of a dairy can look very different during different seasons of the business. Expansion, significant debt repayment, quota purchases, strong profitability, challenging markets or major capital investments can all influence what the business can realistically support. An estate plan that made sense in one season of the business may no longer reflect the realities of the next. As the business evolves through these different seasons, so too must the estate plan. It isn't about updating your will every year. It is about recognizing when the family, the business or its financial realities have changed enough that the plan needs to be revisited. Wealth distribution, ownership transitions and tax strategies need to continue to reflect what the business can realistically sustain if the family’s vision is for the dairy to remain viable for future generations. 6. What if the next generation quits dairying, but not farming? Most people assume that if the successor quits dairying, they will sell out and move to Hawaii. However, it is often more nuanced than that.
What if they sell the dairy and quota but continue farming?
What if they take the dairy sale proceeds and transition into grain, cattle or another agricultural venture? If only a portion of those sale proceeds is required to continue farming, who should benefit from the remaining value?
This is where an important question emerges: Who should benefit from the value that has been built in the dairy business over multiple generations? For many dairy operations, quota represents one of the largest components of family wealth. If quota is sold, and only a portion of those proceeds is required for the successor to continue farming, what happens to the excess value? Should all of it remain with the farming successor(s)? Should some be shared with non-farming siblings? Should some be preserved for future generations? There is no right or wrong answer. Each family will base their decision on their own values and family conversations. However, I encourage families to remember that quota is often viewed as the “golden goose.” The value isn't simply the sale proceeds.   The value is the income-producing engine that generations before us worked tirelessly to build. Every family must decide for themselves whether they want the next generation to have the ability to sell the golden goose – or whether some safeguards could exist to ensure that decision aligns with the family’s long-term intentions. The important thing is that the conversation occurs before the quota is sold, not after. Name it to tame it One phrase I often use with farm families is: “Name it to tame it." Whether we’re discussing retirement income needs, future care costs, tax liabilities, estate ramifications or business succession, uncertainty often creates more stress than the actual numbers. Have your accountant work with a lawyer and ask them: “If I passed away tomorrow and my current will was read, what would the estimated tax bill be?” Because uncertainty is expensive. Assumptions are costly – to both the business and family relationships. A lot of people walk around with this huge fear of what their estimated estate tax liability will be. A proactive estimate allows your family to understand potential tax liabilities, evaluate funding options and build a plan around facts instead of assumptions. This is where the value of a multidisciplinary advisory team is critical. Team members such as your accountant, lawyer, financial planner, lender, insurance professional and transition adviser each bring a different lens to the conversation. When those advisers work collaboratively, families are better positioned to understand the tax implications, legal ramifications, business continuity risks, financial realities and family dynamic considerations surrounding their transition plan. Protect the legacy you’re still building Every conversation, every decision, every document is a way of saying: This matters. You’re not just planning for today – you're protecting what built you and what you will continue to build for your family for generations to come. Remember, this proactive planning isn’t just about land, equipment, livestock, quota, shares or other assets. It is about your values. It is about your relationships. And it is about the legacy you're still writing. An up-to-date will and estate plan helps ensure your wishes are documented, your intentions are understood, and your family is provided with direction during difficult times. After all, protecting a legacy isn’t just about what you leave behind; it is about intentionally shaping what comes next.
