A view of ‘that other’ trade deal Ralph Pearce October 7, 2026 Dealings with the Mercosur bloc are important to Canada’s agri-food sector, but the beef industry must educate itself to contribute meaningfully to trade talks. Sometimes it’s hard to focus on what’s important when one specific item grabs most of the attention. Ralph Pearce Freelance Writer Ralph Pearce has worked as a freelance journalist and editor in agriculture for more than 30 years. He is based in St. Marys, Ontario.  Related U.S. secures trade and investment deal with Japan In the News: Report highlights value of global trade to U.S. beef industry Canadian cattle industry warns Mercosur deal could threaten fragile herd rebuilding Take the ongoing trade dispute between Canada and the U.S.: Since last July, it has held much of the attention inside and outside of agriculture in both countries. The primary focus is on the effects on consumers, producers and the food value chains. But what’s lost in translation is the issue that’s been building for several months between Canada and the Mercosur bloc, made up of Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay and several associate countries. Last January, the European Union (EU) and Mercosur reached a formal agreement to loosen trade restrictions between the two groups. On the surface, the idea of diversifying trade makes sense, especially with the state of relations between Canada and the U.S. Despite Statistics Canada figures from July 1, 2026, indicating an increase in cattle inventories of 3.2% – to 12.1 million – the prospect of increased beef imports from South America are viewed as a threat to efforts to further strengthen Canadian beef herd numbers. The view from the Canadian Cattle Association (CCA) is that the nation’s beef herd is at a critical stage: If prices encourage more production, the herd can continue building. But less expensive imports will negate any incentives to increase beef production. From his perspective, Tyler Fulton, a Manitoba cow-calf producer and current CCA president, says the group’s position remains unchanged: The inclusion of beef market access in any agreement is strongly opposed. Allowing permanent, broad-based access for Mercosur countries would hurt Canada’s beef industry at a critical point, sending the wrong signal to producers. The federal government has been clear on a National Food Security Standard of needing to feed Canadians while relying less on imports. A deal with Mercosur nations would have the opposite effect. “Canada already relies on imports for approximately 30 percent of domestic beef consumption, significantly higher than major competitors such as the U.S., the EU or Australia,” says Fulton. “After years of drought, rising costs and market disruptions, Canadian producers are only now beginning to see signs of recovery.”  Advertisement More than the meat Animal health and regulatory standards in some parts of the Mercosur countries are added concerns for Canadian beef producers and the CCA. Stated simply, some countries do not compete on what Fulton sees as a level playing field and have been identified as such in communications with the federal government. Specifically, there are disparities in animal disease preparedness, decentralized disease surveillance and enforcement and support for frontier regions that tend to have lower surveillance capacity. “Our producers view early detection and immediate response and control of diseases as crucial for the industry,” says Fulton. “Until recently, many of these countries did not have access into Canada due to concerns on sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) measures. Canada expanded this SPS access in these countries, which means more product is eligible for export into Canada.” The result is an increase in imports from the region caused by increased access and the ability to absorb the out-of-quota tariff (26.5%) relatively easily, given their very low production and labour costs. Based on data from 2025, imports from Mercosur members increased 283% year over year, with 70% paying the existing 26.5% tariff. Further access through a free trade agreement is viewed as a huge risk for Canadian beef producers. Tyler Fulton, a Manitoba cow-calf producer and current CCA president, believes that allowing permanent, broad-based access for Mercosur countries would hurt Canada’s beef industry at a critical point, sending the wrong signal to producers. Image by Golden Hour Photography. Impact on farms and communities Fulton also maintains the effects on producers and the communities where they live would be negative in a long-term scenario. Environmental standards and stewardship of the land could suffer. “This is not protectionism as we’re not impeding trade, but rather ensuring new trade agreements make sense for our industry and for Canadians,” he says. “Canadian beef imports have been at the highest levels since 1993 and now represent 30 percent of our domestic consumption. This is higher than similar jurisdictions like the U.S. [19 percent], the EU [7 percent] and Australia [3 percent].” With China’s current ban on imports, Fulton maintains Canada can expect a larger flood of beef imported from Brazil and Australia. Free trade agreements have been strategic, with partners that were complementary, helping producers increase herd value. But least-cost arrangements hurt the ability of producers to rebuild their numbers. Less is more From Sylvain Charlebois’ perspective, it’s not that any imports that will damage Canada’s beef producers (the “what”); it’s the “how.” He believes import volumes might not be significant enough to affect prices, provided any new access is moderate and phased in. He notes that prices reflect cattle supplies, processing, transportation, labour, exchange rates and retail margins, as well as processing capacity. “Beef is among the most contentious issues,” says Charlebois, director of the Agri-Food Analytics Lab at Dalhousie University. “Canadian cattle organizations support trade diversification in principle but oppose granting significantly greater access to lower-cost Mercosur beef without meaningful reciprocal opportunities for Canadian beef. Mercosur producers already export beef to Canada under existing tariff-rate quotas and, in some cases, by paying applicable above-quota tariffs.” On face value, he adds, imports are not inherently bad, especially since Canada exports premium and fed beef while importing significant quantities of lean beef for hamburger production. The two-way trade helps processors balance the carcass and satisfy different consumer demands. The more important issue to Charlebois is the potential cumulative, longer-term effect. Permanent access for large volumes of lower-cost beef could weaken the price signals encouraging Canadian producers to retain breeding animals and rebuild the herd. In 2025, Canada imported roughly 253,000 tonnes of beef and veal, playing a meaningful role in domestic supply. The concern rises, however, when Canada grants permanent access to lower-cost production without equivalent commercial opportunities abroad or for meeting comparable standards. “With drought, war-related energy and transportation risks and tight North American cattle supplies, imports provide useful flexibility,” notes Charlebois, who then echoes Fulton’s concerns. “But opening the market too aggressively could discourage the very domestic herd expansion Canada currently needs.” He maintains that Canada needs a stronger domestic cattle and processing base, while maintaining diversified import and export relationships. Food security doesn’t mean complete self-sufficiency; it translates to retaining enough domestic capacity that the country isn’t dangerously exposed when borders, transportation routes or foreign policies change. Instead, Charlebois’ focus is on three pillars:
Live cattle, premium beef cuts and lean beef trimmings are different markets, and Canada can both export cattle and high-value beef and import lean trimmings for ground beef.
Market access and food safety are two separate issues. It’s possible to deem a product safe for import while debating reciprocity, traceability, sustainability and competitive conditions.
Canada’s cattle market is structurally integrated with the U.S. Mercosur may provide diversification, but it can’t replace the U.S. market in the foreseeable future.
“Short term, cattle prices should remain strong and beef prices elevated,” says Charlebois, referring to slow biological cycles in cattle, drought and pasture conditions, high costs for feed and financing, along with labour shortages. “The central policy challenge is to diversify without sacrificing domestic production or destabilizing the North American relationship further.” Related U.S. secures trade and investment deal with Japan In the News: Report highlights value of global trade to U.S. beef industry Canadian cattle industry warns Mercosur deal could threaten fragile herd rebuilding Ralph Pearce Freelance Writer Ralph Pearce has worked as a freelance journalist and editor in agriculture for more than 30 years.
