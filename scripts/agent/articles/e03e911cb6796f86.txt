Comparing apples to apples with other dairies Nicole Colgren and Morgan Roth September 25, 2026 Comparing feed costs and milk production to their peers has opened the door for these Michigan producers to prioritize changes that directly influence profitability. About four years ago, producers in our region of Michigan wanted to know whether the money they were spending on feed and management was truly generating a return. They were also curious to know how they compared on feed cost and production to other dairies in the area. Their traditional accounting reports could show income and expenses, but they couldn’t answer the peer comparison question in a meaningful, apples-to-apples way. For participating dairies, it anonymously shows their ranking compared to peers in a variety of production and cost metrics. Since then, it has become a dynamic, producer-driven benchmark that pinpoints where dollars are working, where they aren’t and where the biggest opportunities for influencing profitability are. It surprises dairies when they learn where they land, either overall or on a specific metric. Having this knowledge and glimpsing into different practices leads to more direct conversations about what is driving costs and production, and where the biggest opportunities lie. Revealing what leads to what This benchmarking tool has allowed us to have the discussion of what metric leads to profitability. As a farm, are you looking at income over feed cost, total energy-corrected milk, total pounds of components or milk per stall? While comparisons won’t tell you which cow to cull or which barn to build, instead it tells you where the biggest inefficiencies are hiding and what questions the on-farm teams should be asking. It also makes visible the trade-offs a dairy is making and how those trade-offs are being managed. Advertisement It can be eye-opening to see where your dairy falls among its peers. These are examples of what we were able to reveal for some dairies using the comparison tool: 1. ‘Saved’ on feed but left money on the table One dairy was particularly proud of keeping purchased feed costs low. They relied heavily on homegrown forages and high-moisture corn, and their mindset was to “save their way to profitability.” When we plugged this herd into the comparison tool, their thought process on cost and production shifted. Yes, their purchased feed cost per cow was lower than most, but their milk per cow and diet performance lagged behind other herds in the tool. When we modeled what would happen if they invested an additional 20 cents per cow per day in the diet, it showed the herd gained 4 to 5 pounds of milk per cow. For this dairy, it more than covered the extra feed cost and gave them a better sense of maximizing their profitability. 2. Are high herds really feeding that much forage? One assumption we fact checked was: “The highest-producing herds must be feeding very little forage to hit such high milk and component efficiency.” Part of our approach is to use real data, which we then use to test assumptions rather than taking them at face value. The data told us that the highest-producing herds were feeding a similar percentage of forage or more. The comparison allowed for a more honest discussion about bottlenecks and opportunities. Rather than assuming it is out of our control, we can make the comparison and move onto a strategy or solution based on original data from their feed and cows. 3. DMI ranked high, but production ranked low In another case, a herd showed 2 pounds higher dry matter intake (DMI) than any dairy making similar pounds of components. When we dug into why, it pointed us toward cow comfort and facilities. This specific dairy had a longer walk to the parlor, extended time standing in the holding pen and overcrowding. The producer already knew these issues existed, but seeing how they translated into higher feed use for the same components gave them more confidence to rethink culling strategy, stocking density and investment priorities. Working beyond traditional income over feed cost When producing milk today, more isn’t automatically better. With tighter and more volatile milk markets, the thinking has shifted from pushing for higher components or pounds of milk to asking whether each extra pound is profitable. In terms of production and profitability, the question we are now asking is: Is the way I’m getting to my component level or milk quantity actually putting money in the checkbook after I pay for the feed? Using income over feed cost (IOFC) is (and has been) the traditional calculation for profitability because it uses a dairy’s actual milk check and feed costs. However, because each dairy may calculate IOFC differently depending on their inputs and outputs, it does not give us the truest calculation for herd‑to‑herd comparisons. For a better assessment, we use a targeted profitability measure designed specifically for benchmarking similar dairies in the same region. This approach standardizes both the income side and the feed cost side so that herds can compare themselves on a more level playing field. It doesn’t replace your bank statement, but it does offer a consistent, apples‑to‑apples view of how effectively each herd is turning feed into salable milk.  Also, by standardizing numbers across each dairy, the comparison tool helps tease out whether regional costs are playing a role or whether farm-specific management issues – high days in milk (DIM), overstocking or weaker reproduction performance – are more likely to be holding back results. Making it original No two dairies are alike. At its core, comparing ourselves to our peers is not about winning a ranking. It is about revealing the level of production that maximizes profitability and long-term efficiency for an individual dairy. Some herds are focusing on growth. Others want to stabilize, reduce risk or prioritize longevity. The right level of production and efficiency is always tied to what that dairy would like to achieve. Ultimately, we realized that when dairies can compare inputs and output with their peers, it can better show us what to prioritize.
Is it feed cost, feed efficiency, components or a combination?
