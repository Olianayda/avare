In 1947, at the Wisconsin State Reformatory in Green Bay, a bull calf was loaded onto a truck bound for Kansas, and not one dollar changed hands.
The Reformatory ran the best Holstein herd of all the Wisconsin state institutions — and yes, that's exactly what it sounds like. A prison. With a bull barn that half the Midwest wanted access to. Their blood went into serious herds, the kind of operations that produced bulls like Osborndale Ivanhoe, and a breeder who got access to it got it because somebody there thought well of him.
Elmer Dawdy had no money to speak of.
What he had was a young bull sired by Dunloggin Golden Cross — and he'd gotten that mating almost by accident. Golden Cross spent fourteen days at Dawdy's place while en route to Colorado, and Dawdy bred one cow to him during the stopover. One cow. That was the whole opportunity, and he took it.
So he offered that son to the Reformatory, straight across, for a five-week-old calf named Wis Burke Ideal , born May 20, 1947.
They took the deal.
A five-week-old calf. Understand what that means — Dawdy wasn't buying proven genetics; he was buying a guess. Nobody knew what that calf would become. He could have grown into nothing at all and cost Dawdy the only good bull he'd ever bred by accident.
Instead, Wis Burke Ideal sired Tidy Burke Elevation. Tidy Burke Elevation sired Round Oak Rag Apple Elevation. And Elevation — the bull Holstein International crowned Bull of the Century in 1999 — sits behind roughly half of every active Holstein A.I. sire in North America today.
The other half traces to a man in Brampton, Ontario, whose Glenvue prefix Dawdy had probably never bothered to learn, whose favorite bloodline Dawdy would have thrown him out of the barn for mentioning, and who was building the identical monument from the opposite end.
ACT I: BREEDING BLIND
Understand what these men were working with, because it changes how you read everything that follows.
When Doug Dunton bought his first Holstein in 1920, formal classification didn't exist in Canada yet. Holstein Canada wouldn't launch its program until 1925. And even once inspectors were coming through with clipboards and scorecards, what they left behind was a letter on a card — nothing more.
No sire summaries. No daughter averages published in a booklet you could carry to a sale. No reliability figures, no indexes, no way to compare one bull's daughters against another's except by driving to see them.
So that's what you did. If you wanted to know whether a bull's daughters had udders worth having, you got in a car — or a wagon — and you went and looked. You asked the neighbors. You trusted your eye.
Doug Dunton put it plainly years later: if you couldn't see an animal's ancestors to judge how good the type was, and whether it passed along, "you were working blind."
Compare that to what's sitting in your pocket. Genomic proofs. Reliability percentages. Haplotype screens on animals that haven't been born yet. These two men laid the foundation of the modern Holstein cow with less usable information than you'd bring to buying a used truck.
The one cow that ended the Shorthorn experiment
Doug Dunton came to Holsteins sideways, and late.
In 1920 he signed up for a dairy short course run by the Ontario Department of Agriculture. Up to then he and his father had been crossing Dual Purpose Shorthorns — an experiment the record describes, with what I read as considerable mercy, as having "little apparent success."
During the course, Doug got into some good Holstein barns for the first time. He came home and bought one cow. One. Not a conversion — a test.
She milked 80 pounds in a day.
Twice what any Shorthorn on the place had ever given. Picture her standing in that tie-stall row, a big black-and-white stranger among the roans, filling a pail while the home-bred cows on either side of her filled half of one.
You don't need a scorecard to read that. Doug and his father went out and bought four more Holsteins, and the Shorthorns were finished.
Three of the four came from Lorne Davidson's farm at Meadowvale. Two of those three built everything.
Bruach Noelle hit the ground on Christmas Day, 1928 — a daughter of King Segis Vale Spofford. She'd become the maternal great-granddam of two All-Canadian winners.
Her maternal sister, Bruach Luella Acme , was a daughter of King Toitilla Acme, the Davidson herd sire. She started the line that ran down to Glenvue Jean Inka B.
Hold onto Jean Inka B. She comes back at the end, and when she does, it's going to land.
Dunton had no college degree. What he had instead was a conviction so fixed it worked like faith. He believed in close breeding — " breeding warm ," he called it, which is honestly the most farmer description of inbreeding ever committed to print — and he believed the King Toitilla Acme line was better than whatever anyone else was using.
In roughly four decades of breeding, Doug Dunton used an outcross bull exactly once.
The verdict on that experiment is the driest sentence in the whole record: he "didn't do much harm," but "neither did he do much good."
No more outcross bulls were used. Ever.
The Kansas boy who sold his herd to buy an education
Elmer Alfred Dawdy was born in 1915 on his father's place in Washington County, north-central Kansas. He came to dairy cattle through 4-H — with a Guernsey calf, which ought to make every Holstein person reading this smile. By the time college came around, he'd built a small herd of his own.
Then he sold it. All of it. For tuition.
He sold the herd. That's Elmer Dawdy. He'd spend his whole life treating cattle as instruments rather than ornaments, and he opened by liquidating his own to buy an animal husbandry degree from Kansas State.
He graduated in 1938 into extension work — assistant county ag rep for Saline County, then head county agent six months later. That got him named Southwestern fieldman for the Holstein Association, seven states: Texas, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Nebraska, Colorado, Wyoming, Kansas.
And at that point Elmer Dawdy had never owned a Holstein cow in his life.
Think about the strangeness of that. The Association's man across a seven-state territory, and he didn't own one of the animals he was paid to promote.
But the job gave him something no amount of money could buy — he now knew where every bargain, every overlooked cow, and every seller in trouble was located across a quarter of the country.
So he bought a hundred acres in Saline County and started collecting.
Crooked cows from institutions
The Tidy Burke herd never exceeded twenty-four head of milking age.
Twenty-four. Smaller than most modern hospital pens. Smaller than the string a lot of you would cull in a bad year. That's the operation we're talking about.
And the cows in it were, to put it charitably, crooked. Bought for a song, many carrying some physical defect — the kind of fault that makes a breeder's eye slide right past an animal at a sale. Dawdy fixed what he could and lived with the rest.
Two came out of Clyde Hill Farm in Clyde, Missouri. One of them, Clyde Hill Jewel Carine Mercedes , was classified Good the day he bought her.
She was never raised. Not once, the entire time he owned her.
Good. In an era when Good meant middling — the score you didn't mention at the feed store, the cow you apologized for. Year after year the classifier came through, ran his eye over her, and left the card exactly where it was.
That cow founded the family that produced Tidy Burke Elevation.
Sit with that if you've ever culled a heifer on her score alone. The maternal foundation behind half the breed's paternal lineage was a permanently Good cow from a Missouri farm, bought cheap by a man who couldn't afford better.
The rest of the foundation reads like a list of places nobody shops for cattle.
The other Clyde Hill cow became the dam of Raven Burke Elsie — three Gold Medal sons. And at the Colorado State Home for Dependent Children, Dawdy found Posch Ormsby Johanna Bess . She'd produce Ormsby Burke Pontiac Mose, sire of Paclamar Astronaut.
An orphanage. A children's home. A prison herd. A Missouri dispersal.
Even his first herd sire came through persuasion. Dawdy talked Sister Xavier of St. Joseph's Orphanage into buying Sir Bess Tidy from Fred Eiten of Waterloo, Iowa — Eiten proved the bull, the orphanage owned him, and Dawdy used him artificially.
A nun financed the first sire in a program that would reshape the global Holstein population, and there's nothing in the record suggesting Dawdy ever found that arrangement remarkable.
They would not have gotten along
Doug Dunton was pleasant. Accommodating. The kind of man neighbors dropped in on without calling first. He worked as an official classifier for the Canadian association — not a job they hand to men who can't be diplomatic in somebody else's barn. And when his sons Ralph and Earl came of age, they became his partners.
Elmer Dawdy could be abrasive and abrupt, inclined to speak his mind regardless of consequences. His own biographer noted that his normal ways of doing business were at times "very much at odds with accepted methods and behaviours."
His two sons left home after high school. The record's phrasing is nine words long and quietly devastating: they couldn't, it seemed, get away fast enough.
There's a whole story in those nine words, and I'm not going to pretend I know it. But anyone who's worked a farm alongside family understands what a kitchen can feel like when the man running things is certain about everything.
Then there's the bloodline schism, which was absolute. Dunton was a King Toitilla Acme man who braided in the Rag Apple line and spent decades weaving the two together.
Dawdy was a Burke man. Tried and true.
Mention "Rag Apple" in Elmer Dawdy's barn, and he'd throw you out.
Not argue. Throw you out.
ACT II: THE TRADE AND THE TWELVE-LACTATION COW
A row of matched half-sisters
By the mid-1950s, Glenvue Farm at Brampton was producing cows people drove to see.
There's a photograph from those years that captures it better than any production string could — a row of A.B.C. Midnight Joe daughters standing in Dunton's stable, and the record notes they "made an impressive sight."
Picture what that actually means. Half-sisters, tied side by side, matched enough in their toplines and udder attachments that a visitor walking the alley could tell before anyone said a word which bull had sired the lot of them.
That's what breeders came for. Not one great cow — anybody gets lucky once. A row of them, predictable enough to look like they'd been stamped.
The individuals were extraordinary too.
A.B.C. Inka May (EX), All-Canadian 4-year-old in 1947, made 24,142 pounds of milk at 4.67% with 1,128 pounds of fat over 365 days, milked three times a day — that's what "3X" means on an old record, and it was the going method for pushing a cow to her limit before anyone had heard of a robot.
In 1947. When the good cow in your neighbor's barn made 12,000, and he talked about her all winter.
Glenvue Noelle Inka (EX-4*), All-American and All-Canadian aged cow, 1947. Haltered and posed against the gate — this is what Dunton's type-first breeding looked like when it worked. Best in her class on both sides of the border in one season, and her line ran forward to Walkway Chief Mark.
Glenvue Noelle Inka (EX-4*) took All-American and All-Canadian aged cow in 1947 — both sides of the border in one season. Glenvue Rilla Top Grade (EX) was Reserve All-Canadian aged cow in 1951.
And Glenvue Rosalie Fond Hope (VG), out of Rilla Top Grade by Spring Farm Fond Hope, stood grand champion at the Royal Winter Fair in 1955 and took All-Canadian 4-year-old the same year — on the tanbark at Toronto, in front of a crowd that considered the Royal the only show in Canada that truly counted.
Nine Glenvue matrons made Dave Morrow's list when he asked "Who Breeds the Brood Cows?" in the June 10, 1962 Holstein World . Nine, from one farm.
And standing above all of them, an old cow named Glenvue Nettie Jemima (EX-13*).
Glenvue Nettie Jemima (EX-13*), All-Canadian aged cow, 1954. That's not a young cow — twelve lactations wore that frame down. In a barn culling on Good Plus, Dunton chose her twelve separate times.
Twelve lactations. 218,215 pounds of milk. 8,193 pounds of fat.
All-Canadian aged cow, 1954 — in a herd where a cow graded Good Plus or she went down the road.
The number that matters there isn't the milk. It's the twelve.
Because Dunton ran the hardest cull rule in the country: to stay at Glenvue, a cow had to grade Good Plus or better. One exception, only one — a two-year-old who classified Good but carried a Good Plus udder got another year, because Dunton had seen too many good udders show up late.
Everything else went down the road. No sentiment, no second thoughts.
So twelve lactations in that herd means Doug Dunton stood in front of that cow twelve separate times, in an operation where merely good wasn't good enough, and chose her. Through her prime and well past it.
A pleasant, accommodating man who culled without mercy kept looking at Nettie Jemima and deciding, again, that she stayed.
Picture her in her later years — a big, well-worn old cow with the hips and the hollows a twelve-lactation record leaves behind, still carrying an Excellent card, standing in a barn full of animals young enough to be her great-granddaughters. Anyone who's kept an old favorite past the point where the spreadsheet says to knows exactly what that looks like.
She paid him for every one of those decisions. She's the dam of Rosafe Centurion , and of Magician, Signet, and Citation R.
That "13*" on her card, for anyone under fifty: the star system marked qualifying superior offspring. Thirteen of them, in an era of hand-kept records and postal reporting, was a maternal record that bordered on absurd.
The cruel part of Doug Dunton's career
Here's what I find genuinely hard about his story.
Those cows — and type-wise they were far beyond anything Dawdy ever bred, a man who never once placed a cow on the All-American calendar — made other men famous.
A.B.C. Inka May was sold in dam — sold while carrying the calf. A.B.C. Farms bought her mother, Temple Farm May, and under the rules of the day the buyer registered the resulting offspring under his own prefix.
So the cow Doug Dunton bred carried somebody else's name. And her son by Montvic Rag Apple Sovereign — A.B.C. Reflection Sovereign , called by some the greatest bull the breed has produced — carried it too.
Dunton bred the brood cows. Other men banked the bulls.
In his entire career, exactly one bull carrying the Glenvue prefix achieved major impact. His name was Glenvue Clipper , and he'll matter shortly.
What he was actually selecting for
Before the trade, sit with Dunton's philosophy a minute, because it's the most useful thing in this story for anybody building a mating list in 2026.
"It takes longer to breed type than production," he told a reporter, "but once you get type, it is not so difficult to get higher production."
Type first. Always. Forced to choose, type won.
And he selected for three things that appeared nowhere on a pedigree in his day — no index, no proof, no number attached to any of them: temperament. How a cow milks out. Longevity.
Read that list again and tell me it isn't the conversation this industry is finally having, seventy years late, about functional traits, productive life, and whether we bred ourselves into a corner chasing yield.
Dunton was selecting for traits that wouldn't get formal evaluations for another half-century. He did it because he'd decided they mattered more than the things he could measure.
His four stated reasons for Glenvue's consistency, from a 1963 Farmers Advocate interview: strict culling. Slow-maturing animals — his two-year-olds were never pushed, and a thousand pounds of milk in a month satisfied him. Line breeding. The Good Plus rule.
Never pushed. In a business that has spent eighty years pushing.
1947, and the fortnight bull
Down in Kansas, Dawdy had been watching Wisconsin Admiral Burke Lad tear through the breed in the early 1940s — "witnessing the havoc," the record says — and he jumped in with both feet.
He bought Weber Burke Hazelwooder, a Burke Lad son. Then he borrowed Weber Hazelwood Burke Raven from Jake Zarnowski.
Borrowed. Neighbor to neighbor, on a handshake, the way things actually got done before contracts.
Raven was out of an inbred Burke Lad daughter, and he sired Raven Burke Elsie plus Raven Burke Carine Mercedes — the maternal granddam of Tidy Burke Elevation.
Then came the trade.
Wis Burke Ideal , born May 20, 1947, was sired by Wis Ideal (EX-GM) out of Wis Juanita — and both of them were sired by Weber Burke Cyclone , the great inbred Burke son standing at Green Bay.
A son of Cyclone bred to a daughter of Cyclone. That's the calf Dawdy got for a bull out of a fourteen-day stopover.
Wis Burke Ideal (VG-88 GM), born May 20, 1947. Cyclone on both sides — a son of Weber Burke Cyclone bred to a Cyclone daughter. Elmer Dawdy got him from the Green Bay Reformatory as a five-week-old calf, for a bull he'd bred almost by accident. Look at the depth through that chest. Half the Holstein breed's paternal line runs through it.
And then he doubled down. Dawdy bred Wis Burke Ideal to a daughter of Wisconsin Admiral Burke Lad — inbreeding stacked on inbreeding, on purpose, by a man whose entire philosophy on the subject ran five words long:
"You can never get too much of a good thing."
He meant it literally. He was spectacularly right, and — as the breed would learn expensively, decades later — he was also wrong.
The calf was Tidy Burke Elevation.

And here's the collision that makes these two men inseparable rather than rivals.
Elevation's sire came from Dawdy. But run up the maternal line: Elevation's maternal granddam was Round Oak Nettie Emaline , born January 7, 1959, sired by Glenafton Gaiety — and Gaiety was sired by Rosafe Centurion , the son of Dunton's Inka Supreme Reflection out of Glenvue Nettie Jemima.
The old twelve-lactation cow. The one Doug Dunton kept choosing.
The bull named for the Rag Apple line that Elmer Dawdy would have thrown a man out of his barn for mentioning carried Doug Dunton's brood cow on his dam's side.
Neither man had any say in it. Neither, so far as anyone can tell, ever met the other.

Three years before Elevation, the other half of the breed was born — and not where you'd expect. Lester Fishler's Pawnee Farm sat on the south edge of Central City, Nebraska, and when he dispersed the herd on April 14, 1962, a pregnant cow named Pawnee Farm Glenvue Beauty sold for $4,300 to Wally Lindskoog of Arlinda Farms. She rode a train to Turlock, California, and calved twenty-five days later.
The dam was Pawnee Farm Glenvue Beauty (EX) — sired by Glenvue Clipper. Dunton's one great bull.
The sire was Pawnee Farm Reflection Admiral (born February 16, 1960, EX, Gold Medal Sire).
