Last but certainly not least: Tom and Sandy Morris to manage their final World Classic Sale Patryce Eldredge LeFevre September 12, 2026 Cattle sales have always been a valuable part of World Dairy Expo. This year, the Morrises will manage their last World Classic Sale. For the past 38 years, Tom and Sandy Morris have managed the World Classic Sale at World Dairy Expo. Over the years, they have seen changes, built friendships and made memories, but this year will be their last heading up the sale. Patryce Eldredge LeFevre Editorial Intern / Progressive Dairy Related Three producers prioritize data used to manage their dairies Penn State holds 27th Nittany Lion Fall Classic Sale Cal Poly Classic Sale Recap “This is our final tour. We’ve had a good run,” Tom says. “It’s a little tough, but life is change. It’s time to change.” Reflecting on his first World Dairy Expo experience in 1967, Tom recalls that the expo was much smaller at the time. He says it began primarily as a cattle show, with commercial exhibitors joining in the years that followed. Since then, the expo has continued to evolve and expand. “It was a dream people had,” Tom says. “I wish I had movie cameras all the way through the 60 years, because it’s really changed. They started with little or nothing, and it just kept pushing and growing.” The Morrises say some of their favorite memories are the creative sale openings held when the event took place in the Coliseum. One year, the sales force and a calf arrived in a limousine. Another memorable moment involved a giant apple being lowered from the rafters in honor of the great Apple and her progeny. Advertisement The Morrises also express that one of their favorite parts of managing the sale has been meeting people from all over the world with whom they can reconnect each year while continuing to build lasting friendships. Attendees fill the Coliseum for the 2005 World Classic Sale. Image provided by Sandy Morris. History and value of the sales Cattle sales, like the World Classic, have been an integral part of World Dairy Expo from the very beginning. When the expo first began, Norm Magnussen, former manager of the Brown Swiss sale and friend of the Morris family, was just a child. However, his father, Norman E. Magnussen, was one of the founding fathers of World Dairy Expo. Even at a young age, Magnussen saw the importance of cattle sales to the expo. Magnussen says the event had very little money in its early years. Cattle sales were held in the spring of 1967 and 1969 to help generate funding for the newly established World Dairy Expo. Breeders from Wisconsin and the surrounding Midwest states donated calves to be sold to help raise the funds needed to launch the show. Magnussen had the opportunity to travel with Arbor Rose Chat Expo, “Miss Expo,” a March-born calf from Oregon, that topped one of the first fundraising all-breed sales at $1,100. After the sale, the calf, which was purchased by American Exchange Bank, continued to serve as a promotional ambassador, given her name and association with the sales at World Dairy Expo. “That heifer, after the sale, came to my father’s farm in Lake Mills, and the American Exchange Bank sent us a snowmobile trailer that was like a camper,” Magnussen says. “We had built a stall in there. Whenever American Exchange wanted to use that calf as a promotion, we’d have to clip her, wash her and put her in that trailer. Somebody’d come and get her and take her to the Dane County Fair or June Dairy Days or whatever it was for that one year.” Sandy says that today, sales such as the World Classic still provide a commission to World Dairy Expo. Over the last 15 years, the sale has grossed an average of $1.5 million by selling fewer than 50 lots per year with a lot average of $32,000. “During that time, the World Classic established the market for both the high-type cattle and cutting-edge genomic leaders,” Sandy says. While money is a major component of the sale, the sale is also valuable advertising for breeders. “When you’re advertising sale cattle, you’re also advertising World Dairy Expo,” Magnussen says. “At World Dairy Expo, it’s not only the sale. If you’re promoting registered dairy cattle, you’re promoting the particular breed you’re selling. You’re drawing international guests to the event.” Magnussen also says the sales reflect the market and set the standard for high-quality cattle. Because World Dairy Expo attracts an international audience, Tom adds that the World Classic establishes the value of elite cattle worldwide. Shifts in the sale The World Classic has shifted and evolved to better reflect the changing market of dairy cattle genetics over the years. “We started out just selling cows and heifers. Then we started selling embryos, and now we've gone to genomics,” Sandy says. “The genomic market has exploded, and so we've certainly included them, and they've been a big part of it. We went to first choices, IVF [in vitro fertilization] sessions, and we've been selling groups of heifers from high-genomic heifers from one herd as a group. We've also included really top-end animals from different breeds. We've been expanding just as the market has.” The event has also undergone a venue change, moving from the Coliseum after 33 sales to the Tanbark. The Morrises say the new location creates a spacious, relaxing and social atmosphere for sale attendees. It takes a village In addition to managing the World Classic, the Morrises have been long-time cattle and commercial exhibitors at the expo. Tom has also served on the World Dairy Expo Dairy Cattle Exhibitor Committee and the board of directors’ executive committee, with Sandy as a valuable behind-the-scenes partner. Tom and Sandy have been in the auction business for more than 50 years, managing approximately 575 sales across the country during that time. However, Tom and Sandy do not do it all alone; they rely on a great sale crew that helps make each sale possible. The Morrises and their team work to select consignments, prepare the sale sites, design advertising and develop catalogs to showcase the animals offered at each sale. An event of this magnitude does not come without challenges. Tom says one of the toughest challenges is turning away breeders who want to participate and offer their best animals but whose heifers or cows do not quite meet the sale’s qualifications. For the animals that make it into the sale, the Morrises understand the value and responsibility of showcasing the genetics they have been entrusted with. “People entrust to us not only their one or two elite heifers to sell in some elite sale, but over the years, people have entrusted their life’s work with us to sell their dispersal,” Sandy says. “We know that is a huge responsibility, and we’ve taken it very seriously.” A sale barn crowd views the animals to be sold in the 2015 World Classic. Image provided by Sandy Morris. Leaving a legacy Both Tom and Sandy recall being blessed with priceless mentors. Because of their experiences, they hope to be the same for the youth with whom they interact. “It has been gratifying to see so many of these young people go on to highly successful careers as auctioneers, sale managers, sire analysts, veterinarians, nutritionists, communicators and breeders of their own elite herds,” Tom says. For Magnussen, this pivotal point in Tom and Sandy’s career solidifies his admiration for the couple. “I have a lot of respect for Tom and Sandy, the effort they have put in for the sales, World Dairy Expo, the positions that Tom has held and all the work that Sandy does,” Magnussen says. When the final gavel falls this year, the Morrises hope to reflect not only on their roles at World Dairy Expo but also on their place in the dairy cattle industry as a whole. They hope to have made a positive difference for the breeders who entrusted their cattle and life’s work to them. “It’s what we dreamed about, to have a good sale every year and support World Dairy Expo,” Tom says.
