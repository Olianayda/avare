Carmen Willmore September 21, 2026 Heifer retention seems to be the million-dollar question circulating throughout the beef industry. With cattle prices remaining strong and many Western ranchers under drought conditions, the decision rarely has a straightforward answer. The question of whether to retain heifers comes up annually as the fall run approaches, and it is an extremely personal decision for each individual ranch. With beef cow and calf crop numbers down, the pressure to start pulling heifers out of the feeder supply and keeping them for the breeding herd is higher than ever. However, retaining heifers also comes at a high cost. Especially with much of the West experiencing drought this year, reduced grazing availability and high feed costs may cause producers to shy away from herd rebuilding. One key to making the right decision is to compare the cash value of selling a heifer today with the potential long-term value of retaining her. Producers should consider the expected value of the replacement cow and her future calf production, minus development costs and the risks associated with getting her successfully into the cow herd. As with any replacement animal, producers also need to determine which heifers actually deserve a place in the breeding herd. Not every heifer needs to be retained. Producers should evaluate individual animal performance, genetic potential, structural soundness, health history, disposition, growth and expected mature size. Selecting the right females now can impact the productivity and profitability of the herd for years. Reproductive potential should also be a major consideration. First-calf heifers need to breed and calve successfully and then remain productive. When selecting replacements, producers should make sure heifers can reach an appropriate breeding weight and reproductive readiness ahead of the breeding season. Giving first-calf heifers additional time to adjust to the demands of breeding and calving can help them succeed. Advertisement Probably the biggest determining factor for many producers this fall will be feed and forage availability. Do you have enough forage to develop these heifers appropriately? If not, producers have other options. Depending on feed costs and available programs, working with a local feedlot or custom development operation may allow heifers to be developed without additional pressure on limited resources. Otherwise, selling the heifers as feeders and adding replacements in a future year may make more economic sense. Ultimately, there is no universal answer to the heifer retention question. Every ranch has different genetics, forage resources, feed costs, labor availability and financial goals. In a year of strong cattle prices and challenging drought conditions, the goal shouldn't necessarily be to retain the most heifers possible but to retain the right heifers. Careful evaluation of today's market value against tomorrow's potential helps make decisions that fit both the current market and the future operation. Related West: Are your bulls ready for spring?
