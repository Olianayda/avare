Mycotoxins: A potential hidden threat to reproductive efficiency Cathy Bandyk September 1, 2026 If this year’s preg check reveals an unusually high amount of open or late-bred females, it may be worth investigating whether mycotoxin exposure is the culprit in your herd. Fall preg check brings the importance of establishing and maintaining pregnancies in the cow herd into sharp focus. Nothing impacts returns per head as much as delivering a viable calf, whose individual income potential is strongly impacted by how early in the calving season it arrives. So when a group of cows or heifers comes through the chute exhibiting a disappointing number of open and late-bred females, it’s critical to establish the cause. Cathy Bandyk Technical Development Manager / Innovad Email Cathy Bandyk Related Selecting for reproductive efficiency in beef herds Organic trace minerals’ impact on reproductive efficiency and fetal programming How trace mineral status affects reproductive efficiency in bulls A potential but often overlooked culprit in reproductive inefficiencies is mycotoxin exposure. These fungal metabolites can be found in essentially all major feedstuffs in cattle diets: wet feeds, byproducts, grain and forage. Many have been shown to negatively impact both male and female reproductive performance through multiple modes of action. Historically, mycotoxins have received limited attention in beef cattle production, largely due to a lack of research information and default reliance on the detoxification processes active in the rumen. However, more recent evidence has made it clear that mycotoxins can be a significant threat in both the breeding herd and finishing yard. These hazards include:
Specific toxins that resist rumen breakdown
Situations where these defenses are impaired or overwhelmed
Compounds, including zearalenone, that are actually converted to a more toxic form in the rumen
Interrelationships that heighten risk with co-exposure to combinations of multiple toxins or toxins plus other stressors
Growing appreciation that even when detoxification takes place, its metabolic requirements divert nutrients away from other uses and may be preceded by physical damage to the digestive tract before the toxin can be broken down or converted.
Documented reproductive impairments Certain mycotoxins primarily impact hormonal and other messaging functions that can lead to reproductive problems and inefficiencies. The most well known, of course, is zearalenone (ZEN), which can bind to estrogen receptor sites and cause estrogenic effects. In females, this can be reflected in decreased fertility, abnormal estrus cycling, swollen vulvas, vaginitis and mammary gland enlargement. Research has identified numerous drivers of affiliated reproductive dysfunction, including delayed or absent heat cycles, fewer active follicles, reduced oocyte quality and ovulation rate, and lowered progesterone. The concerns don’t stop here. The negative influences of ZEN on reproduction are not limited to stimulation of estrogen receptors. Research has directly tied exposure to: Advertisement
Delayed maturation of oocytes
Damage to genetic material
Reduced viability, proliferation and function of ovary cells
Male fertility and genetic contributions are also susceptible to mycotoxins. In bulls, hormonal disruptions caused by ZEN can negatively impact libido, testicular size, testosterone levels and semen quality. High enough exposure can actually leave bulls sterile. Co-exposure of ZEN with ochratoxin, as well as some of the emerging mycotoxins, exacerbates these issues in both sexes. Beyond zearalenone, aflatoxins   appear to directly inhibit semen viability and DNA integrity. These changes can hurt spermatozoa function, fertilization competence and embryonic development. In one study, researchers identified 345 genes that were genetically altered in blastocysts exposed to aflatoxin. The DON metabolite de-epoxy deoxynivalenol (DOM-1) has experimentally reduced sperm motility, stopped follicular growth when injected into the follicle and reduced the rate of blastocyst formation when administered to females during in vitro fertilization (IVF). T-2 toxin is also affiliated with reproductive failure, in particular late-term abortions. This potent fusarium mycotoxin packs a double punch: It suppresses production of essential reproductive hormones, and it causes direct cellular and oxidative damage to both dam and fetus. Estrus cycles are suppressed or completely absent, fertility is reduced, fetal damage and early embryonic losses occur and, at high exposure levels, calves can be aborted. It is also important to consider what are commonly called the “emerging mycotoxins.” These compounds aren’t newly emerging per se, but awareness and understanding of their prevalence and toxicity is relatively recent. It’s worth noting that published mycotoxin feed testing surveys consistently show beauvericin (BEA) and the enniatins (ENNs) present in a large percentage of commercial farm samples. While scientists are still unravelling the complete modes of action of these toxic compounds, beauvericin, alternariol and the enniatins have been linked to impaired reproductive function in both sexes. More specifically, research has shown dramatic inhibitory effects of BEA and ENN on formation of estrogen and progesterone in cattle. In another study, BEA was shown to impair the development of oocytes in pigs and sheep. And studies done with swine showed that ENN inhibited sperm motility and damaged developing embryos. In all these cases, the direct reproductive symptoms of mycotoxin exposure are likely multiplied by the indirect impacts of reduced feed intake, short-term immune activation and inflammation, and long-term immune suppression associated with most of these compounds. Risk assessment and response The cows themselves are always the best indicator of a problem. If herd preg check detects unusually high numbers of open or late-bred females, or abortions are occurring, it makes sense to consider mycotoxins as either a primary or secondary contributing factor. Standardized feed tests are a valid starting point for establishing mycotoxin presence, but the reported values need to be viewed within their actual biologic relevance. Commercial packages only check for a limited set of toxins, providing no information on others. Because mycotoxins form in pockets, it is difficult to obtain bulk feed samples that accurately reflect overall mycotoxin content. Common guidelines assigning a “risk level” to threshold concentrations of individual toxins give no consideration to the damage caused by chronic (low-level, long-term) exposure, to the additive or synergistic impact of exposure to multiple toxins, or to the stacking of mycotoxin challenges on top of other stressors. Biomonitoring, where samples taken directly from representative animals are tested for mycotoxins and their common metabolites, does allow direct measurement of the mycotoxin pressure actually being experienced systemically. One company has scaled this relatively new technology to a farm-level proprietary service based on dried blood spots, offering a significantly increased level of insight. Whether mycotoxin-induced reproductive issues are suspected or confirmed, proven remediation tools exist. While feed additives cannot technically be marketed in the U.S. for this purpose (since there is not an approved FDA classification this would fall under), there are products available that are comparable to those positioned in other countries for mycotoxin control. Comparison studies affirm the added value of formulations that utilize multiple ingredients with known binding affinities, in combination with components known to support or enhance natural defense systems. Reproductive success is the first step toward economic success in the cow herd. The potential role of mycotoxins in reaching these goals deserves a place in management considerations.
