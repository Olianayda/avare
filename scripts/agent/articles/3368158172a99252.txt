Taking proactive steps to protect the health and safety of everyone is essential in any workplace. Even with a well-established health and safety program, there is always a chance that emergencies could arise. Having a clear, effective emergency response plan is crucial for ensuring everyone knows exactly what to do when an emergency occurs. That is why it is important not to rely on individual judgment in these stressful moments. By having a structured emergency plan in place, everyone can stay calm and respond appropriately. Types of emergencies Emergencies in the workplace can take many forms, including:
Medical emergencies, such as heart attacks, strokes or other sudden medical events
Workplace injuries or illnesses like falls, being struck by objects or exposure to harmful substances
Critical injuries or fatalities in the workplace
Fires, explosions or electrical emergencies that pose immediate danger
Exposure to dangerous gases, chemicals or hazardous materials (e.g., manure gas, carbon monoxide, natural gas)
Workplace violence or other physical threats to employee safety
Natural disasters, including tornadoes, earthquakes, severe storms or major power failures
Each of these emergencies can be very stressful and may cause people to react differently. We’ve seen cases where someone’s instinct to save equipment during a fire could endanger themselves and others. That is why relying on a written emergency response plan ensures everyone is ready and informed when a crisis occurs. How do I prepare for an emergency? The first and most important step in preparation is creating an emergency response plan. This plan should be comprehensive and accessible to all employees. Key elements of the plan should include: Advertisement
Contact information for the emergency response coordinator and an alternate in case the primary coordinator is unavailable
Designated safe meeting locations, both outside and inside the building, to assemble after an evacuation
A map of the workplace showing emergency exits, fire extinguishers, first-aid kits, eyewash stations, chemical storage areas and any other relevant safety equipment
Clear instructions on the specific emergency response protocols for different types of emergencies
Once the plan is developed, it is vital to communicate it clearly to all employees. Make sure everyone understands the procedures and knows how to respond in various emergencies. Conduct regular emergency response drills to give employees a chance to practice and become familiar with the plan. These drills also provide an opportunity to identify any areas where the plan may need adjustment or improvement. The emergency response plan should also be posted on the health and safety board and included in the onboarding materials for new hires to ensure everyone is properly trained from the start. What should be done in the event of an emergency? In the event of an emergency, the first step is to call 911 immediately if outside assistance is needed. After that, the most urgent next steps should be taken. These may vary depending on the nature of the emergency, but here are some general guidelines:
Ensure safety.  Assess your own safety first and then ensure the safety of others on-site. Communicate the situation to all employees and visitors.
Provide assistance.  If someone is injured and you are trained in first aid, provide assistance until help arrives. If you are not trained, direct someone who is or call for professional medical help.
Prevent further harm. If it is safe to do so, take action to stop further injury or damage. For example, turn off machinery or equipment that is causing the problem, or contain a chemical spill.
Evacuate the area.  If necessary, evacuate the workplace calmly through the nearest emergency exit. Proceed to the designated meeting point.
Headcount.  Once everyone is safely evacuated, the emergency response coordinator must conduct a headcount to ensure all employees and visitors are accounted for.
Control secondary risks.  Evaluate and control any secondary risks, such as fire spread, explosion or chemical exposure, and make sure everyone stays safe until emergency responders arrive.
Follow instructions.  Always follow the instructions given by emergency responders, your emergency coordinator or your manager. They will guide you on how to proceed safely.
What information should be provided when calling 911? When calling 911 during an emergency, it is important to provide the following details:
Location of the emergency, including the full address and the specific area of the building
Number of people on-site: Let emergency responders know how many individuals are in the building and whether anyone is missing or unaccounted for
Nature of the emergency: Provide a brief description of the situation (e.g., medical emergency, fire, chemical spill)
Injuries or hazardous materials: Communicate any injuries and the types of hazardous materials involved (such as chemicals, fuels or compressed gases)
Where is the designated safe meeting location? Your emergency response plan should include two designated safe meeting locations:
An outdoor safe meeting area away from the building, to be used if an evacuation is necessary
An indoor safe meeting location inside the building in case of internal emergencies
Having backup meeting locations is also a good idea in case the primary locations are inaccessible. What should you do if an emergency occurs while working outside of the workplace? If you are working offsite and an emergency arises, follow the instructions of the most senior person in charge at that location. Provide first aid if possible and call 911 if necessary. Once it is safe to do so, notify your supervisor of the incident. For employees working regularly at offsite locations or on different job sites, ensure that the emergency response steps are documented and communicated to all team members beforehand. What do we do after an emergency? Once the immediate danger has passed and the situation is stabilized, it is important to:
Assess the scene and the people involved. Ensure everyone is safe and identify witnesses to the event.
Document the incident. Take photographs, if possible, and preserve any physical evidence. Control access to the area to prevent contamination of evidence.
Notify appropriate parties, including management and any regulatory bodies, if required.
Work with emergency responders to assess the workplace and obtain approval to re-enter the building.
Conduct an investigation. After the emergency has ended, conduct a thorough investigation to identify potential improvements to the emergency plan and procedures. This process helps prevent similar incidents in the future.
In some situations, the Ministry of Labour may need to be involved, especially in cases of critical injuries or fatalities. You are required to contact the Ministry of Labour and follow specific protocols, including preserving the scene and participating in any investigations led by an Occupational Health and Safety Inspector. By having a solid emergency response plan in place and ensuring that everyone is trained and prepared to handle emergencies, you can minimize the risks and ensure the safety of everyone in your workplace.
