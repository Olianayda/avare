Home &raquo; Blogs &raquo; Ag Proud Idaho Guest Blog &raquo; The Good Dirt: Camping with cows Idaho Ag Proud The Good Dirt: Camping with cows Chloe Nichols August 31, 2026 Alder Creek Cow Camp installed a new handcrafted sign set out at the beginning of the two-track leading down to the willows lining Alder Creek. The camp has a repurposed yellow and silver bus for living quarters, several livestock trailers, an outhouse and a round pen. Chloe Nichols Freelance Writer Chloe Nichols farms with her husband, Derek, in the beautiful Lost River Valley of Idaho where th... Derek and I were headed up the trail to relax at a favorite campsite not quite 10 miles from the edge of our farm when we saw the sign. I decided right then and there I am going to spend the summers of my second childhood in a cow camp. I need to put this decision in writing and have my two daughters initial it, acknowledging they will follow my wishes no matter how irascible I become about requiring modern conveniences such as electricity and indoor plumbing. There is always a sentimental look that crosses people’s faces when they tell you they grew up living months at a time in a cow camp with their family. I noticed the gleam in Debbie’s eyes when I mentioned it must have been wonderful. “It was the best raisin’ ever. I wouldn’t trade any of it. As a child, it was free and unrestrained,” she said. “In a good way, though. We rode horse, played in the creek, used our imaginations. You never once heard us saying we were bored.” A cow camp seems like maybe it could embody the best of both worlds: the rejuvenating restfulness of camping life and productive work to do every day. We get a taste of it when we pull our little red and white ’57 camper across four different cattle guards to where we camp. It’s a rattling, narrow road with tight turns and rocks to bump over and around. We wait for the cows to move their babies out of the way into the brush or up the hill. If it’s early enough in the afternoon, the cows are grouped in the shade of clumps of aspen, chewing their cuds. Time slows. I take a deep breath and reset to the pace of the mountain meadows and wildflowers, the creeks and the trees, only briefly speculating if anyone remembered to throw in toilet paper. Advertisement I’ve sometimes wondered if we should have kept our outhouse in case we ever acquire a cow camp. It sat in the side yard of our place, too close to the road. I had visions of our teenage daughters taking up permanent residence in the one bathroom of our old house, and my husband or myself becoming desperate enough to venture out to the decrepit privy. Not wanting this scenario to develop into a reality, we let our friend Jayson cart it off to his farmyard. I’m thinking we made the right decision after hearing an account of rattlesnakes in a cow camp outhouse. The gal telling the story left the impression the ensuing excitement from discovering the uninvited visitors caused everyone involved to have accelerated heart rates, maybe even shortened their life span by a few years. I can only imagine the trepidation the next time you needed that particular facility. The way I remember it, this incident happened close to Oreana. We don’t have many rattlesnakes at our altitude, but to be fair, we should probably warn Jayson. Without fail, there’s always that jumpy individual on any overnight camping trip I’ve gone on. It usually happens after dark. The steak kebobs with onions, peppers and mushrooms are polished off, the moon has risen, the campfire dances. Everyone settles into their camp chair, listening to the silence between stories and easy laughter. All of a sudden, there is a loud rustling in the brush and a massive, bulky shadow looms out of the night. The jumpy individual leaps to their feet, hot cocoa mug flying, gets tangled in the legs of the camp chair, sprawls headlong, leaps again, ready to head for tall timber. “It’s just a cow,” says their by-sitter. “Oh,” says the spooked one, sheepishly. They right their chair, find the empty mug and try to pretend they aren’t breathing hard. Everyone chuckles and watches the fire. It quiets down again, and the cow can be heard pulling at bunches of grass or drinking from the creek before she meanders off down the cow trail. I reckon any form of farming or ranching connects us to elements of yesteryear, but Idaho’s cow camps are a living link to the past. Maybe in half a century, when the Alder Creek Cow Camp sign is weathered and faded, the following generations will be there to refurbish and spruce it up. I sure hope so. And when we pull our relic of a camper up beside a rock fire ring, I don’t mind kicking a few dried cow pies aside while Derek builds a cooking fire and I set the cowboy coffee on to boil.
