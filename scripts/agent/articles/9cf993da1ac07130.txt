When culture breaks down: How to spot and address misalignment early Santiago Ledwith August 31, 2026 Listening intentionally, clarifying expectations, consistently leading by example and supporting supervisors are a few ways to realign the farm culture when it starts to drift. In my previous article , I wrote about how supervisors can strengthen culture through regular, intentional conversations with their employees. Those conversations matter because they help connect values, expectations and behaviors in the daily reality of the farm. But there is another side of the culture conversation leaders also need to pay attention to: What happens when those elements begin to drift apart? But when values say one thing, expectations are unclear and behaviors tell a different story, culture begins to weaken. The challenge is that cultural misalignment does not usually happen overnight. It often starts quietly, through small signs leaders may overlook. Early signs of cultural misalignment 1. Communication starts to break down This does not always mean people stop talking. In many cases, there is still plenty of communication happening, but the message is unclear, inconsistent or incomplete. Advertisement This may show up when:
Employees hear one thing from ownership, another from the manager and something different from their direct supervisor.
Priorities change, but no one explains why.
Instructions are given quickly, but expectations are not clearly confirmed.
Employees avoid asking questions because they are unsure how their questions will be received.
Rumors begin to fill the space where clear communication is missing.
When communication becomes inconsistent, employees start filling in the blanks. They make assumptions, rely on rumors and may begin to lose trust. 2. Employees become disengaged Disengagement can look different from person to person. Some employees become quiet. Others do only what is required. Some stop offering ideas, asking questions or taking initiative. Leaders may hear comments such as:
“That’s not my job.”
“Nobody listens anyway.”
“We’ve always done it this way.”
“I just do what they tell me.”
These comments may sound small, but they often reveal something deeper. They may be signs that employees no longer feel connected to the purpose, the team or the leadership around them. 3. Supervisors behave inconsistently Supervisors are culture carriers. Employees watch how they communicate, how they correct mistakes, how they recognize good work and how they handle pressure. Misalignment often appears when:
One supervisor reinforces expectations while another ignores them.
Rules are applied differently depending on the person, shift or day.
Employees receive feedback only when something goes wrong.
Shortcuts are tolerated during busy times, even when they go against the farm’s stated values.
Supervisors say one thing but model another behavior.
Culture is not shaped only by what leaders say. It is shaped by what leaders repeat, reward, correct and tolerate. 4. Values are stated but not lived Many farms talk about values such as respect, teamwork, safety, accountability and care for people. The problem begins when employees do not see those values reflected in daily behavior. For example:
The farm says safety comes first, but shortcuts are accepted when things get busy.
The farm says people matter, but employees only hear from leaders when there is a problem.
The farm says teamwork is important, but departments blame each other.
The farm says respect matters, but correction is done harshly or inconsistently.
Employees pay close attention to the gap between what is said and what is practiced. When that gap becomes too large, credibility suffers. 5. People stop speaking up One of the clearest signs of misalignment is silence. When employees stop asking questions, sharing concerns or offering ideas, leaders should not assume everything is fine. Silence may mean people have learned that speaking up does not make a difference or that it may create problems for them. A healthy culture does not mean everyone agrees all the time. It means people feel safe enough to speak honestly and respectfully. When people stop speaking up, leaders lose access to important information. Misalignment is not always intentional Cultural misalignment does not always happen because people have bad intentions. In many cases, it happens because the operation has grown, roles have changed or expectations have not been communicated clearly enough. A small team may function well with informal communication, but as the farm grows, those same informal habits may no longer be enough. New employees, new supervisors, language differences, generational differences and increased complexity can all create gaps. Sometimes leaders assume expectations are obvious. But what is obvious to one person may not be obvious to another. That is why leaders need to be curious, not defensive, when signs of misalignment appear. How to address misalignment early When culture begins to drift, leaders do not need to wait for a major crisis before taking action. It is much easier to realign early than to rebuild trust after it has been damaged. A simple way to approach cultural misalignment is to follow four practical steps (Figure 1). 1. Pay attention Leaders should not wait until turnover increases, conflicts become personal or performance drops before asking culture-related questions. Early signs often appear in small patterns. Helpful questions to ask include:
Are our stated values visible in daily behavior?
Do supervisors communicate expectations consistently?
Do employees feel safe asking questions or raising concerns?
Are we recognizing the behaviors we say we want?
Are we tolerating behaviors that work against our culture?
The goal is not to blame. The goal is to notice where values, expectations and behaviors may be drifting apart. 2. Listen intentionally Once leaders notice signs of misalignment, the next step is to listen with purpose. Anonymous surveys, small group conversations and one-on-one check-ins can help leaders understand what employees are experiencing. The goal is not to collect complaints. The goal is to identify patterns. Pay attention when several employees mention similar concerns, such as:
Unclear instructions
Inconsistent discipline
Lack of recognition
Poor communication between shifts
Confusion about priorities
Those are not just isolated comments. They may be cultural signals. 3. Clarify expectations Values need to be translated into daily behaviors. It is not enough to say that respect, teamwork, safety or accountability are important. Leaders and supervisors need to define what those values look like in real situations. For example:
If respect is a value, what does respect look like during a stressful shift?
If teamwork is a value, what does teamwork look like between departments?
If safety is a value, what behaviors must never be ignored, even when the team is busy?
If accountability is a value, how should supervisors model accountability before asking for it from employees?
The more practical the expectations are, the easier they are to reinforce. 4. Support supervisors Supervisors are often the bridge between leadership’s intentions and the employee’s daily experience. If supervisors are not aligned, the culture will not be aligned. That is why supervisor development is not just a training activity. It is a culture strategy. Supervisors need tools to:
Communicate expectations clearly
Listen without becoming defensive
Give feedback in a respectful and timely way
Recognize good performance
Correct behaviors consistently
Handle conflict before it grows
Lead across language and cultural differences
When supervisors are supported, they are better prepared to bring values, expectations and behaviors together in the daily work of the farm. Bottom line We know that culture does not usually break down overnight. It weakens gradually through missed conversations, unclear expectations, inconsistent behavior, silence and unresolved frustration. But the good news is that those same areas can also become the starting point for realignment. When leaders pay attention early, listen with humility and help supervisors lead with consistency, they can bring values, expectations and behaviors back together before misalignment becomes the norm. Every farm has a culture. The question is whether that culture is being shaped intentionally or allowed to drift by default. And when culture begins to drift, the best time to realign it is early, before small signs become big problems.
