Choosing the right bedding material: Looking beyond the price tag Dan Veeneman September 1, 2026 Deep-bedded sand can be excellent for cows but only when managed correctly. The best bedding system is the one a farm understands, believes in and will maintain daily. Walk into 10 dairy barns and you are likely to hear 10 different opinions on the best bedding material. Some producers swear by deep-bedded sand. Others have made recycled manure solids work well. Still others have managed mattress systems successfully for years. But when it is done poorly, it can quickly become one of the worst environments for cows. Low stalls, contaminated sand, poor drainage, uneven bedding, inadequate grooming or a manure system that cannot handle sand can turn a good idea into a daily problem. That is why the real question is not “What is the best bedding material?” The better question is “Which bedding system fits our farm’s management style, facilities and labour – and which one will we maintain correctly every day?” A practical evaluation starts with five criteria: cow comfort, udder health, labour, manure system compatibility and total cost of ownership. Cow comfort comes first Lactating dairy cows typically need about 12 to 14 hours of rest each day, and lying time is closely tied to rumination, hoof health, blood flow to the udder and overall welfare. A University of Tennessee Extension review notes that Wisconsin freestall herds averaged 11.9 hours per day resting, while Canadian freestall research reported a median lying time of 10.6 hours per day across 111 farms. Those numbers matter because cows that cannot rest enough are often telling us something about stall design, stocking density, time away from the pen, heat stress or bedding comfort. From a comfort standpoint, well-managed deep-bedded sand remains the benchmark on many farms. Properly maintained sand conforms to the cow’s body, provides reliable footing as cows rise and lie down, drains well and helps reduce pressure on hocks and knees. But those benefits depend on management. Sand that is allowed to pack, become contaminated, develop low spots or remain wet no longer functions like the bedding system producers intended. Advertisement Udder health depends on dry, clean bedding The cleanest stall is not always the one with the least bedding. It is the one that stays dry and limits bacterial growth where the teat end contacts the stall surface. Because sand is inorganic, it provides fewer nutrients for bacteria than organic materials. In a 168-herd study published in the Journal of Dairy Science , manure solids bedding was generally associated with higher bedding bacteria counts, dirtier udders, higher coliform and streptococci-like organism counts in bulk tank milk, and poorer udder health measures compared with new sand, reclaimed sand or organic non-manure bedding. That does not mean organic bedding cannot work. Sawdust, shavings, straw, composted packs and recycled manure solids can all be part of successful dairy systems. The management requirement is simply higher. Moisture is the enemy. As bedding moisture rises, bacteria have more opportunity to multiply. Frequent bedding additions, routine stall grooming, good air exchange and aggressive manure removal are not extras; they are the system. Be honest about labour Every bedding system requires maintenance, but not every system requires the same type or amount of work. Deep-bedded sand needs regular grooming, leveling and replenishment to prevent low spots and keep stalls inviting. Organic bedding requires frequent replacement or conditioning to control moisture and bacterial growth. Compost-bedded packs require daily cultivation and careful attention to pack moisture, especially during humid weather. Mattress systems usually reduce bedding volume and can simplify daily routines, but they still need regular cleaning and enough bedding on top to keep cows comfortable and dry. Before committing to a bedding material, producers should ask a blunt question: Can our team maintain this system consistently on weekends, holidays, during cropping season and when labour is short? If the answer is no, the bedding may look good on paper but fail in the barn. A system that fits the farm and is maintained correctly will outperform a better system that the farm is not set up to manage. Match bedding to the manure system Manure handling is often treated as a secondary consideration during bedding selection, but it can determine whether the system remains practical over the long term. Sand is comfortable for cows, but it is abrasive. Pumps, scrapers, separators, transfer equipment and storage systems must be designed with sand in mind. Farms planning to use deep-bedded sand should consider sand separation and recovery during facility planning, not after equipment wear or storage issues appear. Organic bedding generally moves more easily through conventional manure systems, but it can affect storage characteristics, separator performance and nutrient management. Recycled manure solids, in particular, need careful processing and handling because the bedding source itself originates in the manure stream. Look at total cost, not just purchase price The delivered price of bedding is only one line in the budget. Labour, equipment maintenance, manure handling, stall repair, mastitis treatment, discarded milk, lameness, cull rate, replacement bedding and milk components all contribute to the true cost of a bedding system. Deep-bedded sand may require more investment in manure-handling infrastructure, but that investment can be offset when it supports better cow comfort, cleaner udders and fewer health events. Recycled manure solids may reduce purchased bedding costs, but they raise the bar for processing, moisture control and hygiene monitoring. Mattress systems may lower bedding use, yet they still depend on good stall design and routine bedding additions. The lowest-cost bedding material on the invoice is not always the lowest-cost system over the life of the barn. Stall design and ventilation still decide the outcome No bedding material can compensate for poor stall design. Stall width and length, neck rail placement, brisket locator position, curb height and lunge space all influence whether cows choose to use a stall. Ventilation is just as important. Air exchange and air speed help remove moisture from the bedding surface and from the barn environment. Even high-quality bedding will disappoint if moisture is allowed to accumulate. For farms that can manage it correctly, deep-bedded sand remains one of the strongest options for cow comfort and udder health. Its advantages are supported by decades of university and field research. But sand is not magic. If stalls are not maintained, if sand quality is poor, if manure handling is not designed for it or if daily grooming slips, deep-bedded sand can go from best-in-class to a liability. Ultimately, choosing bedding is not about chasing the single best material. It is about choosing the best-managed system for that dairy. The right bedding is the one that fits the people, facilities, manure system and daily routines on the farm. When those pieces line up, cows respond with cleaner udders, healthier feet, longer rest periods and better performance. Does the bedding encourage cows to lie down for 12 hours or more per day?
Can the team keep stalls dry, level and clean every day?
Does the manure system match the bedding material?
Have mastitis risk, lameness risk and equipment wear been included in the cost calculation?
Are stall design and ventilation supporting the bedding program?
