The telephone calls came the following year, and each one asked the same thing.
Ayrshire breeders across the American Northeast were ringing officials at Waterloo, Iowa — home of the National Dairy Cattle Congress, the show that mattered most on that side of the border until the dairy associations pulled their support in 1965 and took the big shows to Madison. The callers weren't asking about entry deadlines or stall assignments.
Are the LaFranchis coming to Waterloo this year?
Yes, they are.
We won't be there, then. What's the use?
Grown men who'd given their lives to Ayrshire cattle, men whose prefixes carried real weight — Lippitt, Meredith, the staid old Northeastern establishments — deciding it wasn't worth loading the truck. All because three brothers from Calistoga, California had come to Iowa the previous fall with Oak Ridge Farm and walked out with seven class winners and most of the grand championships.
Norman Nabholz, who wrote Millionaires in the Cornfields about the herds that dominated Waterloo and who never owned an Ayrshire in his life, said it about as plainly as anybody could: "Probably the finest group of dairy cattle that ever existed."
Here's the part nobody said out loud on those phone calls, because everybody already knew it.
The LaFranchis hadn't invented those cattle. They'd bought the engine. Six of their herd sires were bred at one place — a farm in Glengarry County, Eastern Ontario, four miles northwest of the village of Lancaster, where people still spoke with Scottish burrs and the Ayrshire cow was, as the old accounts have it, religion. Some of their foundation females came from there too.
The prefix was Glengarry. And this is how a family of Scottish immigrants working 180 acres, hauling cows to the bull on a stoneboat behind a pair of Belgians, ended up supplying the genetics that emptied the trucks of an entire show circuit.
ACT I: THE SCOTS BY THE QUEBEC LINE
Livestock historian E.W. Morwick, who preserved most of what we know about this farm, heard the legend as a schoolboy in short pants. Somewhere down by the Quebec border, he was told, was a tight little community — half a dozen small villages — where all the good Ayrshires came from. Names got mentioned. Bonnie Brae. Burnside. Cherry Bank. Mapleshade. But the name he heard most was Glengarry.
Then, as a teenager hauling to the Canadian National Exhibition with Holstein show herds, he stopped to listen every time the Ayrshire results came over the public address. Selwood. Sanholm. Selwood again. Never Glengarry. Never Mapleshade.
So he said what any honest kid would say: maybe those herds down by the Quebec border aren't as good as people claim.
His verdict, written decades later, runs three words. They were better.
Now, the thing about the Ayrshire in that era — you have to picture what these people actually had standing in the tie-stalls. This was the only major dairy breed to come out of the British Isles, developed in the county of Ayr in southwestern Scotland. Red and white, medium-framed, mature cows running just over a thousand pounds. Long, light horns with dark tips, back when nobody dehorned. Remarkably straight toplines carried level over the rump, and udders good enough that breeders bragged about them in print. Milk of fine curd texture that made first-rate cheese — which mattered enormously to Scottish farmers and, later, to the men shipping into Montreal.
Scottish settlers brought them over in the early 1800s. The Quebec importers organized in 1870, Ontario's breeders in 1872, and the two amalgamated in 1898 into the association that still registers the breed today. So by the time this story starts, Canadian Ayrshire breeding had herd books, institutions, and a settled habit of going back to the old country for fresh blood.
John Cumming came out of Kintyre, Argyllshire, in 1825 and settled at Allan's Corners near Ormstown, in the Châteauguay Valley southwest of Montreal. His son Donald was born there on April 18, 1844.
Donald's name first appears in Volume 2 of the Ayrshire herd book, as owner of a female called Snowball 3854, born May 6, 1886, bought from James Cottingham of Ormstown. Volumes 3, 4, and 5 list many more.
About two decades after those first Ayrshires, Donald moved the whole outfit across the St. Lawrence into Glengarry County and took up 180 acres in the McLennan Settlement. Ten children. The eldest son was Malcolm — Mac to everyone. The youngest was Donald Jr.
Those two are where a good herd becomes a dynasty.
When the old man retired in 1915 — he'd die in 1927 at 83 — he split the farms and the herds between them. Donald Jr. took the home place. Mac set up on the next concession. Their father wanted two separate operations, and on paper that's exactly what he got.
The brothers had other ideas.
They'd own the herd sires in partnership. They'd ship milk to Montreal under one contract. Every calf born on either farm would be registered under the Glengarry prefix, bred by Cumming Bros., and anything shown at a fair went into the ring under that single name.
One prefix. One reputation. Two households doing the chores.
And this is where it gets complicated, because the arrangement bred friction as reliably as it bred cattle. The bulls lived at Donald's farm. That meant Mac had to bring his cows down to Donald's yard — usually on a stoneboat, that flat sled farmers dragged over bare ground or snow, hitched behind a team of Belgians.
Donald met him at the barn.
And Donald paid strict attention to which cows got bred to which bull. If Mac showed up with one he didn't like, Donald told him to take her home.
Picture it. Your brother has hitched the team, loaded a cow, driven her down the road — and you're standing in the doorway telling him to turn around.
That isn't stubbornness for its own sake. That's a standard being enforced at the one place standards usually collapse: where saying no costs you something personal. Everything Glengarry became grew out of that refusal.
And don't mistake Mac for the junior partner in the argument. He held the presidency of the local Ayrshire club from 1931 to 1948, the better part of two decades, and by 1941 he was standing in the middle of the ring at the CNE as the man placing the Ayrshire classes — the same show where young Morwick had listened in vain for the Glengarry name. Think about the endorsement buried in that. The breeder who got sent home from his brother's barn door was the breeder Canada's biggest fair trusted to sort everybody else's cattle.
Two men with the eye, then. Neither one folding.
Mac Cumming, Ayrshire judge at the Canadian National Exhibition, with Holstein fieldman Bob Holtby, 1941. Morwick spent his teenage years at this fair waiting to hear Glengarry called over the public address, and never did. What he missed was the man in the middle of the ring, deciding who won.
The Scottish Pipeline
What separates Glengarry from a hundred other capable farms is that the Cummings never settled for the genetics that happened to be nearby. Year after year, they imported from Scotland themselves or bought out of other men's shipments.
Understand what an import meant then. No semen tank, no embryo, no genomic test to tell you what you'd bought. You paid for a living animal, put her on a ship, and prayed. Sometimes she carried the next generation inside her, and that gamble was the whole point.
Their 1927 shipment brought in Dalfibble Narcissus. In 1928 she made 22,050 pounds of milk on yearly test — the highest cow in Canada that year, any breed. Ayrshire herd averages of that era ran nearer 10,000 pounds over a full lactation, so she was better than double her breed's ordinary. Townhead Julia and Hobsland Nancy came over in the same lot, and both founded families that mattered.
But the cow who paid for that whole era a thousand times over came later, and she reached Glengarry by a roundabout road through one of the great Canadian herds.
The Honourable Frank C. Biggs, proprietor of Glen Campbell Farm near Dundas, and Ontario's Minister of Public Works and Highways from 1919 to 1923. His 1936 order for a dozen Scottish heifers is what sent Adam Montgomerie into the Threave barnyard. Biggs never owned the yearling he found there — the Cummings bought Threave Flirt 2nd when she landed.
