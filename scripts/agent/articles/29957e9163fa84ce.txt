Image courtesy of Lallemand Animal Nutrition. Safety at the silage pile Tyrell Marchant September 4, 2026 The silage pile may seem like the most uneventful place on a ranch or feedlot. But things can change suddenly, and being aware of the best safety practices can prevent disaster. Complacency. We all let it seep in on occasion. When you do something thousands of times without incident, it’s easy to forget that some of the stuff we do can be dangerous. Of course, the occasional slip of the pocketknife or snot-blowing mama cow will wake you up and remind you to pay attention – at least until you get complacent again. Feeding the future: Ground-level takeaways from the International Silage Conference When you’re working on the regular with live animals, heavy equipment, big trucks and medical-grade pharmaceuticals, you could be forgiven for viewing the silage pile as, well … mundane. But there’s a lot of potential for it to become one of the most dangerous places on the operation. “The most important factor [in silage safety] would be complacency, especially for people who have been in this game for a while,” says Renato Schmidt, a forage products specialist at Lallemand Animal Nutrition. “You think, ‘I do this every day,’ and you don’t think it’s as dangerous as it really is. If you’re not really paying attention to the hidden dangers, that’s when the accidents will happen.” A major part of Schmidt’s role at Lallemand is consulting with producers and extension services on maintaining silage quality and labor efficiency while ensuring worker safety. It’s not always an easy balance to strike, but a commonsense approach can swing the pendulum in the right direction. “[Working on the farm or ranch] is a dangerous occupation,” Schmidt continues. “You have all this machinery, you’re working with animals, a lot things going on at the same time. But there are things that we can control, and those are the important things to focus on.” Advertisement When it comes to working with silage, Schmidt points to three primary dangers that every operator should be protecting against: silage avalanches, equipment rollover and toxic gases. Silage avalanches Anyone who’s been to a few feedlots or dairies has seen it: A silage pile built up higher than the loader or rake can reach, the face of the pile concave, the crusty upper layer forming a precarious overhang. “You see a lot of operators just reach as high as they can with their equipment,” says Schmidt. “Sometimes there’s the entire dome of the pile they cannot reach. They form that overhang, just remove what they can and back up quickly, and hope it won’t fall on them.” Anyone working closely with silage should watch carefully for fissures in the pile, which can quickly lead to dangerous avalanches.  Image courtesy of Lallemand Animal Nutrition. The problem with that thinking, of course, is that it can fall on them. A cubic foot of silage can weigh as much as 45 pounds; an avalanche can be hundreds or even thousands of times that weight and force converging on a single point. A significant portion of prevention here is the construction of the silage pile itself. It is recommended that silage be packed in a “progressive wedge” pattern, with a run-to-rise ratio of 3-to-1, meaning 3 feet of horizontal ground is covered for every 1 foot of rise. And while using the bucket to shave off the face of the pile nice and straight may keep it looking pretty, removing material from the pile should mirror the packing to some degree. “The silo shouldn’t be any higher than the equipment you’re using to remove [it],” Schmidt says. “That rake should reach all the way to the top, and once you start, you should pull down from the top instead of just going straight into the pile. That will prevent forming that overhang.” Equipment rollover The leading cause of fatal workplace accidents in agriculture is equipment rollovers, and the environment of a silage pile can brew up the perfect storm to cause one. Several factors can lead to a rollover: machinery breakdown (such as a broken axle or brake failure), an uneven or unexpectedly soft driving surface, steep slopes, human error … the list goes on. Many of the safety practices to prevent avalanches also contribute to stability of the equipment while working in and around the silage pile. Uniformly loaded and packed forage significantly decreases the risk of rollover during filling, particularly if tractors need to be backed up steep slopes. Just about every piece of farm equipment anymore is equipped with a rollover protection structure and a safety belt, and Schmidt’s experience has shown him that wearing that belt is invaluable in keeping a wreck from becoming a tragedy. “Only trained and experienced personnel should be allowed to operate silage equipment,” Schmidt says.  Schmidt points to one final but crucial preventive practice to help prevent anything catastrophic: communication. “It’s really important for you always to be aware of your surroundings,” he says. “Stop, look, listen, and if you’re outside the vehicle and have a reflective safety vest, that helps a lot. If you need to take a forage sample, make sure somebody knows where you are.” Nitrogen dioxide, often called silage gas, produces an orange coloration around the silage pile. It should be properly vented prior to being worked with. Image courtesy of Lallemand Animal Nutrition. Silage gases The ensiling process causes the formation of multiple hazardous gases, most notably carbon dioxide (CO 2 ) and nitrogen dioxide (NO 2 ). And while a silage pile definitely has its own distinct smell, CO 2 itself is invisible and has no odor, which can present a bit of a problem when it comes to detection. However, Schmidt says CO 2 is often accompanied by a visible “heat wave” or shimmering effect that can serve as a warning. NO 2 , often referred to as silo gas, can be even more dangerous. When inhaled, it is converted to nitric acid and nitrous acid in the lungs, causing inflammation in the throat, nose and eyes and ultimately possibly even a narrowing and scarring of the airway. Thankfully, it’s a bit easier to detect than CO 2 . “It’s a gas that is heavier than air, so you can sometimes see it close the ground,” Schmidt says. “You’ll see an orange coloration [of the silage] – sometimes a yellow or a dark red – and it smells kind of like laundry bleach. … If you see any signs, notice a weird smell, see that coloration or gas being released, just walk away, wait for it to dissipate away from that front side of the pile. Don’t have that roll-the-dice mentality.” Safety meetings Schmidt recommends owners hold a meeting at key times to discuss silage safety with employees and go over the dangers, even for those with a lot of experience. “A safety meeting once or twice a year would be ideal,” he says. “For example, preharvest, go over all those points on equipment management, the packing, the covering, safety inspection of the equipment. Make sure you have a fire extinguisher, proper lighting, safety signs, etc. “Just making sure people are aware can go a long way.” Related Bunker silo and silage pile safety Silage pile top layer: Silage or compost?
