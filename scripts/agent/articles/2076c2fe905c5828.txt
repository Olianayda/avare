Receiving calf nutrition: Building the foundation for health, growth and performance Brandon Koch September 11, 2026 Getting calves off to a good start in the feedlot sets the stage for the rest of their time there. An eye toward consistency and calf comfort are critical those first few days. The first few days after calves arrive are some of the most important for successful performance. Stress from weaning, transportation, commingling and a new environment can quickly reduce feed intake and increase the risk of illness. A well-planned and consistent receiving nutrition program helps calves recover faster, supports immune function and lays the foundation for long-term performance. Every nutritional decision made during those first days influences how a calf performs in the later production phases. The objective is not simply to maximize gains during receiving but to establish healthy, consistent growth throughout the entire feeding period. Any practice that reduces stress on cattle during the initial phase after weaning can help lower the risk of health challenges and improve weight gain. Calves that experience less stress are more likely to maintain feed intake, respond appropriately to health protocols and transition successfully into the next feeding phase. Understanding the health history of incoming cattle is also important when developing a receiving program. Calves with no vaccinations or an unknown vaccination history prior to shipment are generally considered high-risk cattle. They require closer observation and more intensive management during the receiving period because they face a greater risk of respiratory disease and other health complications. Nutrition and health management must work together no matter the cattle source, to support calves as they adapt to a new environment and recover from the stresses associated with weaning and transportation. Advertisement Water: The first priority Before producers think about protein, energy or feed additives, they should focus on the most essential nutrient: water. Water intake drives feed intake. If calves are not drinking, they are also unlikely to begin eating, delaying recovery and increasing the likelihood of health challenges. Calves that have just arrived may not immediately know where the water source is located. Making water easy to find can encourage calves to drink sooner. Add additional stock tanks or let water trickle over to support calves unfamiliar with the automatic waterer’s location. Encouraging feed intake Once calves begin drinking, the next priority is getting them to eat as quickly as possible. High-quality, long-stem grass hay should be presented in a way that is familiar and highly visible. Filling bunks with hay that extends above the edge helps calves recognize it as feed and encourages them to investigate. Using high-quality hay during the receiving period instead of lower-quality forage can significantly improve early intake because calves are more likely to consume feed that is recognizable and palatable. As they become comfortable eating from the bunk, producers can gradually introduce calves to a ration over one to two transition days. Slow, deliberate transitions help them adapt while minimizing digestive disruptions. Building a diet that supports health and performance During the receiving phase, calves typically consume less feed than they will later. Because intake is limited, every bite needs to deliver more nutritional value. This means rations should be formulated with concentrated levels of protein and energy to support recovery while supplying the vitamins and minerals calves need to maintain immune function during this stressful transition. Since calves are not consuming a full ration, fortifying the diet helps ensure they receive adequate nutrition despite lower feed intake. Protein and energy often receive the most attention because they are adjusted as calves grow, but vitamins and minerals remain equally important throughout the feeding period. Early in the receiving phase, when calves are eating less than expected, ensuring they receive adequate levels of key nutrients, including trace minerals such as zinc, manganese and copper, along with essential vitamins, helps support normal immune function and prepares calves for the next growth stage. The goal during receiving is not maximum gain. Instead, producers should focus on helping calves overcome stress, establish consistent feed intake and build the nutritional foundation for efficient performance throughout the rest of the feeding period. Consistency is critical One of the biggest challenges during the receiving period is inconsistency. Unexpected ingredient substitutions, feed shortages or abrupt ration changes can disrupt rumen function, reduce feed intake and negatively impact performance. Planning feed inventories before calves arrive helps prevent unnecessary stress. Consistency doesn’t just apply to feed ingredients. Regular feeding schedules, management practices and nutritional programs all contribute to healthier calves and more predictable performance. Management practices that support nutrition Nutrition alone cannot overcome poor management. Newly received calves benefit from adequate rest after transportation before processing, along with immediate access to clean water and quality forage. Producers should also work with their veterinarian and nutritionist before calves arrive to develop vaccination, processing and treatment protocols that fit their operation. Daily observation is equally important. Because cattle naturally mask signs of illness, producers should observe calves multiple times throughout the day and not rely solely on behavior at feeding. Watching for subtle behavioral changes and appetite or feed intake allows producers to identify health concerns earlier, improving treatment success and helping calves stay on track. Monitoring intake to measure receiving success One of the best indicators of the receiving nutrition program’s success is feed intake. Calves that consistently eat are more likely to remain healthy, continue growing and transition successfully into the next phase of production. If intake begins to decline, producers can investigate potential nutritional or health issues before they become larger problems. Ultimately, a successful receiving program is measured by how well calves adapt to their new environment, establish consistent feed intake and perform in the weeks and months that follow. Prioritizing water, encouraging early feed intake, maintaining nutritional consistency and working closely with a veterinarian and nutritionist creates a strong foundation for long-term health and performance. When calves get off to the right start, they’re better positioned to grow efficiently, stay healthier and reach their full potential.
