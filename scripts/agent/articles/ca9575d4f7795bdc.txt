Email Ryan Dennis It was the question my father was always asked whenever he ran into other dairy farmers, whether at a show, an auction or the feedstore. My father’s, “Well, you know” was enough because both parties knew it was a greeting more than a question. Even if it could be answered, what it was probably asking was something along the lines of: How are you managing to survive? In the end, instead of farming like my father, I ended up writing about it. With publishing my second book, Barn Gothic: Three Generations and the Death of the Family Dairy Farm , I got to share our family’s story in the difficult conditions faced by producers since the ’90s, as well as call out the ways in which the American dairy industry has failed its farmers. Since then, the greeting I usually get is, likewise, predictable: “How’s the book going?” By the time this column is published, it will have been 10 months since Barn Gothic was released by Island Press. When an author’s publication day arrives, there’s a feeling of personal elation … which soon gives way to anxiously waiting for reader reactions. Assuming the mail arrives before the afternoon, surely most people would have finished the book by evening, right? Advertisement Sometimes, the second question asked is how many copies were sold. When I give the number, the other person usually shrugs, not sure what to do with it. In total, I’m not sure either. Publishers who have been in the game for a while have perfected the art of suggesting an ambitious sales figure when signing without ever promising that it will happen. I asked the author of a book on a related topic how many copies he had sold. He refused to tell me. “That matters less than how many copies people think were sold,” he said, now having been on television and in a documentary, as well as getting all the speaking engagements he could want. (The same author also gave me other advice: “Get sued by Dairy Farmers of America. That would be great marketing!”) Sometimes I joke that writing is a lot like farming in that it’s a poor financial decision. Or, maybe said more nobly, you neither write nor farm because you want to but because you can’t not write or farm. (Naturally, I don’t point out that one of them involves less broken machinery and calves born backward.) Still, there’s another similarity between farming and writing that I learned from watching my father: At the end of the day, you have to decide for yourself what you’ve accomplished and the impact it has had, because numbers in a milk check or sales report won’t do it for you. Believing that to be true, I have plenty to be grateful for in publishing Barn Gothic . One of the biggest privileges of these 10 months has been how many people have reached out and shared their stories, too. Letters and emails have come in from all across the U.S. and as far away as Sweden. Most of them are farmers or the children of farmers who know what it was like to try to keep a dairy farm from going under during adverse circumstances. They say that they love farming and lament what it has become. Many of them draw parallels between the experiences of their families and mine, from farm-related injuries to family drama. Often, these readers can’t believe how much we have in common, and in total, neither can I. There’s been one phrase I’ve been told over and over again, and that makes it all worth it: “This is my story, too.” Soon, I’ll get to take advantage of another honor: attending the World Dairy Expo in the Progressive Dairy booth. Not living in the U.S. now, I haven’t had as many opportunities as I would like to hold readings and speaking events, or to find other opportunities to talk with readers on the ground. However, Progressive Dairy has offered me the chance to sign books and meet with people at their stand. I owe a lot to Walt Cooley and Progressive Dairy for helping me get this far. When I pitched a “literary” column about dairy farming 15 years ago, Walt was the only editor that took it seriously. Getting to explore the experience of farming in The Milk House laid the foundation for Barn Gothic , and in fact, created some early material for it. Therefore, getting to sit in the Progressive Dairy booth from Sept. 29 to Oct. 2 is both an honor and the feeling of something coming full circle. With The Milk House column for Progressive Dairy , writing and farming were not parallel pursuits for me but an opportunity for them to intersect. At this point, I don’t think Justin Beiber is going to post about Barn Gothic on his Instagram, nor can I think up a good enough scandal to draw useful publicity. Still, I hope to get to hear from more farmers and maybe even some of them in person at the World Dairy Expo. Stop by and tell me how they’re milking.
