Cover crop options after corn silage Heather Smith Thomas September 11, 2026 After corn silage, earlier planting windows open the door to more cover‑crop options, better nutrient capture and stronger soil protection. Cover crops are a natural sequel to corn silage. Bryan Overstreet , Purdue University Extension and conservation agronomist with the Conservation Cropping Systems Initiative
(CCSI), says planting after silage opens a fall window long enough to establish a cover crop and gives producers several species options. The selector tool helps narrow down species that reliably fit your geography and planting window. “What may work in southern Indiana may not work in northern Indiana,” Overstreet says. “Southern Indiana has a several‑weeks‑longer growing season for silage.” Depending on location and how early silage comes off, producers often have more cover‑crop options than they would after grain harvest. Overstreet notes that drilling seed after silage provides better seed‑to‑soil contact and more reliable establishment. Flying seed on ahead of chopping can add a week or two of growth, but standing corn is usually green enough that getting seed down to the soil remains a challenge. Planting date largely determines how much choice you have. Earlier establishment allows more fall growth, stronger root structure and the ability to diversify a mix. After grain harvest, options narrow –
in many cases, cereal rye is the only species that fits the window. Advertisement A well‑established cover crop may also support late‑fall, winter or early‑spring grazing, depending on species. In dairy systems, planting after silage creates a timely place to apply manure in the fall. In the parts of Indiana where Overstreet works, producers often plant cereal rye and later chop it for ryelage, removing additional nutrients and making room for another manure application without pushing soil levels too high. Hairy vetch in full bloom adds nitrogen to the soil and enhances biodiversity in a cover‑crop mix. Image provided by Nathan Choat. “It can be a challenge to keep soil nutrient levels down, especially close to the barns,” he says. Matt Ruark, University of Wisconsin –
Madison Department of Soil and Environmental Sciences, focuses on nutrient management and soil fertility, with research centered on nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) use in Wisconsin’s major crops. He notes that corn silage leaves fields vulnerable to erosion, and many farmers are turning to cover crops to keep soil protected, improve water quality and build long‑term soil health. Ruark’s research has examined manure application after silage harvest followed by cover‑crop establishment. His team found that only modest biomass is needed for soil protection –
roughly 1,000 pounds of dry matter per acre, or about 8 inches of growth. Once biomass exceeds 2,000 pounds per acre (around 16 inches), the cover crop begins to use N that would otherwise be available to the next crop. Between 1,000 and 2,000 pounds of biomass, N availability can drop by about 35%; above 2,000 pounds, reductions can reach 50% to 60%. Although that N isn’t released quickly enough for the following corn crop, Ruark’s preliminary findings show it remains in the soil system. Slow decomposition contributes to organic matter over time, and consistent use of cover crops helps retain N in the soil profile. When cover crops are grazed, the return of manure and urine adds further benefit. Winter rye remains the most popular cover crop in Wisconsin because it reliably survives winter. Oats and spring barley also perform well when planted early, though they winterkill. Ruark’s research shows winter-killed species don’t create negative nutrient effects, but they may not produce enough spring biomass for erosion control. Many dairies face pressure to reduce erosion and N leaching, and winter rye fits that need well. “You don’t get the nitrogen back right away,” Ruark says. “Only through continued use of cover crops will it help build organic matter and nitrogen in the soil. Producers who stay with cover crops over multiple seasons typically refine their management and see greater benefits with fewer drawbacks.” Winter rye cover crop grows in uniform rows after corn silage harvest, protecting soil and reducing erosion through the fall and spring seasons.
Image provided by Matthew Ruark. Cover crops aren’t new, but recent research and on‑farm experience have sharpened how producers use them. Overstreet points out that the concept goes back generations; in fact, he found notes from his grandfather’s agronomy classes at Purdue University from 1932 to 1933 describing green manure crops used to return nutrients to the soil. Early agronomists understood the value of adding organic material, even if they weren’t yet sure how commercial fertilizers and crop rotations would affect long‑term soil health. Today, the consequences of those decades are clearer. Many fields have lost organic matter, and erosion remains a persistent challenge. Overstreet emphasizes that rebuilding soil biology requires feeding the microbial community and reducing disturbance. Cover crops supply the sugars and root exudates microbes depend on, while reduced tillage protects their habitat. He explains that long gaps between crops can leave soil biology without a food source. When corn silage is chopped in August and nothing is planted until the following spring, microbial activity drops sharply. Spring tillage then disrupts what remains. Adding a cover crop after silage keeps living roots in the soil and supports microbial processes that contribute to healthier, more resilient ground. “It’s a cyclical thing; once we get the nutrients back to the soil, it builds on itself,” Overstreet says. A cool‑season grazing mix combines broadleaf brassicas and grasses, providing fall forage while improving soil structure and nutrient cycling. Image provided by Nathan Choat. What to plant Nathan Choat, sales representative for Green Cover in south‑central Nebraska and Kansas, says conversations about cover crops always start with context and goals. There’s no single formula; species and mixes depend on what the producer wants to accomplish. For many operations, the most common goal is forage production. After corn silage, winter triticale or cereal rye are frequently drilled and then chopped in spring –
a silage‑on‑silage rotation. Legumes such as winter peas or hairy vetch can be added to those grasses for additional nitrogen contribution. If the goal is fall or winter grazing, similar species work, with wheat or barley added depending on location and remaining growing season. When planting after silage specifically for fall and winter grazing, the window may allow cool‑season spring annuals that tolerate frost but don’t survive winter –
oats, spring barley, spring peas or spring clovers. These can provide quick forage growth before freeze‑up. Brassicas –
radishes, turnips, collards, kale, rapeseed –
are another option. They’re typically used at low rates (around 2 pounds per acre) alongside grasses to create a balanced mix. Brassicas and grasses excel at nutrient scavenging, capturing leftover fertility that might otherwise leach and contribute to nitrate issues. Holding that nitrogen in plant biomass preserves it for the next growing season. Compaction is another challenge, especially when silage is taken off wet ground. Heavy equipment limits air movement in the soil, restricts root depth and slows microbial activity. Brassicas can help open compacted layers and improve root penetration. Diverse mixes allow producers to target multiple goals at once –  N capture, compaction relief, forage production and grazing. Choat notes that diverse blends aren’t necessarily more expensive. Seeding rates are adjusted downward for each species, and small‑seeded crops like radish contribute significant function at very low cost. “People often think diverse mixes are more expensive, but they are probably cheaper,” adds Choat.
