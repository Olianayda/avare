Trade war with Canada escalates, dairy whey imports banned When Canada’s retaliatory tariffs went into effect on Sept. 8, President Donald Trump pushed back harder, enacting a ban on several goods from Canada. Trade issues between the U.S. and Canada started back in July when the U.S. chose not to renew the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA). Later that month, Trump issued three proclamations pursuant to Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, declaring Canada unfairly discriminated against American autos, alcohol and dairy products. An additional 50% tariffs went into effect on Aug. 22 for these goods. Canada retaliated dollar for dollar on Sept. 8 with targeted tariff protection on the industries exposed to the new U.S. tariffs, including steel, aluminum, cheese and farm equipment. Trump’s response went beyond tariffs to ban some dairy products including whey, various Canadian wines and spirits, and motorcycles and mopeds. These bans are set to take effect on Sept. 29. Advertisement The International Dairy Foods Association (IDFA) is urging U.S. and Canadian officials to return to constructive negotiations to resolve long-standing dairy trade concerns. A statement from IDFA reads, “In response to the President’s executive order banning whey and modified whey imports from Canada effective Sept. 29, IDFA reiterates its strong support for resolving long-standing U.S. dairy trade concerns through meaningful negotiations. The United States imported more than 35 million dollars in whey products from Canada last year. While the concerns about Canadian protein pricing policies continue to remain unresolved, an import ban will not address these underlying issues.” Dairy Farmers of Canada also released a statement. It reads: “The continued escalation of trade actions, including a ban on certain Canadian dairy exports and additional tariffs on others, is deeply concerning for Canada’s dairy sector. As determined by the most recent [USMCA] dispute settlement panel, Canada is currently respecting its dairy trade commitments with the U.S. Unwarranted tariff measures and import bans only serve to create further uncertainty for farmers, processors and businesses on both sides of the border.” U.S. dairy companies, cooperatives and farmer organizations urge Congress to reject S. 5026 The National Milk Producers Federation (NMPF) and IDFA joined by 76 dairy companies, cooperatives, and state and regional producer organizations representing the full spectrum of the U.S. dairy industry, urged congressional leaders to reject S. 5026, the Childhood Diabetes Reduction Act of 2026, warning that the legislation could confuse consumers about the health and nutritional benefits of dairy foods and negatively affect dairy farmers, manufacturers and businesses throughout the dairy supply chain. S. 5026 would establish a broad definition of “ultra-processed foods” and require front-of-package warning labels on products captured by that definition indicating their consumption may be linked to childhood obesity and diabetes. Under the legislation, those warning labels could appear on a wide range of nutrient-dense dairy foods, including whole milk, cheese, most yogurts and other cultured dairy products. In a letter sent to Senate Majority Leader John Thune, Senate Minority Leader Charles Schumer, House Speaker Mike Johnson and House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, the dairy industry expressed shared support for efforts to improve the health of Americans and reduce chronic diet-related disease, while cautioning that nutrition policy must be grounded in sound science and provide clear, consistent information to consumers. “Nutrition policy should help Americans make better-informed choices, not create confusion by placing sweeping warning labels on foods that are an important part of healthy dietary patterns,” said Michael Dykes, DVM, president and CEO of IDFA. “S. 5026 relies on overly broad definitions of ‘ultra-processed foods’ and ‘nutrients of concern’ that fail to account for the nutritional value of individual foods or their role in the overall diet. Congress should reject this approach and ensure federal nutrition policy remains grounded in sound science and provides consumers with clear, meaningful information.” “Whole milk, cheese and yogurt are staples of a healthy diet, not foods that should carry a warning label,” said Gregg Doud, president and CEO of NMPF. “Dairy farmers work hard every day to put nutrient-dense food on America’s tables. S. 5026 gets the science wrong, lumping wholesome dairy foods in with products that have nothing in common with them nutritionally. That’s not informing consumers, it’s misleading them about foods nutrition experts say Americans need more of, not less. Congress should reject this bill and work toward nutrition policy that actually reflects the evidence.” The signatories to the letter span the U.S. dairy supply chain, including dairy cooperatives, processors, and state and regional producer organizations. “The dairy industry is united in opposition to S. 5026,” the organizations wrote. “We respectfully urge congressional leaders to carefully consider the negative consequences this legislation could have for American consumers, dairy farmers and businesses, and the rural communities where dairy foods are produced and manufactured.” Read the full dairy industry letter here . Contract ends worker strike at Foremost Farms in Reedsburg More than 40 Teamsters at Foremost Farms in Reedsburg, Wisconsin, overwhelmingly ratified a new four-year contract. The agreement delivers a 27% wage increase, improved benefits, stronger workplace protections and additional gains for members at the dairy cooperative’s butter production facility. The agreement also ended a strike that began in late August.
