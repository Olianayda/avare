Now little more than a blackened skeleton of metal, it stands as a reminder of someone’s bad day in the field this summer. Email Cassidy Woolsey I don’t know what happened that day, but I do know that most bad days in agriculture don’t start out looking like bad days. Just the other day, my little family set out to get some cows back in that had been spotted by the highway. Despite the circumstances and lack of backup, the chore went pretty smoothly. My crystal ball for the day pictured cows scattered across the road in every direction, cars stopping and a few birds (not the feathered kind) – but luckily, that didn’t happen. We spent the next good portion of the day searching for their escape spot and repairing the fence to keep it from happening again. Once that figurative “fire” was put out, we started home, stopping along the way to check on another group of cows about an hour away. The main concern here was water, and it didn’t take long before things went from bad to worse. When we got to the spring, barely a trickle of water was flowing, and all we could see was a lame bull, no cows and no calves. Advertisement My husband walked a few yards through the trees to an old water holding tank made of a rusted-out culvert pipe, hanging on by a few bolts. There was indeed water there, but also a nice-sized calf who appeared to have been stuck in 3 feet of water for a few days. With some wiggling of the culvert, we were able to get it down enough on one side to push the calf out. It stumbled around before collapsing, exhausted and cold from the spring water and mud. Of course, where it collapsed was an old fenceline, so it tangled itself in the wire. (Eye roll.) Now back to the missing cows … and the other calves. We found them – well, most of them – but they weren’t in our lease. No, they were scattered and mixed in with the neighbor’s cows, a mess that likely won't be sorted out until gathering this fall. As my husband called his dad to fill him in on the different situations (plural), I expected a worried tone, an angry tone or at least some sort of “woe is me.” But instead, he remained cool, calm and collected. He wasn't rattled by the situation but instead took in the information and started making decisions about how to handle it tomorrow. I recently heard this analogy: If you were asked to put all your problems into a hat and then pull one back out, would you risk grabbing someone else’s problems instead of your own? I know I would take my own problems back.
