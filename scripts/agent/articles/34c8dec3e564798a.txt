I was elbow-deep in a difficult calving, nearly 45 minutes into what felt like an impossible delivery. A longtime friend watched me with a coffee cup in his hand, calmly offering advice while I struggled to straighten legs and align the calf’s head into the birth canal. I am sure he had been in this same situation dozens of times before – a young cow trying to deliver a calf that simply didn’t fit. It’s in those moments that you wish you hadn’t been the veterinarian on call that night. Thankfully, the calf survived. A few days later, I watched him nursing contentedly beside that young cow, and I couldn’t help but think back to what that producer had said. He was right. Advertisement Every decision we make as cattle producers is an attempt to manage risk. We vaccinate because disease is risk. We develop nutrition programs because poor performance is risk. We build fences, monitor water systems and prepare for changing weather because we know challenges are inevitable. Genetic selection is no different. For decades, we’ve selected cattle for growth, fertility, carcass quality and maternal performance. Those traits have transformed the beef industry. Today, however, genomics gives us an opportunity to select for something even more fundamental: cattle that are better equipped to remain healthy throughout their lives. The goal isn’t to produce perfect cattle. Biology doesn’t work that way. The goal is to stack the odds in our favor, one generation at a time. Healthy herds begin with healthy cows When people think about herd health, they often think about vaccines or treatment protocols. As a veterinarian, I certainly appreciate those tools. But long before a syringe ever comes out of the refrigerator, herd health begins with the cow herd itself. A productive cow is remarkably resilient. She breeds back consistently, calves with minimal assistance, mothers her calf, produces adequate milk, remains structurally sound and continues doing those things year after year. Those are clearly health indicators. Selecting for calving ease reduces the risk of dystocia and improves calf vigor. Fertility and heifer pregnancy traits increase the likelihood that females remain productive in the herd. Stayability or longevity helps identify cows that continue raising calves over many years. Structural soundness and moderate mature size allow cows to remain functional under commercial conditions rather than leaving the herd prematurely. Individually, these traits may seem unrelated. Together, they describe a cow that handles stress well, remains productive longer and consistently weans a healthy calf. In many ways, selecting better cows is one of the most effective health programs a producer can implement. Building disease resilience Even with exceptional management and productive cows, disease challenges will always exist. Few illustrate that better than bovine respiratory disease (BRD). Despite decades of improvements in vaccination, nutrition and cattle handling, BRD remains one of the costliest diseases affecting the beef industry. Much of its economic impact comes not only from calves that require treatment but also from those with subclinical disease that quietly lose performance without ever being recognized. Research suggests BRD has a heritability of approximately 12% to 18%. That doesn’t mean genetics determines whether an individual calf becomes sick. It does mean genetics influences susceptibility enough that selection can make measurable progress over time. Modern genomic evaluations allow producers to identify cattle with a lower genetic predisposition for BRD. Combined with thoughtful management, those decisions can gradually build cattle that are more resilient when inevitable disease challenges arise. Genetics doesn’t replace good stockmanship. It makes good management more effective. Preparing for emerging challenges The same principle applies to conditions such as bovine congestive heart failure (BCHF). Although researchers are still working to understand every factor contributing to BCHF, genetics clearly appears to influence risk. Environmental conditions, nutrition and management remain important, but genomic evaluations now give producers another way to reduce the likelihood of propagating susceptibility within a herd. Rather than reacting after losses occur, producers can make breeding decisions that gradually move the population toward greater resilience. That’s the long game of genetic improvement. Progress isn’t measured in a single calf crop. It’s measured across generations. Looking beyond individual traits One of the most exciting developments in beef genomics isn’t any single trait; it’s our growing ability to evaluate the whole animal. Rather than chasing one number, producers can balance calving ease, fertility, maternal longevity, growth, carcass merit and health traits to build cattle that fit their environment and production goals. That’s important because healthy cattle aren’t defined by a single genomic score. They’re cows that breed back under challenging conditions. They’re calves born without assistance. They’re feeder cattle that stay healthy enough to express their genetic potential. They’re females that remain productive for years instead of leaving the herd early. When we begin thinking about genetics this way, genomics becomes much more than a selection tool. It becomes another management tool – one that starts working long before a calf ever reaches the ground. We may never eliminate every disease. We may never remove every environmental challenge. Good nutrition, sound management and a strong veterinary relationship will always be the foundation of herd health. But genomics allows us to make breeding decisions today that improve the probability of healthier cattle tomorrow. Every generation gives us another opportunity to stack the odds a little more in our favor. Because in the end, my producer friend was right: Everything starts with a live calf.
