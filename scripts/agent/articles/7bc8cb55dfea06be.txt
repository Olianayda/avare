How is that possible? When the big-picture items like herd size and milk production are nearly identical, a gap that wide has to be an error somewhere in the numbers, right? It would be easy to look for a major area of concern that needs immediate attention. In reality, a discrepancy this wide isn't necessarily a reflection of poor cow management. Instead, it is usually a classic case of missing the structural ground rules of the benchmark data. The impact of cash vs. accrual accounting The first step to a reliable and valuable set of peer benchmark data is ensuring that the numbers are coming from the same accounting method. A quick comparison within a peer group is often done based off cash accounting methods or the raw tax records. Cash accounting will ignore changes in inventories, payables and receivables that can make a major impact on the numbers producers are looking at. For example, Farmer A may have $50,000 in prepaid feed that was purchased in December for the next year, while Farmer B has no prepaids. In theory, when comparing their December financials, Farmer A will look less profitable, while in reality the feed expense should truly be recognized as a part of the next year’s feed bill. True financial benchmarking must use accrual-adjusted records to reflect the true cost of producing milk within that specific window of time. Accrual-adjusted records help provide consistency and power behind the numbers for you to truly be able to make decisions based upon them. To find the numbers that drive profitability and make benchmarking actionable, producers must look beyond the surface numbers and understand the structural differences that impact peer-group data. Line items vs. the full picture Financial benchmarking can be done with farms of all shapes and sizes. Some may depend on purchasing all their forages, therefore having lower capital cost. Some may have a large land base and a new 2026 implement that, while it looks beautiful, carries a significant financing commitment. Producer A may pay himself a very comfortable wage while Producer B prefers to keep the money in the farm checking and pay himself just enough to support the bare essentials. These differences between you and your peers may swing individual line items, but the focus should rely on the overall cost of production. Let’s look at real-life examples and some of the ranges seen in typical financial benchmark data. In 2025, many farms saw a total cost of production range from the low $13 range up to $17 on an energy-corrected basis. There are various factors that play into this cost of production. On one hand, cattle prices continue to hold strong, creating a great outside revenue source for producers. Additionally, average feed cost per hundredweight (cwt) decreased from 2024 in many herds. This decrease could be anywhere from 10 cents per cwt all the way to 70 cents per cwt. We saw some herds able to get their feed cost down to $8.35 and lower while others exceeded $10. Several of these herds on the higher end, however, save on their capital cost and limited repairs as they purchase or custom harvest all their crops. As costs continue to rise, it is pivotal to remember that your operation is your   operation and what works for you may not work for your neighbor and vice versa. It is crucial to know what you do that may be different and how it impacts not just the line items but the bottom line as a whole. The rebuilt tractor mindset Think of your cost of production like rebuilding an old tractor. If you look at the invoice for a new fuel injection pump alone, that cost may look pretty ugly. In fact, it may be enough to make you question the whole project. But when that tractor starts on the first turn and you burn 20% less diesel, the fuel pump starts to make more sense. Financial benchmarking works in a similar way. You cannot judge the tractor by the price of a single part. One producer may have an ugly capital cost line because they recently upgraded to newer machinery, but in turn, their repairs and maintenance has decreased significantly.
