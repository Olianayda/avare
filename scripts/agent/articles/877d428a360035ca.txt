We focus on improving our relationship and mutual trust with family and friends, but it is easy to forget that the supervisor-employee relationship is also an interpersonal relationship that similarly benefits from increased trust. Bob Milligan Professor Emeritus / Cornell University Email Bob Milligan Related Tools to build trust on your farm Tools to build trust on your farm 10 tips to enrich your dairy’s culture I am often asked how age differences between supervisors and employees affects supervision and the development of trust. There are three common age-related scenarios.
Younger supervisor: This often causes stress and a lack of self-confidence in the younger supervisor. In this situation, unfortunately, the younger supervisor often focuses on proving themselves. A more productive approach is to view the older employees as a resource to help the supervisor succeed. Respecting the older employees builds the relationship, increases the confidence of the young supervisor and builds the team.
Similar age: The challenge here is that the employees supervised are often close friends with the supervisor. Staying friends is good; however, the supervisor must be clear that the work relationship must be different from the friend relationship. This may require frank discussions to be clear about the work relationship. The supervisor must also be careful not to favor his or her friends.
Older supervisor: This is the most common scenario. The older supervisor must be aware that younger generations have less respect for authority. Providing clear expectations and quality feedback will be much more effective than a traditional command and control approach.
We now discuss 10 tips to increase mutual trust in all relationships. The increased trust will lead to increased productivity, motivation and retention.  1. Active listening Answer this question: What is communication? Typically, when considering this question, we think about talking. However, listening is often as or more important. This is especially true for you as a supervisor because you are in the power position in the employee-supervisor relationship, meaning the employee may be timid in coming to you. When you do not listen, you send a message that you are not interested in what they have to say or even in the employee as a person. What does active listening” mean? Active listening means you are listening with all your senses. You are listening to understand both the message that is being delivered and the underlying emotions. All senses are important because the emotion/feeling part of the message is often in the voice tone and body language. Advertisement 2. Ask questions Think about a time when you had something to say but were not encouraged or even allowed to share. This is a position common to employees. The best way to involve them is to ask questions. Be certain to give them sufficient time to respond.  Two questions that I and others have found to be effective:
What is going well?
What could be going better?
3. ’Tell me more’ Employees often have issues, ideas and questions. Even when you ask questions and actively listen to get them to open up, they seldom provide all their thoughts. When responding before you have all the available information, you can send the conversation in the wrong direction. To be certain you have everything, ask “tell me more” or another variation, “and what else.” I have found these questions almost always yield additional valuable information.  4. Circle back You often have good, sometimes emotional, discussions with your employees. After intense discussions, we often have ideas, thoughts and even concerns. In these situations, it is good to circle back in a day or two to check in. The purpose is not to restart the conversation but rather to see if the employee has any unresolved thoughts or issues. You can simply say something like: Is there anything you would like to discuss further? If the answer is no, wish them a good day. If yes, discuss. 5. Improve decision-making When an employee comes to you with a question about how to do a task, it is easy to just answer the question. Often, however, this should be viewed as an opportunity to improve the decision-making ability of the employee. Ask the employee how he or she would answer that question. You are providing an opportunity to think, which improves decision-making. The other big advantage is that next time the employee may not have to come to you. Supervisors are often reluctant to ask this question, fearing that the employee will not have an answer. To me, that is not a problem. Even when they do not answer, you have provided an opportunity for them to consider and think. Perhaps next time they will have an answer. 6. Small steps Speaking of learning and growing, I often hear supervisors argue that their employees do not want more responsibility even when offered. Research into human behavior, however, shows that part of human nature is to grow. Why this seeming contradiction? The answer is that we humans also fear failure. I often equate this to a teeter-totter. On one side is the desire to accept the additional responsibility for growth. On the other side is the fear of failure. Two ideas to reduce fear. First, offer new responsibility in small steps. I often say to my clients: You cannot get to Z without going through A, B, C, D, E and F. Second, suggest they develop ideas on how they would manage the new responsibility – a draft. You can then make suggestions to increase their confidence and reduce fear. 7. Follow-up alert Many supervisors correctly worry about being accused of micromanaging when they follow up after training or redirection feedback. To avoid this concern, inform the employee in advance that you will be following up to answer questions or provide anything else they need. The follow-up will then be expected and will not be perceived as micromanaging. 8. Clear expectations Think about a time when you were unclear about what was expected of you. I suspect you were frustrated and potentially lost trust with the person not providing clarity. You do not want your employees to be in this situation. You must always provide clear expectations for behavior and performance.  9. Quality feedback  We have often talked about the three types of feedback – positive, redirection and negative. Effective use of all three will enhance your effectiveness and the effectiveness of the first eight tips. High-quality, specific, positive feedback enhances the employee’s confidence and desire to continue to excel. Redirection feedback, also referred to as learning, increases productivity and the employee’s probability of meeting and exceeding expectations. Negative feedback, hopefully needed sparingly, is best delivered as a choice to meet expectations or incur a consequence rather than as a reprimand.  10. Lead by example The previous nine tips involved your interaction with your employees. This final tip relates to the behaviors and actions you perform that your employees observe. It is crucial that you “practice what you preach” or “walk the talk.” When your actions deviate from the expectations you have of your employees, you lose their trust and your effectiveness in using the first nine tips diminishes.
