More than a field: SwissLane Farms establishes memorial soccer tournament to honor the memory of Sebastian Szymanski Audrey Schmitz September 14, 2026 When Sebastian Szymanski started working at SwissLane Farms in Alto, Michigan, at 16 years old, he began by sweeping floors in the shop. Seven years later, he had become the dairy’s full-time hoof trimmer. However, his life and work at the dairy were cut unexpectedly short when he passed away on his 23rd birthday, leaving behind co-workers, friends and family. We have 90 employees, and 62 of those shirts were at his funeral. Most of those employees who attended barely speak English. Yet somehow, he was able to bridge that language barrier.” Szymanski loved hunting, fishing and soccer. He was the kind of person who wanted to be around others and was always finding something to do with his co-workers. He played soccer in high school, coached youth soccer and participated in league games. Eventually, soccer found its way onto the dairy, and pick-up games would be played behind the dairy barns.  “He always wanted to have other teams from other farms come and play against them, but they never quite made that happen while he was alive,” Oesch says. Advertisement After Szymanski’s death, SwissLane Farms decided to make that vision a reality. The dairy built the Sebastian Szymanski Memorial Field and hosted its inaugural memorial soccer tournament, bringing together SwissLane employees and their families, neighboring dairy farms, Szymanski’s former teammates and members of the local community. Sebastian's former high school soccer team, Lowell High School, won the championship game.  Image courtesy of Alltech. Building a legacy For SwissLane Farms, the decision to build a soccer field was more than creating a place to play, it was about strengthening employee culture and community. The fourth-generation dairy, operated by Oesch, his brother Matt and cousin Annie, has grown from about 2,100 cows in 2012 to 5,600 cows milked across three sites today. With a workforce representing a variety of cultural backgrounds, Oesch says creating opportunities for employees to connect and build community is essential. “We were looking for a way to help our people build community, so we started talking about different ways to do that,” Oesch says. “Transportation is a big deal, so we needed something in close proximity to where they live. We house a lot of them, so we kicked around the idea of making a more official soccer field for them to play on, something better than just the rough yard in the back of the dairy.” When Szymanski passed away, the idea took on a deeper meaning. “When Sebastian passed, we thought, ‘All right, let’s go all in on this soccer field and honor him with it,’” Oesch says. What started as an idea to spend a few thousand dollars on a basic field eventually became a significant investment. SwissLane brought in topsoil, installed underground irrigation and purchased tournament-size soccer goals. The field was tilled, seeded and fertilized, with employees and members of the Oesch family helping prepare the ground. The dairy’s leadership team initially authorized about $10,000 for the project. By completion, SwissLane had invested roughly $50,000. “It was a total team effort to get it done,” Oesch says. Making the field permanent was intentional. Oesch believes a one-time event would not have been enough to preserve Szymanski’s memory. “I’ve heard it said, and I think it’s true: People are more afraid of being forgotten than they are of death itself. People who have lost loved ones, especially family members too early, don’t want their loved ones to be forgotten,” Oesch says. “A one-time tournament doesn’t cover that. A memorial field is more permanent, and we don’t want to forget him either.” That permanence took on special meaning during the field’s dedication. For the ribbon-cutting ceremony, members of his family used one of Szymanski’s hoof-trimming tools to cut the string, a fitting tribute to the work he had grown into at SwissLane. From left to right: Uriel Perez, a hoof trimmer whom Sebastian worked alongside every day; his sister, Avery Szymanski; brother, Carter Szymanski; mother, Chastity Szymanski; and father, Chris Szymanski, participate in the ribbon-cutting ceremony.  Image courtesy of Alltech. A day of community The inaugural tournament quickly became more than just a soccer competition. SwissLane employees brought their families and spent the day playing and cheering alongside one another. “All of their families were there. We had a bounce house, ice cream and a food truck, along with other things for the kids to do,” Oesch says. “There were a lot of smiles, laughter and joy.” The tournament also drew support from SwissLane’s industry partners. GPS Dairy and Alltech helped support the dairy’s employee culture efforts, while AIS Equipment provided a hospitality trailer and cooked burgers for the crowd. “What made this tournament especially meaningful was the purpose behind where everyone was gathering,” says Jorge Delgado, talent development and retention program specialist for Alltech. “It wasn't simply a field where we happened to play soccer; it was a place connected to Sebastian's memory. That gave the day a deeper meaning.”  Neighboring dairy farms also brought teams to compete, with six teams participating in total. SwissLane Farms, Spring Creek Farms, Chase Crest Hill Farms, Crossroads Dairy and Willow Point Dairy were among those represented. Two soccer fields were used at the same time, with referees brought in to keep the competition running smoothly. “It was really awesome to see that kind of support from the neighboring farms,” Oesch says. “It meant a lot to us, and it was a better day than I could have imagined.” Tommy Oesch shares a message alongside Impact Church Executive Pastor Ryan Kresge (right) and Maria Calderon, translator (left).  Image courtesy of Alltech. A lasting place Looking ahead, Oesch hopes the field becomes a gathering place not only for SwissLane employees, but potentially for local teams and youth programs. “You can’t bring a body back to life, but the spirit of a person can live on through a place and an event,” Oesch says. “I just hope it’s a place where laughter and fun are shared for a really long time.” For SwissLane, that legacy also reflects a broader belief about what it means to build a dairy business. “If you want to keep good talent long term, you have to provide more than a paycheck,” Oesch says. “The paycheck is important, but you also have to provide an opportunity for people to build community. That’s how they become invested in your business.” “I hope farms see that building a strong workplace culture doesn't always have to happen inside the workplace. SwissLane's example shows that you can honor the impact an employee had while creating something that continues bringing people together,” Delgado says. “When farms intentionally invest in their people and their families, they send a powerful message: You are important to this farm, and you are part of this community. That's the kind of message that can have an impact long after the event is over.” The memorial field stands as a lasting reminder of Szymanski and the connections he built, representing something greater than soccer or employee culture for Oesch. “At the end of the day, there’s a lot more than money that we’re doing this dairy farming thing for,” Oesch says. “You have to lean into some of those things to have a full life, I think.
