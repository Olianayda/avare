Athena Angela Gaffud October 6, 2026 One of the most common questions after calving is whether a cow's uterine discharge is part of a normal recovery or a sign of infection. Two conditions often mentioned are metritis and endometritis. Although the names sound similar, they are not the same disease, and knowing the difference helps producers decide when a cow needs prompt veterinary attention. The easiest way to remember them is this: Metritis happens early and affects all layers of the uterine wall. Endometritis happens later, affects only the inner lining of the uterus and impacts fertility more than the cow’s overall health. When does each condition occur? Metritis typically develops within the first 21 days after calving, most commonly during the first 10 days. It is a deep infection involving the entire uterine wall. Endometritis usually develops more than 21 days after calving. The inflammation is limited to the inner uterine lining (endometrium), and affected cows often appear healthy even though the uterus has not fully recovered. Timing is often the first clue when evaluating a fresh cow. Advertisement What signs should you watch for? Metritis: The cow looks sick Metritis can range from moderate to severe, but the most serious cases, known as puerperal metritis, cause obvious illness ( Table 1 ). Watch for:
Foul-smelling, watery, reddish-brown uterine discharge
High fever (103.5°F or higher)
Reduced appetite
Sudden drop in milk production
Depression or reluctance to move
Some cows develop clinical metritis, where the foul-smelling discharge is present but the cow does not have a high fever or obvious signs of systemic illness. Even though these cows appear less affected, they still require veterinary attention to help prevent future reproductive problems. Endometritis: The cow looks healthy, but fertility suffers Unlike metritis, cows with endometritis usually continue eating, milking and behaving normally. The biggest problem is reduced reproductive performance. Watch for:
Thick white, yellow or pus-like discharge from the vulva several weeks after calving
Delayed breeding
Repeat breeding
Poor conception despite appearing otherwise healthy
Some cows have subclinical endometritis, meaning there is no visible discharge at all. These silent infections are often discovered only during routine reproductive examinations or after repeated breeding failures. Which cows are at greater risk? Pay extra attention to cows that have experienced:
Retained placenta
Difficult calving (dystocia)
Twin pregnancies
Milk fever or ketosis
Reduced feed intake after calving
These cows have a higher risk of developing postpartum uterine disease and deserve closer monitoring during the fresh-cow period. What should you do? If a recently calved cow develops a foul-smelling discharge together with fever, poor appetite or a sudden drop in milk production, contact your herd veterinarian immediately. Early diagnosis and treatment improve recovery and help reduce production losses. If a cow appears healthy but still has abnormal discharge more than three weeks after calving or is repeating after breeding despite looking normal, schedule a reproductive examination. During routine herd health visits, your veterinarian may use tools such as a Metricheck device, vaginoscopy or uterine cytology to detect infections that cannot be identified through routine observation alone. Prevention starts before calving The best way to reduce uterine infections is to support cows through a successful transition period. Focus on these four areas:
Provide a clean, dry calving environment   to reduce bacterial contamination.
Support transition-cow nutrition   to maximize dry matter intake and strengthen immune function.
Minimize difficult calvings and practice good hygiene whenever assistance is needed.
Monitor fresh cows every day   for appetite, milk production, body temperature and abnormal uterine discharge.
Strong transition-cow management remains one of the most effective ways to protect both herd health and reproductive performance. The fresh-cow and pre-breeding checklist   When checking cows after calving, ask yourself: During the first three weeks after calving ☐ Does she have a foul-smelling, watery uterine discharge? ☐ Does she have a fever, reduced appetite or a sudden drop in milk production? ☐ Does she appear dull or reluctant to move? More than three weeks after calving ☐ Is thick white or yellow discharge visible on the vulva or tail? ☐ Is she repeating after breeding despite appearing healthy? ☐ Is she falling behind the herd's reproductive schedule? If you answer yes to any of these questions, involve your herd veterinarian. Early detection and treatment protect the cow's health, improve reproductive performance and help keep your breeding program on track.
