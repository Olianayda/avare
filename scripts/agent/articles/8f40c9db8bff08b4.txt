Important design elements to consider when retrofitting a barn for automated milking to maximize efficiency Antti Puuri September 24, 2026 Key design elements – such as layout, animal welfare features, handling systems and workflow planning – must be integrated to support both system performance and farm management when retrofitting a barn for automated milking. The increasing adoption of automated milking has reshaped farm operations, offering opportunities to improve labor efficiency, animal welfare and productivity. However, retrofitting existing barns for this use presents significant challenges. A common misconception is that installing milking robots alone will automatically lead to improved efficiency. In reality, these updated systems only achieve their full potential when supported by well-designed barn layouts and management practices. The limitations of technology-driven retrofitting A critical issue in many retrofitted barns is the tendency to introduce milking robots into existing layouts without rethinking the overall system. In such cases, they are often “dropped” into the barn, resulting in minimal improvements in labor efficiency or productivity. While they automate milking, many essential tasks – such as fetching cows, cleaning and animal handling – remain labor-intensive. In practice, this often leads to frustration among farmers who expect immediate improvements after installation. When the surrounding environment is not adapted to support the system, the anticipated reductions in labor and increases in productivity fail to materialize. This gap between expectations and actual outcomes highlights the importance of linking technological investments with appropriate design and management strategies. This points to an important principle: These systems do not independently solve challenges related to labor, animal welfare or farm management. Instead, outcomes depend on how effectively the barn is designed around them. Therefore, retrofitting must be approached as a system-level redesign rather than a simple technological upgrade. Advertisement Layout design and cow flow Efficient layout is essential for enabling smooth cow movement and minimizing labor requirements. Traditional barns often have structural limitations, such as narrow alleys, which disrupt cow traffic and increase handling time. Wide, open alleys (approximately 3 to 4.2 meters or 10 to 14 feet) are essential for allowing cows to move freely and access the milking robot without obstruction. Adequate space around it enables cows to approach and leave easily, reducing stress and increasing voluntary milking. Designing for cow flow is particularly important in automated milking systems, where voluntary movement plays a central role. Layouts must allow cows to move naturally between feeding, resting and milking areas without crowding or competition. Animal-centered design and welfare Animal welfare is a key determinant of both productivity and efficiency in barns using automated milking. Comfortable stalls with appropriate dimensions and soft bedding encourage adequate rest, which directly supports milk production and overall health. Open, dry alleys and proper drainage reduce the risk of lameness and improve hygiene. Additionally, features such as footbaths integrated into traffic routes support hoof health, which is essential for maintaining consistent visits to the milking robots. Special attention should be given to high-priority animal groups, such as fresh and transition cows. Providing dedicated areas – often referred to as “very important cow” pens – ensures better health outcomes and improved performance during critical production stages. Handling elements and labor efficiency Efficient handling systems are central to reducing labor demands in barns using automated milking. Key elements include fetch pens, handling chutes and functional gating systems. Fetch pens allow workers to manage cows that do not voluntarily visit the unit, significantly reducing time spent locating and moving animals. Handling chutes provide a safe and efficient space for routine procedures, such as health checks and treatments. Functional gating systems enable the movement of individual cows or small groups, minimizing the need for multiple workers and reducing labor intensity. These features demonstrate that labor efficiency is closely tied to how easily animals can be managed within the barn environment. The effectiveness of these elements also depends on how easily they can be used during daily routines. Poorly designed handling areas can slow down work and increase physical strain on workers, reducing overall efficiency. In contrast, well-integrated systems allow tasks to be carried out quickly and safely, supporting both labor productivity and animal welfare. Designing work routines before structures One of the most important insights from recent research is that barn design must be based on planned work routines. Designing a barn without considering how daily tasks will be performed often leads to unnecessary work and increased labor demands. Work processes such as cow fetching, sorting and treatment must be mapped out before finalizing the layout. This ensures that the barn supports efficient workflows rather than creating obstacles. As highlighted in our studies, “You do not just design a barn – you design your everyday work.” This perspective shifts the focus from physical structures to operational functionality, highlighting that efficiency results from the interaction between design and management. Variability in labor efficiency Findings from our farm study highlight the importance of design, with observed variation in labor efficiency ranging from approximately 1.8 to 14.6 minutes per cow per day. This variation cannot be explained by technology alone, as similar systems are used across farms. Instead, differences in barn layout, cow flow and management practices are the primary factors influencing performance. Poorly designed retrofits often fail to reduce workload, while well-designed systems can significantly improve efficiency and profitability. Conclusion Improved efficiency requires a well-planned remodeling of the barn environment and work routines. Key design elements – including layout, animal welfare features, handling systems and workflow planning – must be integrated to support both system performance and farm management. Ultimately, success depends not on the technology itself, but on how effectively it is integrated into a well-designed and carefully managed barn system. This underscores the need to approach retrofitting as an overall design challenge, where technology, animal needs and human labor are brought together to achieve optimal outcomes.
