Home &raquo; Progressive Dairy &raquo; Using fresh cow data to target reproductive management decisions Fresh Cows Using fresh cow data to target reproductive management decisions Tony Bruinje September 17, 2026 Fresh cow health records and heat activity during the voluntary waiting period (VWP) can help identify cows with different reproductive potentials to optimize the use of activity monitors and synchronization. Reproductive efficiency continues to be a major contributor to herd performance and profitability. Cows that become pregnant too late will spend more time in lower-producing stages of lactation that are less profitable. These extended lactations can cause an excessive gain in body condition before next calving, increasing transition health problems. Issues may include hypocalcemia, ketosis, retained placenta, metritis, endometritis, persistent purulent vaginal discharge or lameness. These problems can delay the resumption of ovarian cyclicity, reduce heat expression and impact conception rates. It is normal to see about 20% of cows failing to resume cyclicity by 60-70 days in milk (DIM) and about 30% of cows not being detected in heat in programs prioritizing heat detection by activity monitors for first breeding. Reproduction tools Timed synchronization protocols, such as Double Ovsynch, have been used successfully to reduce the dependence on heat detection and improve service and conception rates. They help ensure cows are inseminated despite delayed cyclicity or poor heat expression and create a predictable breeding schedule. However, these protocols require multiple hormone administrations, repeated cow handling and strong compliance with the protocol schedule. For example, missing 5% of cows or injections at any handling event during a Double Ovsynch may result in only 70% of successfully completed protocols. The adoption of automated activity monitors for heat detection can also contribute to improved pregnancy rates, especially when used in combination with synchronization and for early reinsemination of open cows. When cows are not synchronized during the first 21 days after the voluntary waiting period (VWP), about 70% of Holstein cows are detected after the VWP. This means that about 30% of cows go undetected and have delayed first insemination. These cows are often those that had transition health problems and delayed cyclicity. These cows with low reproductive potential would likely benefit the most from an early synchronization, such as Double Ovsynch, that aims to induce an ovulation before the breeding portion of the protocol. On the other hand, cows that have a healthy fresh period and resumed cyclicity early are better candidates for first breeding based on heat detection. The challenge is identifying these two groups of cows before the breeding period. Dairies recording fresh cow health events or using activity monitors have the potential to do that. Advertisement Identifying repro-ready cows In data from over 20 herds and 17,000 cows in different regions in the U.S., fewer cows with reproductive tract disease (dystocia, retained placenta, metritis, persistent purulent discharge) resumed cyclicity by 65 DIM (64%-68%) compared with healthy cows (75%-84%). In a study in Canada, fewer cows with reproductive tract disease, lameness or excessive body condition score loss were detected in heat during the breeding period (59%-65%) compared with healthy cows (73%-80%). Of those cows, 32% were healthy, and out of them, 82% were detected in heat. In contrast, 22% of cows had multiple health problems with only 58% of them detected in heat ( Figure 1 ).
This is where good records become important. Many farms already record calving difficulty, retained placenta and severe health problems that reduce intake, rumination and milk yield. However, some less-severe cases of diseases such as metritis and lameness are missed unless farms have a careful monitoring program. If these cows are not identified, they cannot be treated when appropriate or be classified based on their reproductive potential. Heat activity during VWP as a marker A practical source of information in herds using activity monitors is early heat activity during the VWP. A study with multiple herds with different activity monitoring systems showed that about two-thirds of cows had an early heat activity alert, and 88% of these cows were cycling. This means that heat activity during the VWP is a strong sign that the cow has resumed cyclicity and is ready for the breeding period. However, among cows without an early heat activity, only 35% were not cycling, meaning that some cows may be cycling but not showing enough activity to be detected. In a study with nearly 9,000 cows, cows that had an early heat activity alert were about three times more likely to be detected in heat during the breeding period (see Figure 1) and about two times more likely to be pregnant by 150 DIM compared to cows without an early heat activity. A few trials have tested targeted reproductive management approaches, in which cows are assigned to different protocols based on their early heat activity during the VWP. Cows with an early heat activity were classified as high reproductive potential and assigned to first insemination based on heat detection for 30 to 40 days, with noninseminated cows later synchronized. Cows without an early heat activity were classified as low reproductive potential and were synchronized either after a short period of heat detection or immediately after the VWP. In one herd in Minnesota, 85% to 88% of cows that had an early heat activity were also detected in heat for first insemination, with conception rates of 42% to 44%. In contrast, cows without an early heat activity had lower heat detection and conception rates when initially managed by heat detection but had acceptable performance when enrolled directly in Double Ovsynch. Another study in Mexico showed a similar pattern, with cows without an early heat activity having superior conception rates when managed by blanket synchronization. A third study in two herds in Florida tested a targeted approach in which cows with high-intensity early heat activity during the VWP were managed by heat detection for 42 days after the VWP, while cows without a high-intensity early heat were enrolled directly in Double Ovsynch. Compared with blanket synchronization of all cows, this targeted approach resulted in a greater pregnancy rate, fewer hormone treatments per insemination and $108 greater gross profit per cow. Utilizing available tools Heat activity during the VWP is currently the most studied and practical indicator for targeted reproductive management. Cows with an early heat activity could be good candidates for programs that prioritize heat detection for first insemination before using synchronization. Cows without early heat activity, or with low-intensity early heat, may be better candidates for enrolling directly in a Double Ovsynch protocol. Fresh cow health records can also help identify cows with different reproductive potentials, but more research is needed to test this approach based on fresh cow health. Reproductive management programs are still evolving and can be optimized according to each herd’s needs and goals. In herds with consistent fresh cow monitoring and activity monitors, using cow-level information to guide reproductive decisions may help maintain performance while reducing hormone use, improving labor efficiency and increasing the value of monitoring technologies already present on the farm. The primary goal of any reproductive management program should be to achieve the highest possible pregnancy rates to optimize productivity, replacement needs and beef-on-dairy revenue. Changes away from blanket synchronization should be done carefully and in consultation with the herd veterinarian or reproduction specialist.
