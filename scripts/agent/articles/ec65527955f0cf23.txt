Home &raquo; Progressive Dairy &raquo; Lifelong Learning: Building the future of dairy Lifelong Learning Lifelong Learning: Building the future of dairy September 15, 2026 As dairy farmers, we are always investing in the future. Whether we do that by improving a genetics program, adopting new technologies or building facilities, we’re always looking ahead. Those investments are all critical, though I’d contend that helping more young people discover a passion for dairy is even more important to the future of our farms and industry. Related Lifelong Learning: Building skills for the farm and beyond Lifelong Learning: Building a dairy’s culture and opportunities for team members Lifelong Learning: Building connections leads to learning opportunities With fewer kids growing up on farms, we need to find and inspire the next generation of dairy producers, veterinarians and industry professionals. That starts by giving young people opportunities to experience agriculture firsthand and learn from the people who live it every day. My husband, Paul, and I are involved in several programs that introduce young people to dairy and encourage them to learn about animals and our industry. In 2021, we helped start the St. Croix County Junior Holstein program. We took a lot of ideas from other established programs like the one in Dodge County where Paul grew up. It has been fun to watch the program grow in both numbers and impact. I’m continually impressed with the kids’ commitment as they carve time out of their busy school, activity and work schedules to spend time in a Sunday practice learning about cows. They ask questions, gain confidence and become excited about caring for and showing cattle. In addition to working with our Junior Holstein youth, we support similar programs, including the 4-H Heritage Calf Project, which helps young people start their own herds. We have also donated a calf to a regional newspaper giveaway, which is an annual program where youth ages 17 and under can win a registered dairy heifer calf. These are small investments that can make a big difference to young people. Advertisement Building for the future My own passion for the dairy industry brought me back as the fourth generation to my family’s dairy farm about nine years ago. Albedarned Dairy was named for Albe, my great-grandfather who founded the farm. My dad, Bruce Achterhof; and uncle, Joey Achterhof, own the farm and oversee fieldwork and grazing youngstock. Paul is our herdsman and manages genetics and I focus on human resources, finances, calves and fresh cows. My sister, Vanessa Gunderson, also works part time on the farm with the heifers and getting fieldwork done. In 2019, we moved the farm 20 miles to a new facility near Baldwin, Wisconsin, which allowed us to expand from about 250 cows to 450 cows with the same number of employees and improving efficiencies. We’re also working on building our own genetics program, and it has been exciting to see the genetic decisions we make come through in heifer performance and with successes in the show ring. We’re currently raising the fifth generation on the farm: our son, Maverick, is 19 months old – and our daughter, Cecilia, arrived in June. Watching our son get excited every time he sees a cow or tractor is the best thing ever and a great reminder of what matters most. Dairy farming is rewarding, but it is also a mental game. Every day can bring challenges that test your patience and resilience. When those days come, you have to remember why you do what you do. Sometimes that’s as simple as looking at a healthy newborn calf or watching a group of heifers you’ve worked hard to raise as they continue to thrive. Commitment to learning and connections Working with young people is a good reminder that learning never ends and can take many forms. I enjoy attending events like the Professional Dairy Producers (PDP) Business Conference and Calf Care Connection workshops, and I’ve found that some of the most valuable lessons happen between the official sessions. Conversations with fellow producers often provide new ideas or perspectives and have helped me build a network of people I can call with questions when I am working through a challenge. The PDP Mentor Program also deserves a mention; I participated in this program for three years while I was attending college. The opportunity to visit other dairy farms and learn from them was a valuable experience. When I returned to Albedarned Dairy, I began serving as a PDP host farm mentor with the hopes of sharing some of the things I wished I had known during school or early in my career. I’ve discovered that I learn just as much from the students as they learn from me.
Liza Chamberlain and Jenna Grulke complete calf chores at Grulke's farm, Albedarned Dairy. Image courtesy of Professional Dairy Producers.   Every mentor program student brings different experiences, interests and questions. Some grew up on farms, while others are completely new to agriculture. Their curiosity challenges me to think differently and it’s fun to watch them graduate, begin careers in the dairy industry and continue growing as professionals. The future of dairy will depend on people who are willing to stay curious, share what they’ve learned and encourage others along the way. If we continue creating opportunities for young people while staying committed to our own lifelong learning, our industry has a bright future. Jenna Grulke is part of the fourth generation to farm at Albedarned Dairy near Baldwin, Wisconsin. She farms with her husband, Paul, father, Bruce Achterhof, uncle, Joey Achterhof, and sister, Vanessa Gunderson. Together, they milk about 500 cows. She and Paul have two children under the age of 2; their son, Maverick, and daughter, Cecelia, born in June. This column is contributed by  Professional Dairy Producers (PDP) , the nation’s largest dairy producer-led organization of its kind. PDP focuses on producer professionalism, stakeholder engagement and unified outreach to share ideas, solutions, resources and experiences that help dairy producers succeed.
