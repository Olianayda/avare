Alvaro Garcia September 24, 2026 Before investing in automated milking systems, ask if the technology will generate enough profit to justify the cost. Few decisions influence the long-term economics of a dairy as much as investing in an automated milking system. Depending on herd size and facility design, that investment can easily reach several million dollars. Before purchasing milking robots, producers should ask one simple question: Will this investment generate enough additional profit to justify its cost? Looking beyond labor savings Labor shortages have become one of the strongest drivers behind the adoption of automated milking systems. By automating the milking process, the most successful automated milking dairies redirect employee time toward activities that have a greater impact on profitability than routine milking. Instead of standing in the parlor attaching milking units, employees can spend more time monitoring fresh cows, improving reproductive performance, observing cow behavior and identifying health problems before they become costly. These activities generate value that extends well beyond labor savings and help explain why two farms with identical automated milking systems can experience very different economic outcomes. Automated milking systems allow cows to determine much of their own milking schedule, enabling high-producing cows, particularly during early lactation, to be milked more frequently than twice daily. However, more milk does not automatically translate into greater profit because every additional pound of milk requires additional nutrients, electricity, water, equipment utilization and management, making the goal not to maximize milk production but net returns. Why three milkings matter A recent study published in the Journal of Dairy Science examined one of the most important management questions facing automated milking dairies: Does encouraging fresh cows to average at least three milkings per day improve economic performance? Advertisement Researchers evaluated records from commercial Wisconsin dairies using guided-flow automated milking systems, comparing cows that averaged at least three milkings per day during the first 21 days after calving with those that did not. Cows achieving the three-times-per-day target produced approximately 6 pounds more milk per day between 22 and 150 days in milk. Using average U.S. milk prices, this translated into approximately 5.4% greater milk revenue, or about $172 more revenue per cow during the study period. The researchers also reported higher butterfat and protein yields together with lower somatic cell counts in cows reaching the early-lactation target, further increasing the value of the milk produced ( Table 2 ).  One finding deserves particular attention because of its practical implications. Although each additional milking increased milk yield, the response became progressively smaller as milking frequency increased, indicating diminishing economic returns.  Importantly, the authors acknowledge that their analysis evaluated milk revenue rather than overall profitability. Although cows averaging three milkings per day generated more milk and greater milk revenue, the study did not account for the additional costs required to produce that milk. More frequent milking may require greater feed intake while increasing electricity use, water consumption, equipment wear and maintenance. That distinction is critical because a management practice that increases revenue does not necessarily increase profit. The additional income must exceed the additional costs of production. Because production costs vary among dairies, each farm must determine whether the additional revenue exceeds the additional cost of producing it. Five questions to ask before blaming the milking system When automated milking performance falls short of expectations, the equipment is often blamed first. The milking robot is usually responding to management decisions made elsewhere on the farm. Before concluding that the milking robots are underperforming, producers should step back and evaluate the factors that most strongly influence voluntary cow traffic and robot utilization. Five questions can help identify where the real opportunity lies.
Are fresh cows receiving the nutritional support they need?  Early lactation is the period when cows have the greatest biological potential to respond to more frequent milking. Poor transition management limits that potential long before robot settings become the problem.
Is the feeding program encouraging voluntary robot visits?  Proper partial mixed ration (PMR) formulation and strategic concentrate allocation are essential. Concentrate should encourage cows to visit the robot without replacing nutrients that belong in the PMR. As discussed previously, feeding strategies should motivate voluntary traffic while maintaining rumen health and consistent nutrient intake.
Is stocking density limiting cow traffic?  Overstocked pens increase competition for stalls, feed and robot access. Cows that spend more time waiting spend less time eating, resting and voluntarily visiting the robot.
Are cows comfortable enough to walk to the robot?  Lameness, heat stress, poor bedding, slippery floors and inadequate stall design all reduce voluntary robot visits. Comfortable cows move more, eat more and are more likely to maintain consistent milking frequency.
Are employees solving problems or preventing them?  Fetching cows is sometimes necessary, but routinely fetching cows should prompt a different question: Why didn't they come on their own? Identifying and correcting the underlying cause usually produces a much greater economic return than repeatedly moving cows to the robot.
Robots collect data; managers create profit Automated milking systems continuously monitor milk yield, milking frequency, activity, rumination, bodyweight, milk conductivity and many other variables, providing producers with more information than ever before. Yet data alone have no economic value. Their value lies in how they are interpreted and, more importantly, in the management decisions they support. The recent Journal of Dairy Science study provides evidence that helping fresh cows achieve an average of three milkings per day is associated with greater milk production and higher milk revenue. The challenge, however, is not simply encouraging more robot visits, but creating the nutritional, environmental and management conditions that allow cows to make those additional visits voluntarily. Automated milking systems should therefore be viewed as management platforms rather than labor-saving machines. Farms that consistently achieve the greatest return on this investment are those that make the best management decisions every day. Nutrition, cow comfort, transition management, stocking density and the timely interpretation of robot data ultimately determine whether these systems reach their full potential. Automation can milk cows, but it cannot replace sound management. The greatest return on investment does not come from installing robots; it comes from managing cows in ways that allow the system to perform as intended.
