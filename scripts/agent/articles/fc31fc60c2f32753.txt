When forage is in short supply, don’t overlook energy needs Rebecca Kern-Lunbery October 6, 2026 Nebraska’s wildfire and drought losses have left producers short on forage, making energy – not just protein – a critical focus for drylotting cow‑calf pairs. As cows move into lactation, their higher TDN needs outpace what most 2025 grass hay can provide. Over the past year, Nebraska has experienced our largest wildfires followed by some extreme drought conditions across the state. For cow-calf producers, this has been a trying year to say the least. Many have made the difficult decision to liquidate a large portion of their herds. Others have relied on hay stores from last year and hay donations to drylot their herds. Don’t skimp on nutrition when growing replacement females Last month, Ward Laboratories hosted a small roundtable discussion with representatives from trusted feed stores serving Nebraska producers. Jake Fairley, owner at Laird Feeds in Gothenburg, brought up the concern of supplementation. He says, “How do I get producers out of a protein mindset and guide them toward an understanding that they need energy too?” Holly Heil, beef cattle nutrition specialist at Ackerman Ag Service and Supply in Alliance, Nebraska, agreed. “I’m hesitant on recommending protein tubs when forage availability is already limited. They have a place in the right situation. With the drought conditions, I know the producer is trying to compensate for the lack of forage. But adding additional protein can actually increase the animal’s appetite and consequentially be harder on the land, making forage recovery in the coming year more difficult.” Angus bred heifers
stand on open pasture, showing how limited forage conditions increase the need to balance energy with available feed resources. Image by Holly Heil. Lactation increases the need for energy Something that struck me about the observations Fairley and Heil had about energy needs is that this is a conversation about cattle in a different production state than the cows we are typically feeding hay or stored feed to. The producers drylotting to get through this year are feeding cow-calf pairs. The nutritional needs of a lactating beef cow are higher than those for the gestating beef cows typically getting our stored forages. In our example, we will use a 1,200-pound cow; through lactation she needs 11% crude protein dry basis and 60% total digestible nutrients (TDN or energy) dry basis. A gestating beef cow, by contrast, would only require 9% crude protein and 54% TDN dry basis. Advertisement 2025 fall Nebraska grass hay nutritional value Looking at the grass hay samples submitted to Ward Labs from September through December of 2025, the supplementation needs shift from a protein emphasis to truly needing to evaluate both due to the shift in beef cow requirements from gestation to lactation. Figure 1 shows that for these grass hay samples, the majority meet or exceed energy requirements for gestating cows, but more than half require protein supplementation. At the same time, most of these forages don’t meet lactating beef cow requirements for either protein or energy. Of these forages, 80% would require energy supplementation for a lactating beef cow versus just 33% for a gestating beef cow (Figure 1). So producers are used to thinking about protein supplementation, but under these difficult circumstances it is imperative to consider both protein and energy. If energy is not considered, cows will lose body condition and have difficulty rebreeding. If the impact is severe enough, it can reduce calf growth performance as well due to lowered milk yield and energy content. However, simply providing protein and energy supplementation cannot replace forage altogether. Ruminants need fiber, too. Finding feed when hay inventories are low Now, we are heading into the fall/winter of 2026. Which means the majority of the cow herd should be gestating and therefore have lowered nutritional requirements. I have heard from forage and livestock producers around the state that their hay production has been anywhere from half to three-quarters what it typically is. Additionally, some producers in the western part of the state have told me they are anxious because hay prices are most certainly on the rise, and they will be needing to truck some hay in from as far east as Iowa and Minnesota. So what are our feed options? Red Angus bulls
feed from a bunk containing stored hay, a drylot setup used to maintain energy intake when forage availability is low.   Image by Holly Heil. First, many producers may have the opportunity to send cattle east to graze crop residues such as cornstalks on irrigated ground. Another option is mixing with ethanol co-products. Wet distillers can increase nutritional value including energy. Chopped hay can be mixed with these co-products and fed out in small batches or ensiled for longer-term storage. If ensiling this mixture, it is best to keep dry matter at or below 50% to ensure an anaerobic environment to ensure fermentation, not rot. Finally, if forage is very scarce, some producers may need to consider non-forage fiber sources, although shipment will be yet another obstacle in getting these. One commonly used non-forage fiber source in western Nebraska or eastern Colorado is beet pulp. Producers facing the same challenges in other areas of the country may feed oat hulls, soyhulls, cotton hulls or brewers grains. Plan for winter feeding Regardless of the unique strategy each operation will deploy, there are several steps they can take to set themselves up for success: 1. Evaluate body condition of the cows After the difficult year we’ve had, it’s highly likely cattle may have lost some body condition. When setting nutrition goals, it will be key to consider any need for extra protein or energy. Cattle in poor body condition will have a more difficult time handling winter's cold windy conditions. Supplementing them this fall after weaning is a good time to put on some weight before the winter storms start to hit. Red Angus cows on semiarid rangeland under midseason conditions are used to evaluate forage utilization efficiency and nutrient intake relative to pasture biomass and quality. Image by Holly Heil. 2. Test feeds and forages This year more than any in recent memory, it will be key to know what is in your forages and supplemental feeds. Growing conditions across the country have ranged from well-irrigated controlled to extremely drought-stressed. This means they are variable. That variability lies in the protein content, fibrous portion of the feed and ultimately the energy. Understanding what the forage is providing is key to choosing feeds that complement those protein and energy levels to meet animal requirements and production performance goals. 3. Develop a ration or supplementation plan Use the information and animal nutritional needs you found from evaluating body condition and the numbers from your forage and feed tests to come up with a solid ration or supplementation plan. Make sure you have the stores of feed needed to execute the plan to get through to spring grazing (she said hopefully). Without a plan, you’ll likely miss the mark on animal production performance or, in extreme cases, health. When forages are hard to find, the answer is rarely as simple as purchasing a protein tub. Energy often goes overlooked but goes hand in hand with fiber. Start with the cows in front of you, evaluate their body condition, test the feeds available and balance the entire diet for protein and energy, and be sure to consider fiber. Then secure enough feed to carry that plan through spring. Protein supplementation still has an important place, but it cannot fill an empty pasture, haystack or, perhaps most importantly, rumen. This year, the operations that plan early and understand exactly what their feeds provide will be in the best position to manage cow condition, reproductive performance and next year’s calf crop.
