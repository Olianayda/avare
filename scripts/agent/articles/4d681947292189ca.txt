Slow down to speed up: Improve milking parlor throughput with proper stimulation Shaun Hardtke September 4, 2026 Taking the time to correctly perform milking prep procedures will create a more efficient parlor. Efficiency matters everywhere. Parlor throughput, labor utilization and cow flow are constant conversations on dairies of every size. Yet in the pursuit of speed, we sometimes sacrifice the very thing that creates it. If we truly want to speed up the parlor, we first have to slow down. The foundation: Tactile stimulation Before we ever talk about prep-lag timing (time from initial stimulation to unit attach), we need to start with stimulation. Cows consistently milk out the quickest and most completely when they receive enough real tactile stimulation to the udder and teats. That stimulation can come from forestripping, a mechanical scrub brush, in-liner stimulation, manual towel prep or any thoughtful combination of these methods. What matters is not just chemical contact but intentional tactile contact. The udder is not a bag with a faucet you simply turn on. It is a hormone-driven system. Oxytocin release is triggered by stimulation, and that hormonal cascade is what initiates proper milk letdown. You wouldn’t cold start your tractor at 20 degrees below zero, immediately flip the power takeoff (PTO) on, begin loading and mixing with no warmup time without expecting problems. Yet in some parlors, we expect cows to do exactly that – go from zero to peak performance without a proper warm-up. Advertisement The 90- to 120-second window Once adequate stimulation has occurred, timing becomes critical. Decades of research and field experience continue to point to one proven target: 90 to 120 seconds of prep-lag time from first stimulation to unit attachment. That window allows oxytocin to circulate, alveoli to contract and milk to move down into the cistern. When units are attached in that range, peak flow comes sooner, average flow rates increase, units come off faster, milkout is more complete and overmilking is reduced. This is not theory. It is time-tested physiology. A real-world lesson from the parlor On a large commercial dairy I have worked closely with, what we found was simple. When technicians consistently spent eight to 10 or more seconds of mechanical teat scrubber contact on each cow – even when their overall prep routine was longer – that line of cows was released about 30 seconds sooner than lines where stimulation was shorter. Thirty seconds may not sound like much, but on a parlor running 5.5 turns per hour, it equated to roughly 20 to 30 minutes of regained time over a 7.5-hour milking shift. No new equipment. No extra labor. No rushing. Just proper stimulation. Since seeing that firsthand, I have worked with dairies across North America, South America, Europe and the Middle East to replicate the same concept. While every parlor layout and labor structure is different, the outcome has been remarkably consistent. When teams commit to adequate stimulation and disciplined prep-lag timing, unit-on time drops, throughput improves, and cows leave the parlor sooner. Good physiology translates across languages, cultures and parlor designs. Time spent on the front side truly saves time on the back side. The cow experience When we combine eight-plus seconds of tactile stimulation with a consistent 90- to 120-second prep lag, we dramatically improve the cow’s parlor experience. We see smoother milk letdown, fewer bimodal curves, reduced liner slip, less teat-end congestion and a lower long-term risk of hyperkeratosis. Cows are calmer because the routine is predictable, and milk flow matches what the machine is asking them to do. A properly stimulated cow with correct timing milks efficiently because her body is ready. There is less vacuum stress, less frustration and less time standing attached to equipment waiting for milk to arrive. Cow comfort isn’t just bedding or ventilation; it also includes how she is milked. Slow down to speed up This is the part that feels counterintuitive. Spending a few more intentional seconds on the front side creates measurable efficiency on the back side. True efficiency respects the cow’s physiology. Slow down to stimulate correctly, and slow down to hit the 90- to 120-second window. Then watch what happens after a week or two of consistency. Milk flows faster. Units come off sooner. Cows get back to their pens quicker. The parlor becomes more efficient, not because we rushed but because we prepared correctly. Those extra seconds of stimulation and disciplined prep-lag timing create a compounding effect. Across hundreds or thousands of cows per day, the gains become substantial and sustainable. Sometimes the fastest way forward is simply honoring the fundamentals. In the parlor, the fundamentals begin with stimulation and timing.
