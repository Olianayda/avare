Image by Jason Cavadini. What should I plant in my pasture? Jason Cavadini September 15, 2026 Most pasture questions come down to one decision: plant new seed or rely on better grazing management. The call depends on your goals, the forage already present and how the pasture is being managed. As a grazing specialist, I spend quite a bit of time answering questions from farmers about pasture management. I have noticed there are a few topics that come up every season. These frequently asked questions have inspired some of the extension grazing resources we have developed in recent years. Fall pasture management considerations One of those resources is titled: “What should I plant in my pasture?”   It is intended to walk you through the step-by-step process of answering that question for your farm because it is not an easy one to answer. It depends on several factors that are unique to your farm such as soil type (and drainage), type of livestock, level of grazing management and the other forage species already present. Further complicating this question is the fact that planting is not always necessary in the first place. Whether expanding grazing acres or getting more production out of existing pastures, we often think first of fertilization or planting. But those are also the costliest options that should only be used if necessary. The first question to ask is “Do I need to plant?” So how do you make that determination for your farm? What’s the goal? Is the primary objective to increase production of existing pastures or to increase acres of pasture available for grazing? In either case, if there is existing vegetation present that can be grazed, we often recommend starting out by “planting fence posts” rather than planting new seed. Good management is often the most economical step to transitioning existing forage to high-quality pasture. Even a pure stand of alfalfa or thick stand of reed canarygrass, while each poses its own challenges for grazing, can be managed to meet the nutritional requirements of most classes of livestock. Any stand of forage can be strategically improved over time with good grazing management and renovation practices. In both cases, planting is not the first step in the process. The only scenarios necessitating planting as a first step are cropland being converted to pasture, or existing pastures that need to be completely replanted because they are damaged beyond repair or overrun with undesirable plant species. Advertisement What is good grazing management? When I say “good grazing management” can improve established forages over time, what do I mean? First and foremost, it refers to the principles of managed grazing: 1) maintaining at least 4 inches of post-grazed residual, 2) rotating paddocks every one to three days and 3) resting paddocks for at least 30 days before grazing again. Second, good grazing management requires managing forage maturity. The name of the game with grazing is forage intake – the whole goal of the practice is for the animals to eat as much of the forage as possible down to the 4-inch residual height. It’s easy to grow frustrated with forages if they are rejected. Selective grazing by livestock gives farmers the impression that one forage is inferior to those around it, but in many cases, this is a result of inadequate pasture management. It happens because mature forages are less palatable than those that have regrown from an earlier grazing event, and it can be corrected by eliminating stemmy seed stalks by harvesting, clipping or trampling once reproductive growth is complete early in the season. This resets the entire pasture back to vegetative growth that locks most species into a high level of nutrition and palatability for the remainder of the grazing season, limiting the amount of selective grazing. This also increases pasture utilization, which is important for extending the grazing season as late into the year as possible. A helpful concept to remember is that all pasture grasses and legumes are high-quality at some point; it’s plant maturity that makes the difference . Image by Jason Cavadini. When replanting is necessary There will come a point in every grazing operation where some sort of planting is called for. Whether it's new annual crop acres being seeded to pasture or established pastures being renovated to improve composition, it is important to choose species that fit the farm’s landscape and goals. While commercial seed mixes are convenient and can be very good, you must be certain that every species being planted is compatible with the soil, climate, management and existing vegetation of your farm. Incompatible species result in selective grazing and altogether poor pasture production. The following considerations must be addressed to choose compatible species:
Soil type. The array of species available vary largely by the type of drainage they thrive in, which is a function of soil type. For example, smooth brome, orchardgrass and alfalfa can persist in droughty, sandy soils while Kentucky bluegrass, reed canarygrass and alsike clover thrive in poorly drained, clay soils. Many of the common species such as timothy, perennial ryegrass and red clover thrive in well-drained, silt loam soils. The point is that pastures with poorly drained or droughty soils will do better with species acclimated to such conditions.
Grazing management. You must give some honest consideration to how you will manage the pastures. Will the principles of managed grazing be strictly followed, or is there a chance pastures will be frequently overgrazed? Some classes of livestock such as sheep and horses are more likely to graze close to the ground even when managed carefully. Species that can tolerate heavy grazing pressure such as improved varieties of Kentucky bluegrass or tall fescue and white clover are best suited for these scenarios.
Palatability. Due to differences in plant chemistry, some species taste better than others. When multiple species exist in a pasture, selective grazing occurs when one is preferred over the other. Forage utilization will be the best when those species are similar in palatability. While clipping or harvesting to keep forage vegetative can help, it’s a good idea for seed mixes to contain species that are complementary to each other in palatability. For example, meadow fescue and tall fescue are both high-quality grasses, but meadow fescue is more palatable than tall fescue for most of the grazing season, and animals will almost always choose it first. This leads to repeated overgrazing of meadow fescue, ultimately reducing its presence over time. This consideration is especially important when renovating pastures. Species that are introduced into the stand should be complementary to those already there.
Seedbank.  What are the most common grasses and legumes that show up as “weeds” on the farm? These are probably species that have been planted in the past and are those most likely to show up in new plantings. They often persist because they are acclimated to the area. Rather than fighting these species, you may be better served to work with them. Regardless, species selected for planting should be complementary to those likely to show up. For example, a pasture dominated by tall fescue is unlikely to be improved by introducing orchardgrass. Renovating with tall fescue will yield better grazing results.
Fall is a good time to assess the composition, quantity and quality of pastures. Do they currently fit the farm's landscape and grazing goals?
