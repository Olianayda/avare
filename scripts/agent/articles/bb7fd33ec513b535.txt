Virtual Fencing for Cattle: Real Producers, Real Results September 1, 2026 SPONSORED Two livestock graziers share how using virtual fencing from Nofence helps them better manage their herds and pastures. Related Virtual fences with real results Using virtual fencing as a tool to keep cattle off of burned rangeland The future of virtual fencing Virtual fencing is making its way into more pastures across the U.S. as producers see how effective the technology is for managing cattle and forage. Virtual fence providers, such as Nofence, operate using GPS-enabled collars to keep cattle within boundaries created by producers via an app on their phone. The collar delivers an audio cue when an animal approaches a boundary. Should the animal reach the boundary, the collar delivers a mild electrical pulse as a deterrent. In addition to containment, Nofence collars track data about cattle movement and behavior. The data, as well as alerts to changes in cattle behavior, are delivered to the producer’s app via cellular and satellite connectivity. Nofence customers always own their data, and it is never sold or shared with third parties. Two producers who use Nofence in very different parts of the country answer questions about how they are managing land and cattle differently than before they had the systems. Here is some background on their operations before getting into their responses: Advertisement Tyler Chesshir, Big Lake, Texas
Grazes 15,000 acres of leased property with 200 Corriente-cross cows
Pastures are extremely arid and rocky across plains and mesas with native grasses, brush and cactus.
This area of West Texas averages approximately 18.2 inches of precipitation per year.
Chesshir previously implemented rotational grazing with electric fence.
Started using Nofence on a trial basis with five cows in November 2024, increased to 48 collars within months and now uses virtual fencing for the whole herd
Greg Brann, Adolphus, Kentucky
Uses multispecies grazing with 165 crossbred beef cows, 40 grass-finished beef cattle, 18 Jersey dairy cows, 70 goats and 140 sheep
Markets directly to consumers and works as a grazing consultant
Pastures are primarily Kentucky 31 fescue, along with other forages such as orchardgrass, johnsongrass, indiangrass, big bluestem and clover across rolling hills.
The area of southern Kentucky averages approximately 54 inches of precipitation per year.
The goats and dairy cows all have Nofence collars. Brann plans to put collars on all of his beef cows in the fall of 2026.
Greg Brann What were some of the triggers that made you go to virtual fence and then ultimately select Nofence? Chesshir : Installing electric fencing in our area is tough. You have to clear the brush first. Then you have to put up the wire and carry a drill around with you to drill holes into the rock to get your posts in the ground. Even if you use step-in pigtails, it’s almost impossible to take up and move the fence continuously. I knew that I wanted virtual fencing collars because it sounded amazing. Then it was a matter of finding a company that would deal with me, and I’m glad we went with Nofence because they are awesome. Brann : We went with Nofence because the customer service is so good. We started with the dairy because it was the most intensively managed, and it was only 18 collars. It was a good test. With the labor savings, I figured that if it could save five hours per week, then it would pay for the collars in a year. Now, I think it saves about 10-12 hours per week, so it’s kind of a no-brainer on that one. I’m a grazing specialist, so I talk to a lot of groups. For beef producers with 30 cows, which is the national average, they’re the ones that could really benefit from the collars. I can do more with those collars than I can do with a tractor. What are some of the benefits that you’ve experienced since implementing Nofence virtual fencing on your operation? Chesshir : After getting Nofence, I’m able to keep the cattle in smaller pastures while watching the grass and cows, so I do a much better job of managing both. That’s something that I’m really excited about. Knowing where your animals are located is a huge advantage. The time and fuel you save by not searching for cattle is beneficial. It’s helped me change my way of thinking. I can hold my cattle on sidehills they normally wouldn’t graze. I can go after forage that normally wouldn’t be utilized. Brann : The time savings is a big one. Now we aren’t spending time building fence and physically moving sheep, goats and cattle. We’ll put up a virtual back fence after three days because we don’t want to get it too tight during the grazing. We let them graze for three days, then we put up the back fence to rest pasture and move forward to fresh forage. That’s my entertainment: looking at them on the phone and seeing their grazing distribution. With our improved grazing, I think reasonably we’ll be able to go up 20% on stocking rates easily, but it may be more than that, more like 30%-40%. Tyler Chesshir  How much time would you say you spend on creating paddocks with virtual fence? Chesshir : Oh, about five minutes in the morning drinking coffee from my recliner. Brann : Well, per group it would just be no time at all. Five to 10 minutes at the tops. I do like to be in the field when I draw new grazing areas so I can see where I’m drawing the line. What surprised you that you weren’t expecting on your operation once you got the Nofence virtual fencing system? Chesshir : Well, there’s a lot. I don’t have to hire as much labor anymore. You can get the information on the heat map to see what they’re eating, what they like to eat and what they’re leaving. Just being able to know where they’re at is important. Brann : Some of the app stuff with the tracking is pretty darn cool. Having the ability to do a herd replay in the Nofence app to see where the animals are going via an animated map is neat. Being able to draw grazing lines so the goats or cattle don’t get into a creek and still utilize all of the forage has been nice. Have you adjusted your water system using virtual fencing? Chesshir : I’ve made a portable water system. Previously, during cold spells when the troughs would freeze, I would have busted ice on 20 water sites. Now I’m only worried about a handful of troughs because I can limit where the cattle have access. That’s helped reduce the time I’m out checking water sites any time of the year. Brann : We already have pretty good water distribution. It’s gotten so easy to lane back to our water, to partition off and let them come back and forth from water to forage using virtual fencing. Greg Brann Where do you think the future of virtual fencing is going to go in the next five to 10 years? Chesshir : It will allow people to do rotational grazing where it wasn’t an easy option. It’s going to make it easier for people to be better stewards of the land. I think that’s the main thing: being able to graze efficiently, have more control over where your cows are, what they’re doing and how long they’re in a spot. Brann : It’s a super opportunity. Going forward, we want it to calculate our grazing days per acre for each field. Then we can compare our management, make improvements and see where that’s taking us. Monitoring movement more precisely and even heat detection will be great pieces of data to have for decision-making in the future. What are your closing thoughts on virtual fencing after implementing it? Chesshir : It’s life-changing. What I’ve been able to do with these collars is very impressive. Brann : I do think that it's a thing of the future. There’s so much opportunity in virtual fencing. I can’t even see what they all are, so I’ve got to dream more.
