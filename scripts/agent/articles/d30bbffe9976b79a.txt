Home &raquo; Progressive Cattle &raquo; West: Winter parasite management Pests & Wildlife West: Winter parasite management Carmen Willmore October 9, 2026 Overall health, growth, reproduction and production can all be negatively affected by external parasites in cattle. For cattle producers, winter can bring increased pressure from lice and other external parasites. Cooler temperatures, thicker winter hair coats and closer contact between cattle all create favorable conditions for lice populations to increase. Carmen Willmore Extension Educator / University of Idaho Extension – Bingham County Email Carmen Willmore Related West: Winter facilities and resource planning in the Intermountain West West: Winter blues and mental health West: Parasite control in the winter months Cattle host two functional groups of lice: sucking lice, which pierce the skin and feed on blood, and biting lice, which feed primarily on skin debris and hair. Both types spend their entire life cycle on cattle. During hot summer weather, lice populations are generally less active, and reproductive activity slows. As temperatures cool in the fall, lice become more active and reproduction increases. Lice lay eggs, or nits, that are cemented to individual hair shafts. These eggs hatch into nymphs, which develop into adults capable of reproducing. Under favorable conditions, this life cycle can be completed in approximately 28 to 30 days. This relatively short cycle allows populations to build rapidly during the winter. Understanding this cycle is important when determining treatment timing and whether a follow-up treatment may be necessary. Lice are host-specific, meaning cattle lice are adapted to cattle. This is important when producers are concerned about lice moving between different livestock species. While cattle may share environments with sheep, goats, horses or other animals, cattle lice do not normally establish persistent infestations on other livestock species. By the time cattle are visibly rubbing against fence posts, buildings or equipment, a lice infestation may already be well established. Common signs include: Advertisement
Patchy hair loss, particularly around the neck, shoulders, withers and rump
Excessive rubbing and scratching
Restless behavior
Rough or dull hair coats
Skin irritation or scabbing
Damage to fences, posts and equipment from rubbing
Reduced weight gain and body condition
Anemia in cattle with heavy sucking-lice infestations
The economic impact can also be significant. Research and field observations have associated heavy lice infestations with reduced average daily gain (ADG), decreased milk production and poorer body condition. Even when cattle do not appear severely affected, the energy spent responding to irritation can interfere with normal eating, resting and growth. For herds with a history of winter lice problems, monitoring should begin as cattle develop their winter coats. Producers should watch for increased rubbing, hair loss, visible lice and changes in body condition during the fall and winter. Treatment timing should be based on parasite pressure and the product label. Treating too early may provide limited protection if lice populations increase later in the winter, while waiting until cattle have significant hair loss or poor condition can allow production losses to occur. Because eggs may survive an initial treatment, some products or situations may require a follow-up treatment. The appropriate re-treatment interval varies by product and parasite, so producers should follow label directions rather than relying on a standard calendar date. Mites can also cause mange in cattle, resulting in itching, hair loss, crusting and thickened skin. Because treatment varies by parasite species, suspected mite infestations should be properly identified with assistance from a veterinarian. Regular observation throughout the fall, winter and early spring, combined with good nutrition and timely treatment, can help Western cattle producers minimize the effects of external parasites and maintain cattle health and productivity.
