Home &raquo; Blogs &raquo; Tales of a Hay Hauler &raquo; Tales of a Hay Hauler: And the life goes on Tales of a Hay Hauler: And the life goes on Brad Nelson September 1, 2026 Counting tires, chasing leaks and trusting hundred‑mile‑an‑hour tape framed a trip put together by family stories and practical fixes. Pulling the 1979 Avion back into service marked a return to real camping after years of tents, pop‑ups and rental RVs. Count the tires. The first time the grandson heard that phrase, he gave me a puzzled look. A tire on a vehicle, by my definition, is one that is holding adequate air pressure to do its job. The boy grinned. Same grin as when he found out what I meant when I handed him the male end of an extension cord and sent him to find a “current bush.” Brad Nelson Retired Hay Hauler Email Brad Nelson One day he asked me why I always called duct tape “hundred-mile-an-hour tape.” That’s one I picked up from my old friend Leo Ritthaler. He drove on the Alaska pipeline project. He said the drivers would patch the cracks in the fiberglass (and other) tilting hoods on the haul, and the repair was adequate to hold that hood together even if the driver ran off the road “doing a hundred-miles-an-hour.” So I dutifully counted the supposedly six tires on my 1979 Avion travel trailer and got five that met my definition of a tire. I aired it up, then grabbed a bottle of Windex and sprayed around the bead and the valve stem. Lots of bubbles around the base of the valve stem. It was the heavy truck two-piece valve stem with the outside nut, so I tightened the nut and stopped the bubbles. Then added air back to the prescribed 70 pounds and proceeded with the adventure at hand. My kids started organizing a family reunion at a private resort near Conconully, Washington, a handful of years back. Elli thought proper camping involved a tent, so we did a wilderness campsite adventure a couple of years. I had discovered that a 10-foot pop-up canopy with side curtains was more agreeable to my 6-foot-4-inch carcass than any domed tent, so off we went. Cabins and rental RVs were an option, but our tendency to count the nickels had us declining. We did use the rental RV option a couple of times, but I went back to real camping after we lost Elli. For this year, I decided to pull the Avion out of retirement. Advertisement Several months ago, at the end of last year’s warm and hot days, I bought a 6,000 BTU room air conditioner because “It was marked down to $85 from the almost $200 they’d been priced at all summer, and when I actually needed one, it would cost more.” I set this up inside the Avion next to a screened window and shrouded it with cardboard. I had cold air. Yes, while in transit that little AC unit rides on the sofa. My son Ryan, the RV tech, told me there were two chances that the gas/electric fridge in the Avion would ever work again. Slim and none. Costco had a mini fridge on sale, so I added that. The factory electrical system on the Avion had been funky, so I completely ignored it. For lights, I hung some single-bulb LED shop lights down the center. An hour with the shop vacuum, and I had a livable grandpa cave. I hooked it up to go to the tire store and replace the quarter-century-old tires that had been holding between little and no air, and –
no brake or turn signal lights. I did find places on the back my magnetic towing lights would anchor to, so thusly rigged, we were off to feed money to the tire store. Back home, I tried the trailer light pigtail, and voila. All the outside trailer lights worked. The wasp nest I had removed from the hitch must have left residue; it took a trip to town to establish a ground for the lights. To simplify the ingress and egress, I parked a 6-foot folded stepladder on the factory trailer step, laid a strip of plywood on top of the ladder and anchored it (and a piece of two-by-four lumber as a handrail) in place with bungee cords. It was impressively stable on the rough roads to and from the reunion. Pulled with my 2006 Dodge diesel pickup (with 540,000 miles on it) and had no trouble running with traffic and passing the occasional slow traveler  – 12 miles per gallon fuel mileage. Saturday afternoon, another party got their pickup and boat trailer off the boat loading ramp. Badly. The right rear wheel was totally underwater. My grandson Luke tried to help with his half-ton pickup and made four new holes in the sand. My son Dan followed with his Dodge diesel with similar results. I added “old Red” to the equation. Dan was hooked to the stuck truck with a chain. I positioned ahead of his rig and connected his rig and mine with my tow strap. Dan asked how I wanted to go about this. “Slow and steady.” My rig is an automatic, Dan’s a stick shift. As soon as I tightened up the tow strap between us, Dan eased on his clutch and hardly breaking traction, the water-bound rig popped out of its hole in the lake. Nice to see my boys being helpful. Got hugs and an enormous cookie out of the deal, too.
