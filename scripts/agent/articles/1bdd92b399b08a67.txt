Breaking into the beef industry today Julia McCarthy September 21, 2026 Good news, risks, cautions and advice for prospective cattlemen, from across the industry Seasoned rancher Merrill Beyeler of Leadore recalls meeting with an economist as a young cattleman. “He said, ‘There is a time to buy and there is a time to sell. I can’t tell you what is the time, but when the time comes, I’ll know,’” Beyeler chuckles. Bearing that illuminating insight in mind, what unique challenges and opportunities do current market conditions offer to young people seeking to enter the beef industry? Julia McCarthy Freelance Writer Julia McCarthy is a freelance writer based in north-central Idaho. While the beef market “continues to exceed expectations,” he says, peaks and valleys are still there – just smaller. One of the factors keeping beef prices high is the historically low domestic and international inventory. “Cattle numbers are low, low, low,” Beyeler says, due in part to continuing drought and the loss of relatively small operations. As of July 1, the nation’s all-cattle inventory was up 0.2%, indicating that it may have entered the “rebuild” phase. However, Wilder says that there is reason to believe that inventory will still be in a flat or contractionary window for another year, meaning high prices would continue. Further, the demand for beef products is still very strong. “Relative to the share of other items in the grocery store, demand has never been higher. The beef industry has done a good job of making itself inelastic,” says Wilder. Advertisement In fact, U.S. total beef consumption is the highest in the world. “High demand gives people hope that there is opportunity,” he says. Weighing in the prospective cattleman’s favor are access to risk management tools and high value of all cattle, right down to fresh calves. “You at least have some confidence [of selling high],” he says. “That is a bit of a safety net.” High value, high risk But will these prices stay high long enough to profit someone buying cows for the first time? “The high prices will eventually bring enough participants into the marketplace to bring prices down,” says Adam Carter, an ag banker with Western Ag Credit and a rancher south of Malad. “We all remember 2014-2016,” Beyeler says. “You’ve got to look at how to reduce risk. I would not go out and buy bred cows and heifers right now – find something less risky.” “You’re banking on that calf being able to bring a lot of money,” says Anna Lickley, fifth-generation rancher and ag program manager of the Idaho State Department of Agriculture's (ISDA) Farm & Ranch Center. “I feel it is a difficult economic situation for a young producer to be in – trying to grow an ag operation and buy groceries to feed a family. Building a family doesn’t give the flexibility to take risks.” She suggests that a less risky option is rethinking one’s entry point into the industry. “I think now is a great time to learn as much as possible from other producers,” Lickley says. With extra income, established producers may have the income – and extra work and knowledge – to be able to invest in young producers. “There is no replacement for knowledge and experience,” she says. “You should think about, ‘What skills and knowledge do I need to set me up for success when the cycle turns?’” From recordkeeping to developing a business plan, she sees current market conditions as an opportunity to develop skills that will make a young producer valuable to an operation. She adds that those in such a position should consider asking older generations what skills and attributes they would like to see their successors have. “The family members who have built up the operation you are considering entering … deserve the respect of asking them those questions.” Consider all opportunities For some, the time for market entry is now, based on local land lease availability or family dynamics – but that doesn’t mean a traditional operation is the best option. “Young members of generational operations should make sure they understand the financial situation they’re entering,” says Lickley. “Different parts of the industry have a margin at different parts of the cycle.” “There are always segments of the beef industry where you can get in, always an opportunity,” Carter says. “Somewhere, someone is making money in the cull cow business.” An understanding of the markets, a good financial management system and a solid plan are factors he says can make or break a new operation. “Look for opportunities others are not seeing,” he says. “Look for gaps in the market … Most producers are looking at production rather than profit.” Carter particularly advises prospective young cattlemen to take stock of their cash and feed availability. “You will never go broke owning too much cash and too much feed,” he says. “You will go broke with too many cattle and not enough grass or feed.” Wilder recommends starting small and cautions against making “lifestyle purchases” like new pickups and squeeze chutes. “That’s the easiest way to get upside down – getting more equipment, getting more fixed costs than you can service with the number of cows that you have,” he says. “If you want to do this as a business, not a hobby, focus on buying what you need .” Some would even add land to that category of lifestyle purchases. “Owning real estate and owning cattle are two separate businesses,” says Carter. “The price is no longer tied to what it can produce, and it’s been that way for a long time.” Creative ranching While opportunity exists, it may take some creativity to reduce risk and make it pay. Beyeler suggests that one option to reduce risk would be to purchase open cows direct from a rancher, breed them, raise the calves and sell the bred cows. “That might be the least risky thing to do, if you have a place to run them,” he says. Carter cites a family in which the parents, looking to retire, have set up a lease-to-own arrangement with their child over the next decade. “For parents, they have to consider what are the tax implications if they want to get out,” he says. “With the right structure, I can’t think of a better time, if they don’t have to borrow a ton of money.” That, of course, is “a very individual decision,” says Wilder. “There has to be transparency. There have to be questions asked by both the young and old generations.” High on his list are whether the ranch can support another family, and whether there is a path to ownership for the young operator. He adds that in some situations, the sensible course may be to begin a new operation and run alongside an existing one. Reminiscing on the economist who shared his wisdom about the cattle market so many years ago, Beyeler relates, “There was a time no one wanted to invest any capital in either hay or cattle. We had hay, but no market. Calves were dirt cheap. Words that I shook my head about took on meaning, and we acted. We purchased calves and marketed our hay through them. Sometimes when no one wants in, it is the time to get in. I’m looking at the market right now, and I’m not sure this would be the best time to enter the cattle business in the traditional way.” Opportunities for young producers do exist in specific circumstances or areas of the industry, “But even in that,” says Beyeler, “you have to be conservative and a little creative at the same time.” Related Thomas Cattle Company: Bridging tradition and innovation in the beef industry RancHER: Brandi Buzzard Frobose: Leaving a positive impact on the beef industry RancHER: Jaclyn Wilson: Working, learning and growing in the beef industry Julia McCarthy Freelance Writer Julia McCarthy is a freelance writer based in north-central Idaho.
