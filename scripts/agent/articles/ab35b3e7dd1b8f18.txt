Véronique Dallaire September 17, 2026 Challenging the status quo and continually reevaluating protocols can give your operation a productive edge. When visiting different farms, the question “How do they stay one step ahead?” often comes up. Some always seem a little better prepared. They adapt more quickly, weather tough times with greater confidence and seem ready when opportunities arise. Yet they aren’t necessarily the largest, they don’t always have the newest facilities, and they certainly don’t have a secret formula. So what makes the difference? It is usually not dramatic moves but rather a few repeated habits that eventually create a real advantage. Results rarely come as a surprise On the farm, dozens of decisions are made every day. Some are based on experience, others on observation, still others on intuition. Producers who stay one step ahead usually have another habit: They regularly take the time to check whether what they observe on the farm is actually reflected in their results. Advertisement Not just once a year. It is easier to course-correct when you quickly spot that something isn’t performing as expected. Let’s take three examples: Feed costs increase by a few cents per kilogram, a repair takes an hour longer than expected, an employee works a little more overtime during harvest season. Taken separately, these deviations may seem minor, but by the end of the year, they can add up to several thousand dollars. Waiting several months to take stock is a bit like driving while looking mostly in the rearview mirror. You may eventually see where you’ve been, but sometimes it is too late to correct your course. Experience remains invaluable, but when it is backed by concrete results, it can give you even greater confidence in the decisions you make. Small adjustments often beat major changes No company is perfect. Costs fluctuate, markets change, production conditions vary. Some decisions yield the desired results. Others do not. What makes the difference isn’t avoiding mistakes, it is detecting them before they become problems. Sometimes, a small adjustment made at the right time is often more effective than a major reorganization undertaken when it is already too late. This ability to adapt is often more valuable than striving for the perfect decision. Consider a slightly adjusted ration after noticing a rise in feed costs, or equipment repaired before a breakdown interrupts operations in the middle of the season. Small adjustments often go unnoticed until you realize just how much trouble they helped prevent. The status quo is rarely free Agriculture is constantly evolving. What worked very well 10 years ago isn’t necessarily what will yield the best results tomorrow. Seasoned managers don’t just change everything, they remain curious. They ask questions and challenge the status quo: Why do we do things this way? Is this still the best approach? Is there another way to do it? Let’s say a certain method of feeding the herd has been producing good results for several years. Why change it? Then, the cost of inputs changes. Production goals shift. New knowledge becomes available. The method hasn’t become bad, but it may no longer be synonymous with performance. These questions don’t call into question the experience gained. On the contrary, they help ensure that experience continues to create value. Because one of the costliest phrases in business is: “We’ve always done it this way.” They make decisions before they have to Let’s be honest: There are decisions we know are necessary but that we put off anyway. Replacing equipment that still works, revising a process that yields acceptable results, initiating a difficult conversation with a business partner or family member, investing in a project whose benefits won’t be visible for several years. Putting off a decision often provides some short-term comfort. After all, if there is no rush, why not wait a little longer? Yet the decisions we avoid have a strange way of coming back to haunt us. Equipment costs a little more to repair each year, a method gradually becomes less effective, a postponed discussion often becomes more difficult over time. It is not the event itself that is costly. It is often the delay in taking action. What we face fades from view, what we run from follows us. The best managers don’t always make the right decisions on the first try. They’ve simply started thinking about some of them before circumstances force them to do so. What if that is where the edge lies? The most resilient farms don’t all have the same goals. They don’t all produce in the same way. They don’t all have the same resources. However, resilient farms share one common habit: They take the time to observe what is changing, to question certain things and to act before decisions become urgent. Perhaps their true edge isn’t visible in their buildings or equipment, it is built long before the results are noticeable. Because the best decisions are rarely made in a rush. A quick audit Planning ahead doesn’t mean second-guessing everything every week. But once a month, or a few times a year, it can be helpful to take a step back and ask yourself a few questions:
What is a little annoyance that comes up often on the farm, but that I still consider normal?
What decision have I been putting off longer than I’d care to admit?
Are there any difficult but necessary decisions that I’ve been deliberately putting off for too long?
What habit have I never questioned in the past five years?
If I were starting over today, would I make the same decisions regarding certain equipment, expenses or ways of working?
What is working better this year than it did a year ago and why?
If I continue doing things exactly the same way for the next three years, where might my business end up?
These questions aren’t meant to dampen your ambitions or change your plans. They’re meant to help you develop the habit of thinking things through before you’re forced to react.
