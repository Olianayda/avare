Home &raquo; Idaho Ag Proud &raquo; Good markets, bad decisions: Why some ranches fail during the best of times Business Management Good markets, bad decisions: Why some ranches fail during the best of times Steve Smith September 10, 2026 Strong cattle markets can hide financial weaknesses. Ranchers who focus on long-term budgeting, managing debt, building working capital and making disciplined investments are better positioned to weather future downturns. Given today's record cattle prices, one would expect most ranches to be thriving. Higher incomes, stronger margins and expansion opportunities would certainly suggest as much. Yet my experience shows that some ranches continue to struggle financially and, in some cases, fail during these same periods. One might ask why? The explanation is surprisingly simple: good markets often conceal management weaknesses that would be obvious during tougher times. The three legs are production, marketing and financial management. Most producers spend their careers developing expertise in production and marketing. They understand genetics, reproduction, grazing management and cattle markets. The leg that often receives the least attention, especially during profitable years, is financial management. Ironically, it is often the most important determinant of whether a ranch survives the next downturn. The first leg, production, the pride and joy of any ranching operation and is often discussed at the local coffee shop. It includes reproductive efficiency, grazing management, animal health, and genetics. The second leg, marketing, involves understanding market cycles, developing buyer relationships, managing risks such as utilizing Livestock Risk Protection, and capturing the best value for a quality animal. Both are essential. In my opinion, the third leg, financial management, is where many ranches either secure their future or unknowingly create future problems. Financial management is not simply recordkeeping or preparing financial statements for your lender. It is the discipline of making decisions based on long-term economics rather than short-term profitability. It is understanding how today's decisions will affect the ranch in the years to come.  Think long term One of the most common mistakes producers make during strong cattle markets is budgeting around today's prices rather than long-term prices. Record cattle prices can create the illusion that current profitability is normal and sustainable. Major decisions such as land purchases, facility expansions, equipment upgrades and herd growth may suddenly appear affordable. The problem is that these investments often have lives measured in decades, while cattle cycles are much shorter. Advertisement That is why a land purchase financed over 20 years will be evaluated at long term trend prices vs current market prices. Similarly, a new equipment purchase should not depend on exceptional margins. Financially disciplined operators stress-test major decisions using long-term average cattle prices and more conservative assumptions. A simple question should be asked with every large purchase: Will this investment still work if cattle prices return to normal? If the answer is no, the investment should be reconsidered. Making better financial decisions one month at a time Strong financial management also requires the discipline to create budgets and adhere to them. Many operations begin the year with thoughtful financial plans but abandon those plans when profitability exceeds expectations. When cash is abundant, spending becomes easier to justify.  Financial expert Dave Ramsey stated, “A budget is telling your money where to go instead of wondering where it went." The most successful ranches view budgets as management tools rather than paperwork exercises. They compare actual performance against projected performance throughout the year and adjust when spending is out of line.  Recently, a local producer told me one of his best decisions was sharing monthly cash flow budgets with his ranch managers. While they don't make major capital decisions, they manage many of the day-to-day operating expenses. Individually these costs may seem small, but collectively they have a significant impact on profitability. Controlling expenses in good years is often more important than the bad years. Making financial decisions with taxes in mind Another financial trap during profitable periods is allowing tax decisions to drive business decisions. Producers frequently hear the advice that it is better to spend money than pay taxes. While tax management is important, purchasing assets solely to avoid taxes is not a great long-term strategy. In fact, some of the most financially successful producers recognize that paying taxes is often evidence of profitability. Purchases should be evaluated based on expected returns rather than tax consequences. In my 19-year career, producers who make the most prudent financial decisions during commodity booms are best positioned for the downturns that will surely come. Unfortunately, good markets tend to attract increased fixed costs just in the name of not paying income taxes. Working capital is your best friend Dave Kohl, a professor emeritus at Virgina Tech said “Working Capital will be a managers and owner’s best financial friend in the years to come.” Strong markets provide a unique opportunity to build liquidity and strengthen working capital. Working capital is created during the best years and exhausted during the worst years. Successful operators should use profitable periods to increase liquidity rather than find ways to spend it. One thing I have learned over my career is every operation will eventually face hardships such as drought, crop failure, rising interest rates, lower commodity prices or unexpected expenses. The risks go on and on. But the operations with strongest liquidity and manageable debt loads are best positioned to navigate these challenges.  Summary  Ranching is one of the most rewarding lifestyles available. Few professions offer the opportunity to work alongside family, care for livestock and steward the land. Yet ranching must also be treated as a business. The passion that makes ranching special should be supported by budgets, financial analysis and disciplined decision-making. Without profitability, even the strongest ranching legacy becomes difficult to sustain. In the end, ranches rarely fail because producers forget how to raise cattle. More often, they fail because their financial discipline gets lazy. Production and marketing management remain important legs of the stool, but financial management is frequently the leg that determines long-term success. Good markets create opportunities. Financial discipline ensures that opportunity lasts.
