Business Management Should dairy farmers consider feeding market cows before sending them to slaughter? Michael W. Overton , Steven Roche , James Byrne , Hanne Goetz , and Todd Duffield September 24, 2026 Researchers from the U.S. and Canada studied the economic benefits of feeding thin cows to a higher body condition score before slaughter. Results proved this is economically viable for some dairies. Each year, about 30% to 40% of dairy cows in Canada are replaced due to reproductive issues, low milk production, health challenges and other reasons. These cull or market cows are typically sold for slaughter. However, how farmers handle these animals during their final weeks raises important questions about both animal welfare and farm economics. Studies in Ontario and British Columbia found that between 10% and 40% of market dairy cows were very thin, with a body condition score (BCS) of 2.0 or less on a 5-point scale. Market cows may spend days in the marketing system, facing multiple transportation events, unfamiliar environments, mixing with strange animals and irregular access to food and water. Poor body condition at arrival is not only an animal welfare concern but also an economic issue. Thin cows, those with BCS of 2.0 or less commonly receive 20 cents to 63 cents per kilogram of live bodyweight less as compared to cows in better condition. Additionally, recent changes to Canadian animal transportation regulations now classify cows with certain conditions, such as udder engorgement or severe lameness, as unfit for transport, reflecting growing efforts to improve animal welfare. A team of U.S. and Canadian researchers used economic modeling to evaluate whether feeding market cows for 60 days before shipping might improve their economic value while also presumably improving their welfare. Once a decision was made to replace these cows, they were fed a low-energy diet for 14 days to aid in their dry-off and then fed a finishing diet for 46 days to help them gain weight and improve their body condition. Previous studies showed that cows fed for 60 days before slaughter gained an average of 117 kilograms and approximately 1.2 body condition score points, with improvements ranging from 0.5 to 2.0 points. They also had better fitness for transport, improved carcass quality and better meat quality compared to cows shipped directly. The model compared two scenarios: shipping a cow directly to slaughter at a BCS of 2.0 versus feeding her for 60 days to reach a BCS of 3.0. The calculations showed that a cow fed for 60 days and sold at a higher bodyweight (817 kilograms versus 700 kilograms) and better condition could sell for approximately $664 more than a thin cow shipped directly. However, this benefit must be weighed against the costs of keeping the cow on the farm for two additional months. Advertisement The costs included:
Feed costs: About $263 (14-day dry-off diet followed by 46 days on a lactating-style finishing ration, often using feed refusals) plus approximately $43 in daily care costs.
Health costs: A combined health cost of approximately $33 per cow, covering a mastitis risk of about 9% (average treatment cost $32 per case) and teat sealant for mastitis prevention ($30 per cow).
Mortality risk: Average reported mortality risk from the cited research was about 4%, resulting in an average mortality loss of $103 per cow, accounting for the possibility that some cows might die during the 60-day period.
After accounting for all these costs (totaling $442), the net benefit of feeding a cow from a body condition score of 2.0 to 3.0 would be approximately $222 (or a 1.6 return on investment) using the baseline input values, but the actual return from this strategy depends heavily on several factors (which the researchers examined through sensitivity analyses):
Initial body condition: The practice is most profitable for cows that start out very thin. Cows beginning with a body condition score of 2.0 showed the best return on investment. For cows already in better condition, the economic benefit of additional feeding diminishes considerably due to the reduction in anticipated price premiums.
Feed costs: The baseline feed cost was assumed to be 23 cents per kilogram, based on a 25% discount off the original TMR cost when using leftover feed from their lactating cows. If feed costs are higher, the benefit-to-cost ratio becomes less favourable.
Market prices: When beef prices for market dairy cows are strong ($4 to $4.50 per kilogram), the benefit-to-cost ratio reaches about 1.7, making the strategy more attractive.
While the economic model shows potential benefits, several practical factors must be considered:
Farm capacity: This strategy only works if a farm has space to house market cows without overcrowding the rest of the herd. Overcrowding can reduce milk production in lactating cows, increase disease risk and decrease fertility – costs that weren't captured in this model.
Individual cow health: Not all designated market cows are good candidates for a 60-day feeding period. Cows being removed from the herd due to severe lameness or serious illness may not benefit from extended retention and could suffer if kept longer or potentially die. Mortality during the feeding period, though at a low risk, accounted for 25% of the total estimated costs in the model, so carefully selecting which cows to feed is crucial. Selecting healthy cows that have a lower mortality risk can increase the net return by up to about 30%, depending on the final mortality risk.
Variable weight gain: Individual variation in weight gain rates introduces some uncertainty into economic projections, though healthy market cows are generally efficient at gaining weight once milking stops.
Timing of culling decisions: The model highlights the importance of making replacement decisions before cows lose too much condition. Waiting too long to replace a cow may mean missing the opportunity to capture optimal market value and increase the likelihood of welfare problems.
Pre-slaughter feeding aligns animal care with economic viability, reducing the number of thin, stressed animals entering the market while protecting the industry’s reputation. But this is not a one-size-fits-all strategy. The greatest returns were observed with healthy but thin candidates with low risk of disease and death, access to low-cost feed (such as good-quality ration refusals) and sufficient space to house market cows separately. While current analysis is limited by a lack of specific pricing data for varying body conditions, future research could further optimize returns by tailoring feeding durations to individual animals rather than using a fixed 60-day period. With careful management and appropriate selection of candidate cows, dairy farmers can improve animal welfare and economic returns simultaneously – a rare opportunity to do good while doing well.
