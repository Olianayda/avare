Engineered microbes could cut nitrogen costs, sharpen crop efficiency and ease environmental strain Loretta Sorensen August 24, 2026 Engineered microbes could stabilize costs, deliver nutrients directly at the root and cut the environmental footprint of synthetic fertilizer. For decades, synthetic nitrogen fertilizer has been one of agriculture’s greatest achievements and one of its greatest environmental challenges. Tim Schnabel, founder of Switch Bioworks, believes the next revolution in crop nutrition won’t come from chemical factories but from living microbes engineered to work naturally alongside plant roots. Loretta Sorensen Freelance Writer Loretta Sorensen is a freelance writer based in South Dakota. Lower and more stable costs One of the biggest advantages of biologically produced nitrogen fertilizer is the economic stability it provides for growers. “Today’s nitrogen fertilizer prices are closely tied to natural gas prices,” Schnabel says. “When energy prices fluctuate, fertilizer prices fluctuate. That’s not good for farmers.” Since traditional nitrogen fertilizer is manufactured using natural gas through the Haber-Bosch process, global events such as wars, supply chain disruptions and energy shortages can quickly drive up costs. Recent factors contributing to high fertilizer costs include the Iran war, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, the COVID-19 pandemic and other geopolitical events. A microbial fertilizer system would largely decouple crop production from natural gas markets, giving growers a more reliable and predictable fertilizer source. Advertisement Environmental sustainability The environmental benefits of microbial fertilizer would be equally significant. Current nitrogen fertilizer production consumes enormous amounts of gas and contributes substantially to greenhouse gas emissions. Once applied to fields, excess nitrogen can also escape into the atmosphere in the form of nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas nearly 300 times more potent than carbon dioxide. “Nitrogen runoff presents another major problem,” Schnabel says. “Fertilizer that washes into streams and rivers eventually reaches larger waterways, fueling harmful algal blooms and creating oxygen-depleted ‘dead zones,’ including the well-known hypoxic zone in the Gulf of Mexico fed by the Mississippi River.” The Switch Bioworks approach would dramatically reduce these losses. Instead of applying large amounts of synthetic fertilizer across an entire field, the company’s engineered microbes colonize plant roots and act as tiny living fertilizer factories. Applied once, the microbes remain associated with the roots, producing nitrogen exactly where the plant needs it. This targeted delivery means less fertilizer escapes into waterways or the atmosphere. Young corn plants thrive under LED lights in a controlled environment designed to test microbial nitrogen efficiency. Image courtesy of Switch Bioworks . A decade of research Schnabel’s passionate interest in plants and fertilizer is rooted in a childhood gift, a banana plant his uncle presented to him. "It was the first plant I ever owned. I tried to keep it alive and learn its language. A couple of years later, I had accumulated hundreds of houseplants,” Schnabel says. “A really healthy plant that’s thriving just gives me a lot of joy.” His childhood hobby led Schnabel to work in a plant laboratory at Stanford University and “got me addicted to trying to solve problems with biotechnology. Plants don’t have a way to make their own nitrogen fertilizer, but bacteria and microbes do. So what if we can engineer the microbes that naturally live on the roots of plants and making nitrogen for themselves to share the nitrogen with the plant?” Building a better microbial team Although biological crop products have been marketed for years, many have struggled to produce consistent results. “Some products work in some fields but not in others,” Schnabel says. “Rather than improving existing products, we started from scratch.” Schnabel and his team first asked a basic question: How many microbes are actually needed to supply meaningful amounts of nitrogen to a crop? The answer surprised them. “We’re talking about tens to hundreds of billions of microbes per plant,” Schnabel says. “We searched around the world for bacterial strains best suited to colonize crop roots. Instead of relying on a single bacterial species, we assembled teams of complementary microbes.” In the manner of a sports team, different microbes excel under different environmental conditions. If one strain struggles in a particular soil or climate, others can continue performing, creating much more reliable field performance. Teaching microbes to share Naturally occurring nitrogen-fixing bacteria already convert nitrogen gas from the atmosphere into ammonia. The problem is, they keep it for themselves. “Microbes don’t naturally share this precious resource,” Schnabel says. “Why would they just give it away? We are using genetic engineering to change that behavior.” In their research, Switch Bioworks is engineering each microbial strain to release ammonia for plant use instead of retaining it internally. Accomplishing that goal has proved far more difficult than anticipated, requiring years of research to engineer every member of the microbial community. Corn leaves stretch skyward in midsummer, reflecting the promise of nitrogen‑efficient growth through microbial innovation. Image courtesy of Switch Bioworks. The switch that makes it work There’s one more important caveat: If bacteria begin manufacturing ammonia immediately, they divert too much energy toward fertilizer production and lose the ability to thrive on plant roots. The population never grows large enough to provide meaningful nitrogen in the field. The solution became the inspiration for the company’s name. “At Switch Bioworks, we have developed genetic ‘switches’ that delay fertilizer production,” Schnabel says. “After application, the microbes spend their first few weeks colonizing plant roots and building a large population. Once enough microbes have established themselves, the genetic switch activates, directing the bacteria to begin producing ammonia.” This sophisticated genetic control system provides the timing that allows for establishing the microbial population first before fertilizer production takes place. “This technology is still in the research and development stage,” Schnabel says. “Lab studies have produced encouraging results, and field testing is now underway. We completed extensive safety testing to obtain approvals from both the Environmental Protection Agency and the U.S. Department of Agriculture.” Researchers are now evaluating how well the microbes establish themselves on crop roots, whether the genetic switches activate at the proper time and how consistently the system performs under real-world field conditions. “Each growing season provides valuable information that helps us refine the product,” Schnabel says. “We’ll continue generating data until we have a package that shows commercially reliable performance for farmers. We hope to bring the first commercial product to market within the next two to three years.” Farmers are ready Interest from growers has been strong. Several farmers have already invested in Switch Bioworks, and the company is assembling a grower advisory council while signing up early adopters for future commercial launches. “Much of the enthusiasm stems from repeated fertilizer supply crises,” Schnabel says. “There’s always another crisis around the corner. Farmers want a solution that isn’t tied to geopolitics or natural gas.” While growers have seen many biological products come and go, Schnabel believes Switch Bioworks is fundamentally different because the team rebuilt the technology from the ground up instead of making incremental improvements. “One question I hear most often is, ‘When can I have it?’” he says. Starting with corn The company’s first commercial focus will be Midwest corn production, where nitrogen fertilizer represents one of the largest input costs. Once the technology has proven itself, Switch Bioworks plans to expand into additional crops. Ultimately, Schnabel envisions a future in which living microbes reduce fertilizer costs while making agriculture more productive, resilient and environmentally sustainable. “For me, it’s more than science. Other people have dogs. I have a couple hundred plants. When I wake up and see a healthy new leaf, it makes my day. That lifelong fascination and my expertise in biotechnology inspired me to pursue creation of a cleaner, more affordable way to feed crops and, ultimately, the world.” Related Environmental and economic benefits of nitrogen efficiency Reducing hay costs in a down year: Where to cut and where not to cut Manure application strategies for efficiency and environmental benefit Loretta Sorensen Freelance Writer Loretta Sorensen is a freelance writer based in South Dakota.
