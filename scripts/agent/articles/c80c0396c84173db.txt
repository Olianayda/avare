Cattle Q&A with Chris Ashworth, Zinpro Tyrell Marchant September 14, 2026 Chris Ashworth is the beef discovery researcher for Zinpro Corporation, with nearly 40 years of experience in cattle medicine and nutrition as a livestock veterinarian. He directs research and technical nutritional services for the Zinpro beef team worldwide. What is bluetongue, and how does it affect cattle? ASHWORTH:  Bluetongue is an infectious, noncontagious, vector-borne disease that affects wild and domestic ruminants including cattle, deer, sheep, goats, buffalo and many species of African ruminants. Most often, the virus is spread from cow to cow by biting insects such as culicoides mosquitoes. What are the symptoms of bluetongue? ASHWORTH:  Most of the time there are little to no symptoms of the disease in cattle. However, when outbreaks do occur, there can be a significant amount of death. The disease can be viremic in cattle for longer than six months, which makes it easy to overwinter in animals and see new clinical cases in the spring not long after mosquitoes have come back. This also makes the disease hard to control. The disease can also be spread by needles being reused from animal to animal, moving infected blood from one animal to another animal. There are 26 strains of the disease, making it hard to control as vaccines must be specific for the strains that are found. In cattle, the clinical signs that can occur vary widely, depending on which strain of the virus they are infected with. The disease causes a vasculitis or inflammation of the blood vessels. This is why it is called bluetongue; it causes swelling in the blood vessels of the tongue, leading to a color change to blue. In feedlot cattle, you can see swelling in all four limbs, lameness and decreased feed intake. Animals are lethargic, run high fevers and can also be found dead bleeding from the nose, mouth and eyes. Animals can have ulcers in their mouth and esophagus, with bleeding. Cattle can also have excessive salivation, nasal discharge and swelling of the tongue, lips and jaw. Because their mouth is so painful, you can see rapid weight loss, depression and weakness. Profuse diarrhea can occur depending on which strain of the virus comes into cattle and sheep. In sheep, abortions commonly occur. Advertisement In infected cattle, mortality can run from 2% to 30%. Bluetongue is usually seen during and after significant rainfall when mosquitoes’ numbers start to increase significantly. Is bluetongue ever confused with any other diseases? How can I be sure it is or isn’t bluetongue? ASHWORTH:  Due to the oral lesions, it can be confused with foot-and-mouth disease, BVD (bovine viral diarrhea) and, in deer, epizootic hemorrhagic disease. How does bluetongue spread? Are there areas of the country where it is more prevalent than others?  ASHWORTH:  The disease can be found anywhere in the world in which culicoides mosquitoes are found and infected animals are in the same area. It can be found in areas as far north as Scandinavia. What steps can producers take to prevent bluetongue, and how can they treat it if it is diagnosed in their herd? ASHWORTH:  To help control bluetongue, three things are commonly used. First, feed a high level of zinc amino acid complex to help reduce viral replication of the virus. Secondly, control mosquitoes and, if possible, mosquito breeding areas. Lastly, vaccinate for bluetongue. When vaccinating, you must use a vaccine that contains the same strain of the virus that’s circulating in the animals. Can animals infected with bluetongue still enter the food supply? ASHWORTH:  Yes, animals infected with bluetongue can enter the food chain if they are not running a fever above the limits set by USDA’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS), and if the animals are ambulatory and can enter the slaughterhouse under their own power.
