Preg check early, cull strategically: Using pregnancy diagnosis to improve herd profitability Kacie McCarthy September 15, 2026 Preg-checking cows is only the first step to making herd decisions. Consider other factors such as cow utility, resource availability and replacement goals before making culling and reproductive decisions that will have a long-term impact on your cow herd. As summer turns to fall, producers begin evaluating forage supplies, winter feed inventories and herd management plans for the coming year. Pregnancy diagnosis can provide valuable information to guide those decisions while improving both short- and long-term profitability. As producers weigh herd rebuilding opportunities against the value of selling cull cows, early pregnancy detection can help determine which females warrant further investment and which should be marketed. Know what you have Pregnancy diagnosis provides information well beyond a simple bred-or-open answer. Pregnancy can be detected through rectal palpation, ultrasound or blood tests as early as 28 to 45 days after breeding, depending on the method used ( Table 1 ). In addition to determining pregnancy status, palpation and ultrasound can provide an estimate of fetal age, allowing producers to predict calving dates and identify females that conceived early or late in the breeding season. Knowing where females fall within the calving distribution provides valuable insight into reproductive performance. Research has shown that females that conceive early in the breeding season are more likely to remain in the herd longer and continue calving early in subsequent years. Their calves are also generally older and heavier at weaning, increasing the cumulative pounds of calf weaned over the female’s productive lifetime. In contrast, late-bred females often face challenges staying within a defined calving season or resulting in a greater proportion falling out of the herd over time. Identifying those differences allows producers to make more informed management decisions. Advertisement Look beyond pregnancy status Use pregnancy status, performance and functionality to identify the females most likely to strengthen the herd. Open females are typically the first culling candidates because they utilize feed, pasture, labor and management resources without producing a calf the following year. Identifying and marketing these cows before winter feeding begins can reduce feed costs and free up resources for productive animals.  While pregnancy status is important, not every pregnant cow deserves to remain in the herd. Late-bred cows wean younger calves and have less time to rebreed before the next breeding season. Over time, these females frequently remain at the tail end of the calving distribution, may contribute fewer pounds of calf during their productive lives and often fall out of the herd. Production records and physical condition should also guide culling decisions. Females that consistently wean below-average calves, struggle to maintain body condition, require repeated assistance with calving or have problems with feet, legs, udders, eyes, health or disposition may not contribute enough to herd productivity to justify continued investment. At the same time, producers should identify the females most likely to support long-term herd improvement – cows and heifers that conceive early, calve early, have more time to resume cycling and are more likely to remain productive members of the herd. Cow family performance can also guide replacement selection. Daughters of cows that consistently breed early, maintain body condition and wean quality calves may possess the genetics and adaptability needed for long-term success. When evaluating females, consider pregnancy status, expected calving date, past performance and production history, structural soundness and available resources. If herd reductions are necessary, priority should be given to productive, fertile and functional females while lower-priority animals are marketed before feed resources become limiting. Make resources match the herd Strategic culling helps protect profitability when feed or forage is limited. Pregnancy diagnosis can also create marketing opportunities. Open females identified in late summer or early fall may be marketed immediately or placed on a feeding program depending on market conditions. Likewise, bred females with known calving dates may command additional value because buyers have greater confidence in their production stage. Open replacement heifers also deserve timely evaluation. Depending on available feed and market conditions, these females may be marketed immediately, retained as feeders or managed separately from bred replacements. Early pregnancy diagnosis creates flexibility and allows producers to make decisions before winter expenses begin to accumulate. Conclusion Pregnancy diagnosis is far more than a method for identifying open cows. It provides information that can guide culling decisions, replacement selection, feeding strategies and marketing opportunities. By identifying open females early, evaluating calving distribution, retaining productive cow families and protecting high-quality replacements, producers can use pregnancy diagnosis to improve both immediate profitability and long-term herd performance. The most successful operations are not simply reducing cow numbers, they are improving the quality of the females that remain. When producers pregnancy check early and cull strategically, they position their herds for greater productivity, longevity and profitability in the years ahead.
