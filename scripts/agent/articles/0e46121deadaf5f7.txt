In the dairy industry, consistency is one of the most reliable predictors of success. We build feeding programs around it. We design milking routines around it. We train employees to follow it. And we know cows thrive because of it. It could be more than you think Yet when it comes to hoof care, consistency can be one of the first things sacrificed. After working in the hoof care industry for two decades, I've had the opportunity to work with dairies of all sizes. One lesson has become clear: The dairies that achieve the best hoof health outcomes are not necessarily the ones spending the most money or making the most adjustments. They are the ones that commit to a proven protocol and execute it consistently over time. When it comes to managing digital dermatitis (DD), success is rarely about finding the newest product or making the latest adjustment. It's about building a program that works and having the discipline to stick with it. DD isn’t a one-time problem One of the biggest hoof health challenges facing commercial dairies today is DD. Since its introduction into commercial dairy herds decades ago, DD has become an issue that virtually every producer has encountered. Advertisement Unfortunately, once DD becomes established in a herd, it is not something that can simply be eliminated. The bacteria associated with DD can persist within the environment and hide within hoof tissues, creating ongoing opportunities for reinfection. The reality is that DD must be managed – not cured. Fortunately, the industry has learned a great deal about controlling the disease. Consistent footbath programs, proper product application and ongoing monitoring can dramatically reduce lesion prevalence and lameness levels. Consistency is the difference between control and crisis One of the most important principles in managing DD is understanding that success does not mean the disease has been eliminated. Even when active lesions appear to be under control, treponema bacteria remain present within the herd, often in dormant stages that are not immediately visible. As a result, a herd is never truly cured of DD – it is continuously managed. This reality makes consistency in preventive protocols, particularly footbathing, essential to long-term herd health. Stopping a footbath program after achieving good results creates significant risk because the underlying reservoirs of infection remain. Many cows may still carry dormant infections beneath the skin, even when no clinical signs are evident. At the same time, the protective benefits provided by regular footbathing begin to disappear. The hardened, more resilient skin created through consistent use of footbath products gradually softens, leaving the hoof vulnerable to bacterial invasion. The consequences of inconsistency are often hidden at first. When preventive measures are removed, dormant infections can slowly reactivate without obvious symptoms. By the time lameness becomes visible, infection may already have spread throughout a large portion of the herd. What appeared to be a cost-saving decision can quickly become an expensive outbreak requiring intensive treatment, increased labor and a much more aggressive footbath schedule to regain control. The lesson for managers is clear: Consistency is more efficient than recovery. Rather than stopping a successful program, effective operations continuously adapt it. Footbath frequency can be scaled based on herd conditions, increasing during higher-risk periods such as wet seasons or poor hygiene conditions and transitioning to a maintenance schedule when disease prevalence is low. The key is maintaining the discipline to keep preventive systems in place because long-term success comes not from eliminating risk but from consistently managing it. The three worst reasons to change a hoof care program Not all change is bad. However, some reasons for changing protocols usually deliver disappointing results. 1. Chasing the cheapest chemical Price is often one of the first reasons producers consider making a change. The challenge is that chemical cost represents only a small portion of the total economics of lameness. A dairy might save money on product costs while unknowingly increasing expenses associated with milk loss, reproduction challenges, labor and cow turnover. The lowest-cost program is not always the lowest-cost solution. The better question is: What is the cost of an additional lame cow? It is more work to pencil the long-term costs, but it is like stepping over dollars to pick up pennies just because the dollar has a little manure on it. 2. Cutting back because things look better This may be the most common mistake I encounter. As a dairy intensifies its hoof care efforts, DD lesions improve and lameness declines. Then the temptation begins. "Maybe we can cut back a day." "Maybe we don't need copper this week." "Maybe we can save a little money." Before long, the preventive program that created the improvement is reduced. A few weeks or months later, lesions begin increasing again. Success often creates the temptation to abandon the very practices that produced it. Don’t get caught in this trap. 3. Reacting instead of managing The most challenged dairies often operate reactively. When hoof care decisions are driven by the latest outbreak, latest lesion count or latest frustration, consistency disappears. Programs become a series of short-term responses rather than a long-term strategy. The result is often the same vicious cycle:
Increase treatment frequency
Reduce treatment frequency
Change products
Change suppliers
Repeat
Meanwhile, the cows never experience the stability needed for sustained hoof health improvement. Consistency doesn’t mean doing nothing It is important to clarify that consistency should never be confused with complacency. If lameness is increasing, DD lesions are expanding or performance goals are not being achieved, changes may absolutely be necessary. The difference is whether those changes are being made strategically or emotionally. The best dairies use data. They evaluate lesion prevalence. They monitor lameness scores. They seek guidance from trusted advisers. Then they make informed decisions and execute consistently. That is very different from constantly adjusting a program based on the crisis of the week. Lasting hoof health requires more than the right chemistry Successful producers understand that DD is a management challenge that requires continuous attention ( Table 1 ). They rely on proven systems, measuring performance and staying consistent in their approach. The goal isn't simply to treat lame cows – it is to create an environment where lameness becomes the exception rather than the norm. When it comes to hoof health, consistency isn't just part of the solution; it is the foundation of it. The strongest hoof care partnerships extend beyond product recommendations. A trusted provider should help evaluate facilities, assess cow flow, review protocols, identify opportunities for improvement and deliver solutions that fit the unique realities of each dairy. They bring experience, expertise and accountability to an area of the business that can have a significant impact on profitability and animal well-being. After all, lameness is often one of the largest controllable expenses on a dairy. In today’s dairy industry, there is pressure to optimize every dollar spent. That's understandable. But when evaluating hoof care programs, producers should remember that the cost of change can sometimes be far greater than the investment in consistent prevention. Beyond economics, there is another reason consistency matters. The consequences of lameness are ultimately experienced by the cow. Preventing painful hoof lesions helps support comfort, mobility and overall well-being while reinforcing the standards of care that dairy producers take pride in providing every day. Consistent hoof care isn't just about protecting production; it is about protecting cows and helping sustain the trust that supports our industry.
