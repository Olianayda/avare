World Dairy Expo: Opening new doors for dairy Patryce Eldredge LeFevre September 26, 2026 Progressive Dairy spoke with three individuals whose careers have been greatly influenced by opportunities at World Dairy Expo. All throughout the dairy industry, one can find opportunity to learn, network and build upon their careers in the industry. But there is no place quite like World Dairy Expo, where the doors to opportunity are limitless. Progressive Dairy spoke with three individuals who can attest that their involvement at the show in Madison, Wisconsin, opened doors to learn, network and develop careers they are proud of – from a cattle fitter to breeder and educator. Here are their stories. At just 22 years old, he has made a name and built a business for himself as a fitter, a journey that was spurred and has been supported by World Dairy Expo. Welk grew up showing dairy cattle and attended his first World Dairy Expo in 2021, where he showed his first animal on the colored shavings, placing in the top 10 of the junior show. “I always dreamed of going to World Dairy Expo,” Welk says. “It was just amazing seeing all the cows you read about, and then being able to show my own heifer out there was pretty cool too.” While at World Dairy Expo that year, Welk had the opportunity to help clip the legs and heads of some cattle. That experience sparked an interest in fitting that would eventually become his passion and career. Advertisement “I just love the art of fitting,” Welk says. “I love being able to look at a cow, see her flaws and be able to know how to fix that, or see what’s good about a cow and be able to emphasize it.” That attention to detail came naturally to Welk. He says he was always a perfectionist when he was younger and wanted to hire a professional fitter to clip his animals rather than do it himself. “That inspired me,” Welk says. “I gained an appreciation and interest for fitting. Then I just built on the little that I knew, built upon different interactions with people and working with other fitters.” Kyle Welk won the Merle Howard Award in 2025, presented to an accomplished youth exhibitor at World Dairy Expo. Courtesy image. As Welk developed his skills, he also found people willing to help him along the way. He says his parents and family have been tremendous sources of support. When he was new to fitting and still learning, Tom Arrowsmith with Hillacres Jerseys became a valuable mentor, someone Welk could turn to for advice. Those early opportunities and relationships helped Welk turn an interest into a career. Today, he not only clips cattle at World Dairy Expo but is also a self-employed, full-time fitter, clipping cattle for sales and shows across the U.S., Canada and New Zealand on behalf of many respected show strings in the business. He has established business connections, clientele and a full schedule. Welk’s skill and success have not gone unnoticed. In 2024, he was named the senior male division winner in the World Dairy Expo fitting contest. That same year, he received the Micheal Heath Overall Fitter Award as the overall winner of the contest. “It was one of my biggest accomplishments,” Welk says. “I just had a lot of fun doing it.” In 2025, Welk was also presented the Merle Howard Award, given to an accomplished youth exhibitor in honor of longtime World Dairy Expo volunteer Merle Howard. As Welk continues his work as a fitter, he hopes to take the skills he has learned and apply them to something he can continue to develop into a career. Along the way, he wants to give back and help others develop their skills, just as he has been supported and mentored throughout his own journey. Pinzón-Sánchez learns alongside producers through presentation opportunities Carolina Pinzón-Sánchez, a bilingual dairy outreach specialist with the University of Wisconsin – Madison, is a key contributor to Expo en Español. During her 24 years in Wisconsin, she has attended nearly every World Dairy Expo. She began as a translator for international groups visiting the event and, over the years, has had the opportunity to contribute as an educator and presenter. In her role as a bilingual outreach specialist, Pinzón-Sánchez helps dairy teams improve farm efficiency, performance and long-term sustainability through bilingual education programs and presentations focused on milk quality, udder health, animal welfare and milking systems. Through Expo en Español, Spanish-speaking members of the community gain access to the same high-quality, evidence-based education offered throughout the rest of the event. Carolina Pinzón-Sánchez is a presenter for Expo en Español. She has participated at World Dairy Expo for nearly 24 years. Courtesy image. Preparing and presenting With her audience in mind, Pinzón-Sánchez prepares presentations that are practical and engaging, with a focus on translating research into ideas and practices people can immediately apply to their farms. During her educational presentations, she aims to help dairy farm owners and managers view education as an ongoing investment, rather than something that happens only after problems arise. She says training should be proactive, helping employees feel valued, respected and prepared to succeed. “There is nothing more rewarding than seeing someone experience an ‘aha’ moment and truly understand the reason behind a management practice, whether it involves mastitis prevention, milking procedures or animal welfare,” Pinzón-Sánchez says. “Knowing that a presentation can help improve farm performance, animal care or employee engagement makes every opportunity worthwhile.” Learning with attendees While presenting, Pinzón-Sánchez says the energy and engagement of the Spanish-speaking audience are memorable. Attendees ask questions, share experiences and participate in discussions. She especially enjoys what happens after the session ends. “Some of the best conversations take place in the hallway, where producers and farm employees share their experiences, ask follow-up questions and discuss ideas they want to try on their farms,” Pinzón-Sánchez says. “I learn as much from producers' real-world experiences as they learn from the research and educational resources I share.” As a global gathering place for the dairy industry, Pinzón-Sánchez says one of the greatest opportunities of presenting at World Dairy Expo has been collaborating with extension colleagues from other states. These collaborations have led to the exchange of ideas, resources and solutions to common industry challenges. “Many of these connections continue long after World Dairy Expo ends and often lead to new collaborations and educational initiatives,” she says. For instance, Pinzón-Sánchez developed a bilingual trainer certification program called Milk Quality from the Udder World. The program equips farm trainers with the knowledge, tools and confidence they need to effectively train their teams. She says the program was inspired by many of the conversations she’s had at World Dairy Expo. For Pinzón-Sánchez, World Dairy Expo opens doors for networking, education and friendships. “Presenting at World Dairy Expo has expanded my professional network far beyond Wisconsin,” Pinzón-Sánchez says. “It has connected me with dairy producers, educators, veterinarians, industry leaders and organizations that share a commitment to advancing the dairy industry.” Abbott's career in the dairy industry is supported by visibility as a dairy cattle exhibitor As a World Dairy Expo veteran, Tim Abbott has exhibited cattle at the event for years. Together, he and his wife, Sharyn, have built careers in the dairy cattle industry and have started several successful business ventures, including Borderview Genetics and Showbox Sires. Abbott’s first introduction to World Dairy Expo came as a contestant in the national 4-H judging contest. As a 17-year-old who had not seen much of the world, he remembers the experience vividly and recalls seeing Brown Swiss cattle from Voegeli Farms and also the renowned Brookview Tony Charity. “I remember seeing that cow on the grounds and saying, ‘There’s something really cool about this business,’” Abbott says. Abbott returned to World Dairy Expo as a cattle exhibitor nearly 25 years ago and has exhibited there almost every year since. He notes visibility at the event is a large part of his business presence and success. The Abbott family – (left to right) Chelsea, Tim, Caitlyn and Sharyn – and their business partners – Mike and Julie Duckett with their children Chloe and Logan – stand with Oakfield Solomon Footloose, their two-time World Dairy Expo Grand Champion. Courtesy image. “It's just a huge jumping board for us to get exposure, and obviously your success in the ring itself makes a difference,” Abbott says. “When you leave there, it’s having people recognize your cows and your genetics, and wanting them. We sell genetics all year long because of World Dairy Expo.” Abbott knows firsthand the thrill that comes with achieving success at the event, as he has been part of three Grand Champion Holsteins at the show. However, he also knows there is more to exhibiting cattle at World Dairy Expo than the medals and banners. “We truly use World Dairy Expo to help market our cattle,” Abbott says. “It’s a very intense time for us because it’s our biggest marketing event of the year for our cattle.” One of Abbott’s main goals when exhibiting cattle is to ensure the cattle are always presented well. He says exhibitors should remember that the ultimate customer is always watching, making it imperative for animals to make a good first impression. Abbott notes that the display, while sometimes overlooked, is a valuable component. He encourages signage that highlights the farm and the animals being exhibited, describing it as a critical piece of promotion for both farms and their cattle. Abbott says his favorite part of World Dairy Expo is the people, and those relationships keep him returning to the show year after year. He meets individuals from across the globe, many of whom become lifelong friends and valuable business connections. “World Dairy Expo has really spurred our business because we now have dozens and dozens of meetings with people from around the world who want to buy U.S. genetics, or in particular our genetics, whether it's embryos, semen or live cattle,” Abbott says.
