Identifying which is happening matters: fixing the real imbalance boosts milk protein yield, cuts feed costs and reduces nitrogen excretion. Milk urea nitrogen (MUN) has long been recognized as an important diagnostic tool, and many producers strive to maintain bulk tank MUN below 14 and often even below 12 milligrams per decilitre. Higher levels are frequently interpreted to mean that either rumen-available nitrogen or overall dietary protein is excessive. Yet high MUN can also mean cows are lacking one or more nutrients they need to produce milk protein. Essi Evans Dairy Nutritionist / E&E Technical Advisory Services Inc. This relationship was highlighted in a 2003 Utah State University study that analyzed more than 75,000 monthly DHI records collected over 24 months from herds in Utah, Idaho and Montana. Unexpectedly, lower MUN was associated with higher milk protein percentage, suggesting that lower MUN may reflect more efficient use of dietary protein. This paradoxical finding suggests that high MUN may indicate that nutrients are missing from the diet and points to the need for a closer look at the diet formulation. Where does MUN come from? It’s certainly true that oversupplying protein increases MUN. If too much rumen-degradable protein (RUP) is provided relative to what the rumen microbes can use, the excess crosses the rumen wall and travels to the liver, where it is converted to less toxic urea. The urea enters the blood, equilibrates with milk and is ultimately excreted in urine ( Figure 1 ).  It is also correct that providing too much protein in the diet increases MUN. Amino acids cannot be stored. Excess protein is broken down so that the cow can salvage energy from it, while the excess nitrogen, in the form of ammonia, is delivered to the liver for conversion to less toxic urea. MUN as an efficiency gauge Recent research helps explain how high MUN values may point to ration imbalances. One key factor is energy. Advertisement In the rumen, microbes need both nitrogen and energy to grow and synthesize microbial protein. If fermentable carbohydrate is limiting, or not available when rumen ammonia levels are high, microbes cannot capture rumen ammonia to support growth. As a result, microbial protein production falls, and more nitrogen is absorbed from the rumen and converted to urea, which can ultimately be reflected in higher MUN.  Carbohydrate supply also supports energy needs beyond the rumen. Fermentation provides propionate for glucose production, which is essential for milk lactose synthesis and, therefore, milk yield. When energy or glucose precursors are inadequate, absorbed amino acids may be preferentially oxidized for energy rather than preserved for milk protein synthesis, increasing nitrogen loss. As a result, high MUN can reflect too much crude protein, too little usable carbohydrate or poor synchronization between the two. Energy balance is therefore an essential first check. The other major factor is amino acid balance. Even when the metabolizable protein supply appears adequate, milk protein synthesis can be restricted by a single limiting amino acid. Think of milk protein synthesis as an assembly line: Every amino acid is a required part. If one part, such as methionine, is missing, production slows even when all the other parts are available. Excess amino acids cannot be stored, so they are broken down. Their carbon can be used for energy, while their nitrogen is converted to urea, causing MUN to rise. In a Virginia Tech study, high-producing cows received a control treatment or a continuous infusion of 12 grams per day of methionine and 21 grams per day of lysine ( Table 1 ). Supplying these two amino acids increased milk protein concentration and yield while lowering MUN from 12.4 to 11.8 milligrams per decilitre. Milk yield also increased numerically. The response shows how a relatively small change in amino acid supply can redirect nitrogen from urea toward productive protein synthesis. MUN is strongly related to urinary nitrogen excretion because circulating urea is cleared primarily through the kidneys. Wisconsin research estimated urinary urea nitrogen as MUN × 16.23 − 34.2 grams per cow per day, or approximately 16 grams more urinary urea nitrogen for every 1 milligram per decilitre increase in MUN within the range studied. Urinary nitrogen is especially important environmentally because it can be rapidly converted to ammonia and can contribute to nitrous oxide losses after manure handling and land application. If amino acid balance influences MUN, then feed ingredients supplying a more balanced amino acid profile should improve nitrogen utilization. A feeding trial conducted by Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada illustrates how feed source can shift this outcome. Cows in the study received isonitrogenous diets (16% crude protein) containing zero, 8%, 16% or 24% canola meal in place of soybean meal and soybean hulls ( Table 2 ). As canola meal inclusion increased, milk and protein yields rose, MUN declined, and a smaller share of consumed nitrogen was excreted in urine. Using MUN as a diagnostic tool As a cost-effective and straightforward measure of nutritional status, MUN is most useful at the herd level when interpreted alongside milk yield, milk protein and dry matter intake. It should not be treated as a stand-alone assessment on dietary protein. The goal of monitoring MUN is to identify why nitrogen is escaping productive use. The checklist below can serve as a guide:
Crude protein supply and degradability: Is total protein excessive, or is rumen-degradable protein being supplied faster than microbes can use it?
Fermentable carbohydrate: Is there enough non-fibre carbohydrate to capture rumen ammonia and support microbial protein synthesis?
Amino acid balance: Could methionine, lysine or another essential amino acid be limiting milk protein synthesis?
Production context: Are milk yield, milk protein yield, intake and days in milk being considered alongside the MUN result?
Each 1-point reduction in MUN can reflect more efficient nitrogen use, with less nitrogen lost in urine and more available to support productive functions such as growth or milk protein synthesis. By monitoring MUN concentrations, dairy producers can manage feeding programs more effectively, control production costs and reduce environmental nitrogen losses.
