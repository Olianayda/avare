Image by Anna Brown, courtesy of Brown Enterprise . 3B Brown Ranch: Grass, cattle and opportunity Loretta Sorensen September 18, 2026 3B Brown Ranch turns productive forage, careful management and diversified enterprises into a resilient family operation. Cattle are the main enterprise at Ardmore, Oklahoma’s 3B Brown Ranch; however, this family operation also sells hay, occasional crops and hunting leases, and it operates a profitable agritourism business, the 3B Brown Ranch Hay Maze. Loretta Sorensen Freelance Writer Loretta Sorensen is a freelance writer based in South Dakota. Related Rising Sun Ranch: Managing for grass, wildlife and generations Good cattle, dependable horses: Wowk Ranch Across the Fence: A typical ranch day Larry Brown explains that ranching has been part of the family for generations, extending back far enough that there’s no record of exactly how far back its ranching history extends. “My father and grandfather are among two of the generations that managed the ranch," Brown says. “My grandfather established the first 300 acres of the current ranch. Since that time, as the operation generated income, the family has expanded to just under 5,000 acres spread among its properties.” Brown’s son Cody represents the next generation of the family operation. Brown believes that the agricultural economics degree Cody earned at Oklahoma State University is particularly valuable training for managing the business side of ranching. “I consider financial management one of the greatest challenges facing today’s ranchers,” Brown says. “Producers must pay close attention to loans, interest costs and the overall financial position of the operation. We have tried to stay ahead financially by watching numbers closely and limiting debt.” Advertisement When he inherited ranch land from his father and grandfather, Brown’s land was debt-free. Rather than heavily leveraging those assets, the family has reinvested money generated by the operation into additional land and cattle. Large round hay bales – approximately 1.5 to 2.5 million pounds – form the popular hay maze. Image courtesy of Brown Enterprise by Anna Brown. “Controlling assets without a mortgage provides an important advantage in the ranching business,” he says. “Excessive debt can put an operation at serious risk.” Brown describes the family’s Noble Line cattle breed. “It’s a three-way composite breed developed through the Samuel Roberts Noble Foundation in the 1980s,” Brown says. “I became familiar with the breed while studying agriculture at Oklahoma State University and completing an internship at the Noble Foundation.” Brown worked with Jim Pumphrey, who developed the breed, and Brown has continued working with Noble Line cattle since that time. “The composite includes Brahman, Angus [red and black] and Gelbvieh genetics,” Brown says. “We value the three-way cross because it retains hybrid vigor while combining useful traits from each breed. The Angus and Gelbvieh influence contribute to meat, muscle and milk, while the Brahman genetics add hardiness, longevity and heat tolerance, which are important characteristics in southern Oklahoma.” The ranch sells bulls and other breeding stock from its herd, but Brown sees forage production as the foundation of the ranch’s beef enterprise. “Ranchers aren’t simply selling cattle,” he says. “In a practical sense, they are selling the grass that ultimately produces those cattle. Better forage supports the production of better-quality stock.” Among the forage types utilized on the ranch are improved bermudagrasses, such as Midland 99 and Tifton varieties. Weed control is an important element of maintaining productive grass stands. Rotational grazing is also central to the ranch forage program. Fences divide pastures so cattle can be moved efficiently among grazing areas. A combination of grazing management and limited chemical control is used to manage weeds. A visitor has upside down fun at 3B Brown Ranch .  Image courtesy of Brown Enterprise by Anna Brown. “We aren’t heavy chemical users,” Brown says. “When herbicide is needed, 2,4-D is a primary tool for us. We change or adjust products as necessary.” Timing and grazing pressure play a key role in the ranch’s forage production. Grazing relatively high numbers of cattle on smaller areas at the proper stage of plant growth can encourage cattle to consume weeds along with desirable forage. “In this way,” Brown says, “well-managed rotational grazing becomes part of the weed control strategy rather than relying exclusively on herbicides.” When the ranch puts up hay, most of it is good quality bermudagrass. Johnsongrass and bluestem are also harvested, but bermudagrass makes up the majority of the hay supply. Brown also considers wildlife management important to the ranch, especially since they offer annual hunting lease options. “Deer, turkey and other wildlife help create hunting lease income,” he says. “Our family works to control trespassing and to protect wildlife resources. This is one additional source of revenue and further diversifies our operation.” The 3B Brown Ranch Hay Maze is widely known in the region, drawing visitors from as much as 100 miles away each October. The agritourism activity grew out of a former corn maze the Browns established on one of their properties. “We had a corn maze on some of our former peanut fields, but corn didn’t always produce a dependable maze,” Brown says. “It was also difficult for visitors to find the field.” Since the family owned another ranch along the highway south of Ardmore, where plenty of hay was available, they decided to build a maze from round bales instead. Young visitors enjoy the farm-built equipment at 3B Brown Ranch. Image courtesy of Brown Enterprise by Anna Brown. “The maze uses an enormous amount of hay to create the course,” Brown says. “Approximately 1.5 million to 2.5 million pounds. Over time, we have expanded the attraction to far beyond the maze itself.” Visitors can enjoy hayrides, campfires, hot dogs, chips, drinks and s’mores. Other attractions include zip lines, a kiddie corral, a three-story castle, concession stand, rustic photo area and an old town setting. The property also features slides, tetherball, “Big Country” basketball, a boot-walk bridge and a 54-foot inflatable jump pillow. “We just operate during October,” Brown says. “Each year, we draw thousands of visitors. We believe the success of the hay maze is due to the accessible location, activities that appeal to a broad range of ages and continual reinvestment. Each year, a portion of the profit goes back into additional attractions intended to increase attendance.” The hay maze wasn’t immediately a profitable venture. The first five years were difficult, what Brown says were “closer to break-even years.” Profitability improved as the business became established and additional activities were added. “Anyone entering agritourism needs to understand the economics before they open,” Brown advises. “Owners should know how much revenue the business must generate to cover expenses and leave a net profit. Without that understanding, a new agritourism venture can quickly encounter financial trouble. Create experiences that appeal to a broad audience rather than limiting attraction to a narrow group.” Visitors navigate the climbing protion of the hay bale obstacle course at 3B Brown Ranch. Image courtesy of Brown Enterprise by Anna Brown. During October, the Brown family hires employees to assist with hay maze activities. Brown jokes that ranch employees only have to work “half a day – the half when the sun is up.” His humor reflects both the long hours required in ranching and his good-natured approach to the work. General admission is $15, with group rates available. Hay maze hours are Fridays 5 p.m. to 9 p.m. and Saturday/Sunday 2 p.m. to 9 p.m. For more information, visit the website . “Faith is also central to our family’s view of the ranch and its success,” Brown says. “There’s a difference between being rich and being blessed. God provides assets so they can be used to help others. We are grateful for the blessings our family has received, and we recognize those blessings carry responsibilities.” Brown notes that life’s most important inheritance is not land, cattle or material wealth. “A person cannot take a fence post, a spoonful of dirt, a steak or even a ranch brand with him when he dies,” Brown says. “What matters is the spiritual legacy passed on to the next generation. More important than passing along the ranch itself is passing along a godly belief and faith that will remain with the family.” Related Rising Sun Ranch: Managing for grass, wildlife and generations Good cattle, dependable horses: Wowk Ranch Across the Fence: A typical ranch day Loretta Sorensen Freelance Writer Loretta Sorensen is a freelance writer based in South Dakota.
