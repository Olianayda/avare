Home &raquo; Blogs &raquo; Irons in the Fire &raquo; Irons in the Fire: Wendell’s paint horse Irons in the Fire: Wendell’s paint horse Paul Marchant August 31, 2026 Wendell was a big man, well over 6 feet tall, broad-shouldered and thick-built with a mildly stooped and lumbering gait when he walked. He was about the same age as my father, and his kids were around my age, so I always considered him kind of an old guy. I never would have thought him to be a real top hand, but he surely did love his cows and his horses. As often as not, I believe he fed with a team throughout the winter months. I didn’t know him when he was a young man, but apparently he was, like practically all of us back in the day, a decent to good athlete in his school days. Whether that was true or not, I’m uncertain. To look at him when I knew him, you wouldn’t have put much stock in his athletic prowess. The genetics must have been there, though, because some of his kids and grandkids proved to be quite athletically gifted, proving their mettle on the field, on the court and in the rodeo arena. Over the past two or three decades, since I didn’t often venture back to my hometown, I’d only seen Wendell a few times. I sold him a couple of dogs once, but other than that, pretty much my only connection to him – or most of the people from my formative years, for that matter – was through the occasional social media interaction. It was through social media that I learned of Wendell’s passing a few months ago. I took note of the particulars and tuned into the livestream of the memorial services. It was quite a pleasant affair, as far as funerals go – not much sadness, mostly friends and family reminiscing about the good times they’d shared with Wendell. Most entertaining to me were the thoughts offered up by each of his 17 grandchildren as they paraded up to the pulpit to share a thought or two about their grandfather. Most of the kids, depending on their age, simply shared a one- or two-sentence anecdote. Some of the older grandkids, however, went into a little more detail, some of it quite entertaining, about their memories of Grandpa Wendell. One of the grandsons, in his early 20s, is pretty handy with horses and has had some notable success as a tie-down roper. He related a story about his grandfather and what turned out to be his favorite horse. Advertisement It seems that Wendell, though according to his progeny was not a real handy horseman, always loved paint horses and had apparently bought a fancy overo gelding that was supposed to be the perfect horse for him to ride off into the sunset. But the horse wasn’t quite perfect. He came with one flaw: He was hard to catch. One day, Wendell summoned his grandson to the house. He wanted to go ride through a little herd of cows out in the back forty. The dutiful grandson offered to go catch the horses. He got the ponies up into the half-acre pasture behind the barn with no incident, but that’s where the difficulties began. Ol’ Paint refused to be caught. The young cowboy couldn’t even get close enough to flip a houlihan loop over his head. After a spell, Wendell came sauntering out of the house, wondering what the delay was. Upon observing the goings-on, he disappeared into the shop. Moments later he emerged, roaring out on his four-wheeler ATV. He entered the little pasture and, despite his grandson’s admonition to come up with a better plan, proceeded to “encourage” his prized pony from behind, allowing the free-spirited horse to run around the perimeter of the pasture as much as he wanted. Round and round they went for 10 or 15 minutes until the pretty paint had decided that the jig was up. He finally stopped and faced up to Wendell, who gave the go-ahead for his grandson to halter him and lead him over to the tack shed. The younger man, having been versed through various means in the finer points of horsemanship, was not a fan of his grandfather’s tactics and was convinced it was a one-off deal. About a week or two later, Wendell called him. They needed to ride through the cows again. Filled with doubt, he honored his grandfather’s wishes and hopped on the four-wheeler to roust the horses up into the catch pen. To his great surprise and delight, once inside the small pasture, the paint gelding stopped just inside the gate and stood at attention like the King’s Guard at Buckingham Palace, flinching not one iota until he was haltered, at which point, he continued to behave like a perfect gentleman. It appeared that he was now completely “four-wheeler broke,” as he remains to this day. The lessons here are plentiful, yet not easily defined. It’d be nice if it were as simple as being humble enough to accept sage advice from your elders, but Wendell’s methods, practiced on any number of other horses or occasions, could have easily gone south. I guess what I get out of it, other than a good laugh, is that you should roll with the punches. Sometimes the best approach is to hold your tongue and take a few of those punches before you start throwing haymakers.
