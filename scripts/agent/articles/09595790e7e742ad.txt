Rethinking propylene glycol: A fresh look at fresh cow management Nathan Upah September 3, 2026 Petroleum market volatility has raised propylene glycol costs and availability, prompting dairy producers to reconsider fresh cow strategies and explore management improvements or alternative energy sources like calcium propionate. I can think of at least a few movies that have recited some version of the line, “Just because we could doesn’t mean that we should.”  Put a pin in that – we’ll come back to it. I’m not certain who had the Strait of Hormuz on their 2026 bingo card, but if you did, I suspect you won the pot several months ago. I’m not going to pretend to be an economist or venture too far into politics, but we have all watched volatility in petroleum markets affect the cost and availability of agricultural products. Propylene glycol is part of that petrochemical supply chain. For dairy producers who routinely use propylene glycol in fresh cow programs, tighter availability and higher costs have given rise to an obvious question: What do we do now? Before answering that, it is worth stepping back and asking a different question: Why are we using propylene glycol in the first place? A quick refresher on the ruminant energy story Whether we are talking about dairy cows, beef cattle, deer, sheep or goats, ruminants handle glucose differently than you and I. Advertisement Ruminants have to make their own glucose. If you or I eat a Snickers bar, readily available carbohydrate is absorbed, and blood glucose responds relatively quickly. We will see our blood glucose numbers rise within 30 minutes – if you don’t, please consult a doctor. Give that same Snickers to a cow (and no, I am not recommending that) and blood glucose ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) would probably never know. Understand that there are bodyweight differences and there are a few absolutes in a cow’s biology in which their blood sugar will rise after a meal. However, by and large, ruminant animals will take the feedstuffs they consume and, over a period of multiple hours, ferment them in the rumen into volatile fatty acids, acetate, propionate and butyrate. These become important fuels and the building blocks for the cow. For our discussion, propionate and acetate are especially important because the liver can use them to make glucose. Unlike you and me, cows absorb relatively little glucose directly from their diet. They must manufacture a large portion of the glucose they need through gluconeogenesis. And a high-producing dairy cow needs an enormous amount of it, more than a Snickers bar can provide. When demand outpaces intake After calving, nutrient demand rises rapidly while dry matter intake often lags, resulting in something every dairy producer and nutritionist know well: negative energy balance. When she’s in a state of negative energy balance, she will enlist the help of a process called lipolysis to mobilize body fat to support lactational demands. That is a normal biological adaptation. Fat is an extremely energy-dense fuel source, and virtually every high-producing cow will mobilize some body reserves during early lactation. The problem occurs when the amount of fat mobilized exceeds the liver’s ability to adequately process it. But part of the process of converting fat into energy is the release of ketone bodies. Ketones measured in blood serve as a proxy indicating the amount of fatty acid metabolism that is occurring in the liver. During this process, it is possible for the liver to become inundated with triglycerides (fat) contributing to fatty liver. To turn off this lipolytic process, a large release of insulin needs to occur which can be achieved by providing what is called a gluconeogenic precursor – remember our friend propylene glycol. Propylene glycol doesn’t require fermentation or reconfiguration; we’ve simply added another Lego piece to our building blocks. Once given, it can contribute substrates for glucose production and influence the cow’s metabolic response. This is why it has been used for decades as a strategic plan for managing cows with elevated ketones and negative energy balance, because it works. But do we need as much as we use? Until recently, propylene glycol has been relatively accessible, familiar and economical. When something works and is readily available, it is easy to incorporate it into a protocol and leave it there. Until someone yells “Bingo” that is. We now have the opportunity to pause and ask if this should be how we are handling our cows in negative energy balance. There are two paths to go down here, but both point to the fact that now might be time to rethink our early lactation management of fresh cows. 1. Ask yourself why cows need support As an industry, perhaps we need to take a step back to assess our own management and why we need as much supportive propylene glycol. Are we overcrowding? Do our cows carry too much body condition coming out of the dry period? Are our starches as available as they are entered in the computer? There are numerous possibilities and opportunities through further discussions with your veterinarian and nutritionist.  2.  Give an energy boost from the start Energy demand, driven by lactation, will rise faster than dry matter intake. So yes, that means the cow is behind the proverbial eight ball from the jump. She’s almost certainly going to be mobilizing adipose tissue to support milk production. There’s an opportunity to help her out early in lactation by providing one of those glucose building blocks – proprionate through a fresh cow drench supplying calcium propionate. Use the market disruption as an opportunity The current propylene glycol situation may be frustrating, but it also gives the dairy industry a reason to revisit things that we haven’t questioned in some time: When and where are we using propylene glycol? Are there other ways we could better prepare our fresh cows for the demands of lactation? We do not need to be captive to propylene glycol to manage our operations.
