Threats to cattle under the CUSMA renegotiation Jeffrey Kennett October 9, 2026 Restricting Canadian cattle could increase costs for U.S. producers and consumers, while animal health regulations, labelling requirements and ongoing uncertainty present the most significant risks. Part one of this examination of the Canada-U.S.-Mexico Agreement (CUSMA) examined how cattle and beef could get caught in the wider dispute. In second of the two-article series, a look at what tariffs, quotas, animal health restrictions and labelling rules could mean for producers, processors and consumers on both sides of the border. Jeffrey Kennett Editorial Intern / Progressive Cattle Jeffrey Kennett is Progressive Cattle – Canada intern staff writer based in Surrey, British Columbia. Since the first of this two-article series appeared, trade talks between the two countries collapsed. Insults between President Donald Trump and Prime Minister Mark Carney escalated through September, with Carney saying the “ period of deeper economic integration with the United States” was too easy and too risky. “It’s clear that time is over.” What ensued is reciprocal 50% tariffs both nations placed on some $20 billion to $27 billion of various goods from the other nation. Noticeably absent: energy, oil and beef. Waiting out the conflict But how long can that exemption hold out for cattle producers? A tariff or quota intended to protect American ranchers could also weaken feedlots, packing plants and beef markets that purchase their cattle. Advertisement Oklahoma State University livestock economist Dr. Derrell Peel explained that for some northern U.S. processors, Canadian fed animals provide important throughput, and removing that supply could leave individual plants with fewer cattle to cover their fixed costs. “A tariff that prevented Canadian fed cattle from coming into the U.S. might hasten the closure of a packing plant or two in the U.S.,” he said. The same concern applies to feeder cattle entering Northern yards and cull animals supplying specialized cow and bull plants. Those cattle produce lean beef, which is blended with fatty trim from American fed cattle to make hamburger. Without sufficient lean material, Peel said, the trim produced from U.S. cattle becomes less valuable. Restrictions could therefore work against the producers they were intended to protect. The design of any restriction would determine how disruptive it became. Texas A&M University livestock economist Dr. David Anderson said a modest tariff may have a limited effect, while a tariff-rate quota could determine whether a specified volume enters at a lower duty before a much higher rate takes effect – giving exporters an incentive to rush shipments into the market before the quota is filled. Trade can then fall abruptly once the higher tariff applies. “You get a bunch of product, and then you don’t get any product after you trigger the tariff,” Anderson said. He pointed to Brazilian beef shipments as an example, with large volumes arriving early in the year before the quota was exhausted and imports declined. Applied to Canadian cattle or beef, how big of a disruption quotas could be on the market would depend on the volume, but a hard limit could create more volatility than a conventional duty. Canadian agricultural economist Dr. Al Mussell reached a similar conclusion from the Canadian side, describing restrictions on cattle or beef imports as inflationary for the U.S. American consumers would ultimately absorb much of the cost, he said, because the domestic cow herd remains historically small and rebuilding has yet to begin in earnest. “I think the whole thing would really just be inflationary,” Mussell said. “It’s really not in the U.S. interest.” However, the result would not necessarily be a clean transfer from Canadian exporters to American ranchers. Feedlots and processors would compete over fewer animals, while reduced beef supplies could place further upward pressure on retail prices. Labelling as a clashing point The U.S. Department of Agriculture’s voluntary Product of USA rule took full effect Jan. 1, meaning meat carrying the claim must come from animals born, raised, slaughtered and processed entirely in the U.S. Beef from Canadian-born cattle finished or harvested south of the border cannot use the label, but that does not prevent the product from being sold or change its CUSMA tariff status. Peel doubts the voluntary rule will materially alter cattle procurement. He pointed to consumer studies indicating that origin ranks behind price and other factors, while a substantial share of beef is consumed through restaurants and food service where retail labelling has less influence. Mussell is less willing to dismiss the risk. He said some U.S. cattle organizations have long sought mechanisms that make imported livestock more expensive or difficult for domestic processors to use. And so, wider adoption of origin claims could encourage segregation, added verification costs or separate procurement streams. “There are some elements of the cattle industry, particularly in the U.S., that have really wanted to be able to discriminate against imports of all kinds, including Canadian products," Mussell said. The setting also differs from the political period when Canada and Mexico successfully challenged mandatory U.S. country-of-origin labelling at the World Trade Organization. Mussell said the present administration may be more receptive to protective measures and less responsive to a rules-based challenge. No mandatory system has been proposed as part of the CUSMA review, but its return would nonetheless mark a clear escalation from uncertainty toward a genuine market access threat. The cost of uncertainty The July decision caused little immediate disruption for cattle markets, but uncertainty can influence a business built around long production timelines. Producers can manage some of the resulting price exposure through futures, options, basis contracts or livestock insurance, but Anderson said those tools cannot directly hedge the timing and regional disruption of an unexpected tariff, quota or border measure. A heifer retained in 2026 would not produce her first calf until 2028. That offspring would then require additional time to reach a feedlot and enter beef production. With U.S. producers yet to begin retaining females in significant numbers, Peel expects restricted cattle supplies to persist through much of the decade. “The tightest supplies are yet ahead of us at some point because we haven’t started retaining heifers,” he said. “The highest prices are somewhere ahead of us.” Those timelines extend beyond a single CUSMA review. Cross-border investments, procurement arrangements and processing expansions also require confidence that market access will remain available for several years. “I have no doubt in my mind that the uncertainty is going to have some impact over time,” Peel said. The effect may never be precisely measured. A plant expansion that is postponed or a partnership that is never formed does not appear in trade statistics. Businesses considering opportunities across the border will nevertheless attach a greater risk premium to those decisions while the agreement’s future remains unresolved. Canada’s reliance on the U.S. market adds to that exposure. Overseas beef sales provide diversification, particularly for cuts that attract greater value abroad, but live cattle are harder to redirect. Transportation costs and existing processing capacity make the U.S. a natural destination for many Canadian animals. Dependence runs in both directions. American feedlots need cattle while the domestic herd is at a multidecade low, and cow and bull plants rely on sufficient volume. The ground beef sector also needs imported lean material to balance the fatty trim generated by fed cattle. No immediate changes, fewer guarantees As Canadian animals and beef continue to cross the border under existing terms and Washington has not announced a sector-specific restriction, CUSMA remains the operating framework for North American cattle trade. The review has nevertheless exposed competing visions for what comes next. The National Cattlemen's Beef Association (NCBA), Canadian cattle groups and a broad coalition of agricultural organizations want to preserve integrated commerce. Ranchers Cattlemen Action Legal Fund United Stockgrowers of America (R-CALF USA) sees the process as an opening to place limits on imported livestock and tie beef origin more closely to where cattle were born and raised. The Trump administration has not chosen between those cattle positions. By withholding an extension, it has given itself another decade to seek changes. Canadian producers will be watching for any move toward tariffs, quotas, stricter origin rules, mandatory labels or separate bilateral protocols. They will also need to follow animal health decisions that can affect shipments regardless of the broader trade agreement. “We can’t, and we shouldn’t walk away from it, but we have to find other ways to deal in order to safeguard prosperity in this industry,” said Mussell. For now, the border remains open and the rules stay in place, but what the sector has lost is the certainty that those conditions will hopefully resume without being negotiated again next year.
