Jean-Philippe Laroche October 7, 2026 High-oleic soybeans have been proven to increase production. Experienced dairy producers remember when hammer mills first arrived on farms. Back then, they sparked quite a few debates. Today, feeding corn or homegrown cereals has become a common and profitable practice. But what if I told you that producers could also grow a large portion of their protein concentrates and fat supplements right on the farm? Considerations in feeding roasted high-oleic soybeans in automated milking systems As you know, the amount of soybeans that can be fed to dairy cows is limited. One reason is the 19% crude fat content of whole soybeans. This fat is mainly composed of polyunsaturated fatty acids, with approximately 52% of total fatty acids as linoleic acid (18:2) and about 7% as linolenic acid (18:3). These fatty acids can have negative effects on rumen health and reduce milkfat percentage. Several years ago, soybean varieties were developed for human consumption with a greater proportion of oleic acid (18:1). Since this fatty acid is less harmful to rumen function, these varieties quickly attracted attention in the ruminant nutrition industry. High-oleic soybeans contain far less polyunsaturated fatty acids, with approximately 7% of total fatty acids as 18:2 and 2% as 18:3. In theory, these new varieties allow producers to feed greater amounts of soybeans before seeing a negative effect on milkfat percentage. This provides a low-cost source of both protein and fat in dairy cow diets. But what do the real stakeholders – the cows themselves – have to say about it? Soybeans suited specifically for dairy cow nutrition In a study published in 2018, researchers compared high-oleic soybeans with conventional soybeans, fed either whole or ground. As few differences were observed for whole soybeans, only the results for ground soybeans are presented in Table 1 . Protein levels were similar across all diets. Advertisement Compared with conventional soybeans, feeding high-oleic soybeans increased both milkfat percentage and daily fat yield. It is interesting to note that cows fed ground conventional soybeans exceeded the commonly accepted guideline of 400 grams per day of 18:2 intake, which likely explains the lower milkfat percentage compared with the other treatments. These results indicate that high-oleic soybeans are much less detrimental to rumen function than conventional soybeans. A positive response, even at high inclusion rates What happens when the inclusion rate of high-oleic soybeans is gradually increased? A recent study sought to answer this question. Using a corn silage and alfalfa-based diet, researchers compared several inclusion levels of ground, heat-treated high-oleic soybeans ( Table 2 ). As soybean inclusion increased, dry matter intake declined slightly. This is likely explained by the greater energy density of the diet, resulting in increased satiety. Despite this modest reduction in intake, animal productivity improved, even at a soybean inclusion rate of 24% of dietary dry matter. Milkfat percentage also showed a slight increase. The conclusion? High-oleic soybeans can generate a positive production response, even when included at 24% of dietary dry matter .  That corresponds to approximately 7.4 kilograms of soybean dry matter per cow per day. Is grinding and heat treatment necessary? Most nutrition experts agree that some degree of grinding is necessary to obtain positive production responses. Heat treatment, however, is not essential. Another recent study evaluated the impact of roasting high-oleic soybeans at an inclusion rate of approximately 17% of the diet ( Table 3 ). Feeding ground raw high-oleic soybeans increased fat production compared with the control treatment based on soybean meal and soybean hulls. Adding rumen-undegradable protein (RUP) to the ground raw soybean treatment increased protein yield compared with raw soybeans alone. However, the best overall results, both for fat and protein production, were obtained with ground roasted high-oleic soybeans. Roasting appears to unlock the full potential of high-oleic soybeans. Nevertheless, performance still improves when feeding raw high-oleic soybeans compared with conventional low-fat diets. High-oleic soybeans have been available in the U.S. for several years, and producer adoption has been impressive. Economic results have generally been positive. In Canada, high-oleic soybeans are only beginning to enter the marketplace. Currently, some companies are offering high-oleic soybean varieties, although they have high crop heat unit (CHU) requirements. Earlier-maturing varieties could also become available over the next few years. High-oleic soybeans now offer dairy producers the opportunity to produce their own on-farm source of both protein and fat. Research findings and field experience from producers south of the border demonstrate that these soybeans can be included in dairy cow diets at high levels while potentially improving animal performance, particularly when they are ground and roasted. Producers interested in this technology may need to make certain investments. However, feeding raw ground soybeans provides a lower-cost entry point and allows producers to begin exploring the benefits with minimal infrastructure. One thing is certain: High-oleic soybeans are a technology we will be hearing much more about in the years ahead.
