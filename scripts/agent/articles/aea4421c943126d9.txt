Home &raquo; Progressive Forage &raquo; Equipment Hub: First, the simple things Forage Equipment Equipment Hub: First, the simple things Brad Nelson September 21, 2026 A pocketknife may be small, but in farm life it marks the passage from boyhood to responsibility – a tool that carries usefulness, memory and maturity. I once gave a younger brother a pocketknife on his birthday (Dec. 23) and another for a Christmas present. He had, at that time, a reputation for losing his pocketknife. I told him that now, for two out of three days, there were even odds that he would possess a pocketknife. For most, the first couple or several of these would have been gifts from parents. First, of inexpensive cost (and quality) because most would lose it in days or weeks. As age and the ability to take care of one’s possessions increased, the quality and cost of the gift pocketknives increased. In my growing up years, receiving a Schrade “Old Timer” knife meant that your dad saw you as more of a man than a boy. It had three blades and was probably the most useful tool on the farm. You could whittle, clean horses' hooves, clean and trim your fingernails, cut twine on baled hay as you fed livestock and perform the surgery that changed bull calves into steers. Also peel your lunchbox apples and cut your pepperoni sticks and beef jerky. Note that there is a maintenance step between cleaning out a horse’s hoof and peeling an apple you are going to eat. A simple step. Every farm boy knows that two or three wipes of a pocketknife blade across blue jeans (no matter the state of cleanliness) will sterilize the grimiest knife blade. This minimum (and sometimes only) cleanliness step was always done before returning the said knife to its home pocket. Why? Because tomorrow you may be strolling through the apple orchard with that cute young lady from the neighboring farm and need to peel an apple or three. Nothing would kill the mood as would looking at your Old Timer and saying, “This is gross. I’ll have to go clean the horse manure off of this before I can peel that apple for you.” Advertisement Where am I going with this train of thought? Remember Grandpa stowing the plow in the shed after the last field has been plowed for the season – then handing a son or grandson a bucket of grease and a stick or putty knife or grease rag with the directive, “See that every bit of the shiny part of those plowshares get covered with grease. Because if they rust over the winter, it’ll take a day instead of a minute to get them scoured and shiny again so the soil turns smoothly.” A similar line of thought was my drill bit rule. “If you have to use one of my good drill bits, do not put it back in the index. Leave it beside the box, so I know it has been used. Then I can sharpen it or at least check its sharpness before putting it away.” The reason for this rule was that when I ended up there at three in the morning dealing with a breakdown and needed to drill a hole in a piece of steel to make a repair, I didn't have the time or patience to deal with a dull or broken (or lost) drill bit. Now  – consider that implement you’re going to be using tomorrow or next week or next spring –
is it ready to go? Comes to mind the racer at the local oval racetrack near my hometown –
the one with the reputation for arriving with his race car on a trailer. Then opening the hood and trying to remember what happened under that hood the previous Saturday night that caused him to fail to finish the main event. Yeah. Just as prepared for that evening’s race as is the implement you are hooking up to, and your memory is failing to bring up what minor thing on it was so obvious that you couldn’t forget to deal with it before using it again. Every summer, I see pictures posted on Facebook showing a round baler on fire. Sometimes disconnected from the tractor, but all too often not, with the tractor also burning. The "what happened and why" is usually a bearing that overheated (“grease worms” ate all the grease), then it threw sparks and red-hot metal into the dry hay, which combusted. Is there a solution, or cure, or prevention method? Yes. Some bearings are by design non-greaseable, or “sealed.” The reason being that the particular bearing operates in such a dirty environment that normal greasing methods would also push dirt (grinding compound) into the bearing. The other reason being that the place that bearing runs is so awkward to reach that most lube techs will miss (or ignore) it. That bearing is going to wear out eventually, and the best time to replace it is before it runs dry and ignites something expensive. Some machinery tackles the “difficult to reach” problem by plumbing steel lines from the problem bearings to an easier place to reach during normal servicing. It’s not rocket science to plumb a section of steel brake tubing from a difficult-to-reach bearing to a spot that’s handy to mate with the grease gun. My son spent a few years as mechanic/troubleshooter for a southwest Idaho hay grower. One day, the owner came upon the scene of all his balers lined up, empty of hay and with a mobile welding truck on the site welding away. The owner asked Ryan what was going on. One part inside the bale chamber of one baler had broken, and it had to be taken back to the shop for repair. Ryan had checked the other balers and found all of them showing imminent failure at the same spot. As soon as there was a break in the baling schedule, he lined them up and called the welder to reinforce the weakened area. The owner said, paraphrased by Ryan, that they were not accustomed to dealing with something like that. The norm was to have the balers blow up in the middle of the night when they were all out trying to beat incoming rain. Ryan took that as a compliment. Now, as you’re getting your mind around properly being set to go on the farm – consider yourself at a barbecue and the meat on your plate happens to need you to retrieve your trusty pocketknife. As you do, there are two things you want to keep in mind. One, how robust is the paper plate your meat is on, and two, can you remember what you last used that blade for?
