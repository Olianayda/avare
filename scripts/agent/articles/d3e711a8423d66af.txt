Capitalize on drone technology for optimal silage storage and feed pad use Cody McCary September 22, 2026 When 2025 yields exceeded expectations, some farms stretched storage in ways that later compromised safety and feed quality. Drone‑driven inventory tracking now gives producers the tools to plan ahead and avoid those pitfalls. In many parts of the U.S., the 2025 corn silage yield was above average. This created challenging storage conditions on many farms, and in-the-moment decisions had to be made. If this was the case for your farm, hopefully those decisions did not create bottlenecks that led to headaches as you fed your 2025 ensiled feeds. As piles get higher, feedout equipment may be undersized, the silage mass may be more unstable, and safety may be compromised. In addition, making a pile taller than normal while maintaining the same width and length can create feedout challenges. Silage faces that are too large, multiple open faces on a silage mass and too slow of a feedout rate can result in unnecessary exposure to oxygen, ultimately leading to spoilage challenges and lower-quality feed. For several years, drone technology has been used successfully to measure silage inventories and project run-out dates. Today, we’re taking this technology one step further to help farms plan for different harvest and storage scenarios. Ultimately, this allows farms to build contingency plans prior to harvest so they can easily and confidently pivot in situations where actual crop yields drastically differ from predictions. Know your inventory Silage consultants use drone technology that yields measurement accuracy to within 1 inch. Therefore, farm decision-makers can have confidence in inventory and mapping outputs. Initial setup is quick and easy; most drone flights are finished within 15 minutes. With the first drone flight, the silage consultant collects data that is used as a baseline. Subsequent drone flights determine the volume removed from opened silage masses. The density of the removed section can also be calculated. A consistent schedule of drone flights smooths changes in silage density throughout a silage mass. With all this information in hand, the silage consultant creates an inventory report that forecasts current inventory and future run-out dates. Advertisement Farm managers and silage consultants appreciate this technology because it is a repeatable form of measurement that limits human error and provides more consistent inventory measurements. Importantly, drone flights offer a safer means to measure silage masses as employees, and consultants don’t need to get close to a pile to obtain measurements. Build your strategy and contingency plans It is well established that, in poorly managed ensiled feeds, dry matter (DM) losses can approach or even exceed 20%. Many of these losses are avoidable with adequate forethought and planning. One example is the construction of a pile with dimensions that require an excessively slow feedout rate (defined as removing less than 12 inches from the face of a silage mass in hot months or less than 6 inches in cold months). This increases the time that spoilage microorganisms can degrade valuable nutrients. In the bunk, farmers would see visible mold and increased temperature of the ensiled feed. In severe spoilage situations, they will lose forage quantity and quality while intake and milk production will likely also decrease. Ration costs may increase because nutrients consumed by spoilage microorganisms will need to be replaced. Another much-too-common scenario is one in which a farm underestimates the total space needed to store their harvested feed. This situation is often resolved in one of two ways: Build the pile taller or store feed on unprepared ground. Silage stored in areas that have not been prepared are especially prone to high ash concentrations due to soil contamination, water damage because drainage has not been properly addressed and unwanted fermentations because of the increased likelihood of contamination from the unprepared ground. Drone technology can be used as a tool to assist in fine-tuning pile dimensions, alignment and storage space needs without the consequences of trial and error. This is done by creating a to-scale three-dimensional model of a farm’s feed storage area. Current and forecast inventories are included in the model. Then the planning begins. The farm defines its feed and storage requirements for the year ahead. Three-dimensional bunkers or piles can be drawn into the model. The dimensions and total tons stored in each theoretical bunker or pile can quickly be extracted and compared with the farm’s feed requirements. Farm teams and consultants can see and discuss feedout rates, the maximum height of the pile and its effects on length and width, additional equipment needs, carryover inventory location and its ease of feedout and potential drainage issues – all before harvest occurs. After evaluating multiple scenarios and the pros and cons, the team can have confidence in their storage strategy and, if necessary, quickly adapt to a contingency plan if actual yields don’t match projections as harvest unfolds. Of course, drone and three-dimensional modeling technology also can be a great tool when planning beyond the year’s harvest. Farms have used it to determine whether it’s time to move dirt for additional storage space or as they plan new feed pads for herd expansions, etc. Refining silage management protocols When creating forage budgets, it’s common to assume a 10% to 12% shrink on most silage masses. As previously mentioned, shrink can exceed 20% in poorly managed ensiled feeds. As silage consultants, drone technology has helped us better understand silage volume changes, and separate inevitable storage shrink from controllable shrink. To better understand the changes that occur during the preservation and storage of ensiled feed, we monitored the volume of three well-managed corn silage piles for one year. Losses ranged between 7.5% and 8.5% of total volume. The pattern of volume change differed depending on the phase of ensiling: Approximately 60% of the volume change in the three corn silage piles occurred in the first 50 days of ensiling, and the remaining 40% occurred gradually during the next 200 days. Various management practices affect the overall pile volume loss and the pattern of volume disappearance. It is reasonable to assume that management practices similarly affect total DM losses and the rate at which the losses occur. While the previous corn silage piles represented an ideal scenario, we recently evaluated two small-grain silage piles and observed losses of 16% and 17% of total volume prior to opening. When opened, we determined these piles underwent a clostridial fermentation. Clostridial fermentations are known for their high DM and energy losses, leading to such high-volumetric losses in these two piles. These scenarios demonstrate that volume change in ensiled feeds can be used as another metric in evaluating a farm’s silage management strategies. Consistent high-volume loss can signal the need to review silage management protocols and compliance. It may also be an early indication that total DM stored in the mass is less than what was predicted at the time of harvest. This information may give farms and nutritionists more opportunity to be proactive in their feed cost mitigation strategies. Put the technology to work Years ago, feed storage planning and pile design was perhaps as much art as it was science. Today’s drone technology has tipped the scale as producers have access to more accurate inventory calculations and silage use projections. This allows for better planning and strategizing to optimize silage storage and feed pad use while minimizing logistical bottlenecks and controllable shrink.
