Home &raquo; Blogs &raquo; The Outside Circle &raquo; The Outside Circle: Renaissance man Idaho Ag Proud The Outside Circle: Renaissance man Gus Brackett August 25, 2026 At a social gathering, I confessed I was a cattle rancher and a writer. A woman in our conversation said, “So you’re a Renaissance man.” Gus Brackett Freelance Writer Gus Brackett lives and works on his family ranch in Three Creek, Idaho, where they raise cattle, ... I bristled at the insult but maintained my composure, saving my wife the embarrassment. These social situations are awkward enough for a well-behaved cowboy, but I pity my wife when I’m offended and blow things out of proportion. When I got home, I looked up the definition of a Renaissance man. A Renaissance man is someone with broad knowledge, skills and accomplishments across many different fields – especially the arts, sciences and humanities – rather than deep specialization in just one area. With this definition, that woman’s insult makes no sense at all. Surely, she wasn’t complimenting my varied interests … was she? You’ve heard the tired old cliché that farmers and ranchers wear many hats. They are agronomists, mechanics, managers, financiers – and the list goes on. The same is said for small-business owners. Some CEOs like Elon Musk have their fingers in many parts of their various business interests. The wearing of many hats does not a Renaissance man make. To better understand a Renaissance man, perhaps we should study men from the Renaissance. The prototypical Renaissance man is Leonardo Da Vinci. Da Vinci was a philosopher, scientist, inventor, teacher and artist. There was some crossover between his disciplines, as his study of anatomy brought an unprecedented realism to his sculptures. His interests were varied, and he was an expert in all of his fields. Advertisement Michelangelo was a Renaissance man. He was a sculptor, architect, poet and dabbled in painting. In fact, one of his greatest frustrations was painting the Sistine Chapel instead of completing an architectural project that included 40 sculptures as a tomb for Pope Julius II. Benjamin Franklin was perhaps the first American Renaissance man. He was a scientist, inventor, publisher, musician, linguist, businessman, diplomat and statesman. Franklin had little formal education past the age of 10 years old, but he was brilliant, inquisitive and creative. His skill set was diverse, and he was proficient at most everything he tried. Teddy Roosevelt is my favorite American Renaissance man. Roosevelt was a statesman, soldier, biologist, explorer and athlete. Like me, Teddy Roosevelt was a prolific writer and a cattle rancher. We live in a society with little appreciation for a Renaissance man. Our lives and economy are so complicated that hyper-specialization of skills is a feature of modern life. A brain surgeon devotes years to learning their craft and honing their skills. Their precise and high-stakes abilities are nothing short of miraculous. But they’re probably not very good at mowing their lawn or changing a tire. And this specialization makes sense … there are not many brain surgeons, and they should spend their time saving lives. Is agricultural life compatible with Renaissance men and women? If you ask a Midwestern farmer if they live a diverse life, they will tell you that they grow corn, beans  and hogs. Not exactly a Renaissance man, but that mindset is not unique to the Midwest. I understand the importance of specialization in modern life, but I want to acknowledge the agriculturalists who have varied and diverse interests. I know several lawyers who also farm and ranch. How about the architect who also maintains an orchard? Have you heard of the college professor who also raises sheep? The opposite is true. Perhaps a farmer who builds theater sets, the beekeeper who is a theologian or a rancher who is a writer. These are not hypothetical; I can put at least one name with each of these combinations. The primary reason to praise the agriculture Renaissance man is the impact they have on the culture. A lawyer who knows the struggles of agriculture life brings that unique perspective to their profession. An accountant with livestock can speak the language of a farmer or rancher. Have you dealt with an accountant who calls your cow herd “the breeders,” like it’s an episode of the Handmaid’s Tale ? I have encouraged my kids to explore their interests in science, art and history. I have exposed them to music, poetry and theater. They are capable of literary writing and public speaking. I doubt they choose a profession in the humanities, in fact I hope they choose an agricultural career instead. We as farmers and ranchers must tell our story, and I want them equipped with a myriad of story-telling skills. I have seen ranchers who are skilled artists and can paint a story that words fail to capture. I know farmers with such an interest in history that when they share history it sounds a lot like advocating for agriculture. Call it a hobby, call it whatever you want, but there is virtue in exploring pursuits other than your career. If your career is urban and professional, it’s OK to dabble in agriculture. As farmers and ranchers, we can get so focused on our own piece of dirt that we fail to explore other pursuits. I’m not suggesting you need to experiment with lightning, sculpt naked biblical characters or become president. With the rest of society living in a world of specialization, should we live a life of varied interests and activities … an agricultural Renaissance man?
